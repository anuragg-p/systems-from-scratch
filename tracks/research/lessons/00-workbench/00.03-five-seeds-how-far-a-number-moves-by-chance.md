# 00.03 Five seeds: how far a number moves by chance

The seed alone moves the curve fit's test loss by 30%. This lesson runs
every experiment over several seeds and reports a result as the middle and
the width of what came out, gives the lab the one test the track uses to
say whether two settings really differ, and draws every run's curve from
its log.

## Where this fits

![Where 00.03 fits](figures/00.03-map.svg)

This lesson adds two boxes to the lab: seeds, the spread of a result over
several runs and the test that compares two such spreads, and plots, the
curves drawn from the runs' logs. Both stand on the runs of 00.02: each
seed is one run, with its own folder and its own log. Every experiment
above them in the map reports through them from here on, so no result in
the track is ever a single run. After this lesson a number comes with how
far it moves by chance, and a claim that one setting beats another comes
with a measure of whether chance could have made the gap.

## The idea

![The same fit with five seeds: five training curves, and five test losses from 0.0472 to 0.0645, mean 0.0542 with a band of one standard deviation, 0.00668](figures/00.03-seeds.svg)

The runner of 00.02 gives the same number for the same seed, and another
number for another seed:

<!-- verify: session -->
```
$ ./learn run research --at 00.02 -- curve seed=1 | tail -2
train loss 0.0506  test loss 0.0645
run in runs/curve/seed1
```

Seed 0 gave a test loss of 0.0496, seed 1 gives 0.0645: 30% worse, from
the same code and the same settings. The seed chose which twenty points the
cubic was fitted to and which five hundred it was scored on, and that was
enough. Now picture changing the learning rate and the seed between two
runs, which is easy to do without noticing: the 30% would be credited to
the learning rate.

One run's number is one draw from all the numbers the method could have
given. The fix is to make several draws, one run per seed, and report the
middle of them with their width:

- the **mean**, the sum divided by how many there are;
- the **standard deviation** (sd), how far a single run typically lands
  from the mean: the square root of the average squared distance from it,
  with one change explained in How it works;
- the smallest and the largest, the **min** and **max**.

The lab prints those on one line, the result's **spread**:
`0.0542 +- 0.00668 (min 0.0472, max 0.0645, 5 seeds)`.

Two settings then differ only when the gap between their means is wide next
to how much those means move by chance. **Welch's t** measures that: the
gap between two means divided by the noise in the gap. Near 0, chance could
easily have made the gap; past about 3, with five seeds a side, chance
rarely makes a gap that wide.

The curve fit's `main()` now makes one run per seed, seeds 0 to 4 unless
`seeds=` says otherwise, prints each, then the test loss's spread, and
draws every run's training loss into `runs/curve/train_loss.svg`.

### `mean()`, `sd()` and `spread()`

The mean, the sample standard deviation, and the one line a result is
reported as. `spread()` prints three significant digits: the last digits
of a float differ from machine to machine, and at five seeds the third
digit is already noise.

### `welch_t()`

The gap between the means of two lists of results, over the noise in that
gap, as How it works derives it.

### `ticks()` and `lines()`

The lab's line plots, as SVG files: `ticks()` picks round numbers for an
axis, and `lines()` draws one line per run, with its legend. Runs of one
setting share a colour, so several seeds read as one band.

## How it works

### The noise in an average

One seed's test loss moves by its sd. A mean of five moves less, by sd
divided by the square root of 5. In plain steps:

1. The **variance** of a number that chance decides is the average squared
   distance from its middle: the sd squared.
2. For two draws a and b that do not depend on each other, the variance of
   a + b is the variance of a plus the variance of b. Square (a - its
   middle) + (b - its middle): it is the two squares plus twice their
   product, and the product is positive as often and as much as it is
   negative, so on average it is 0. The same holds for a - b.
3. So the sum of n draws has n times the variance of one, and their mean,
   the sum divided by n, has n / n^2 = 1 / n of it. Its sd is the sd of one
   draw divided by the square root of n.

Welch's t follows from step 2. The gap between the means of a and b has
variance `sd_a^2 / n_a + sd_b^2 / n_b`, and the noise in the gap is its
square root:

```
    noise = math.sqrt(sd(a) ** 2 / len(a) + sd(b) ** 2 / len(b))
    return (mean(a) - mean(b)) / noise if noise > 0 else float("inf") * (mean(a) - mean(b))
```

A run checks step 3 in numbers. Run fifty seeds, cut them into ten groups
of five, and compare the spread of the ten means with what the formula
says:

numbers, so their sd is itself a rough measure, and these agree as closely
as ten numbers can show.

### Why n - 1

`sd()` divides the sum of squared distances by n - 1, not n:

```
    return math.sqrt(sum((x - m) ** 2 for x in xs) / (len(xs) - 1))
```

The distances are measured from the mean of these same numbers, m, not from
the true middle, mu, which nobody knows. The mean of a list is the point
closest to all of it, so the distances from m come out a little smaller
than the distances from mu would. By exactly how much:

1. Each x - mu is (x - m) + (m - mu). Square and add over the n numbers:
   the sum of (x - mu)^2 is the sum of (x - m)^2, plus n (m - mu)^2, plus
   twice (m - mu) times the sum of (x - m), which is 0, because that is
   what the mean is.
2. On average the sum of (x - mu)^2 is n times the variance, and (m - mu)^2
   is the variance of a mean, the variance over n (step 3 above).
3. So on average the sum of (x - m)^2 is n - 1 times the variance, and
   dividing by n - 1 gives the variance itself.

With fifty seeds the difference hardly matters. With the track's three to
five, dividing by n would make every spread 10 to 18% narrower than it
should be, and every gap look bigger. (The square root undoes a little of
the correction: an sd from five numbers is still slightly low on average.)

On the test points between those two its squared error averages 0.043,
about the noise; on the test points beyond them, 0.408. Five seeds had no
seed like it, so their sd could not show it. That is why `spread()` prints
the min and max beside the sd, and why a spread from five seeds is an
estimate too: it can only show what its five draws happened to include.

## Decide the boundary

This track runs each setting with three to five seeds and reports the mean,
the sd, the min and the max. The noise in a mean falls only as one over the
square root of the number of seeds: four times the seeds halve it, and each
seed costs a whole run. A study could instead run fifty seeds of every
setting and see small gaps and rare bad seeds, at ten times the cost, or
one seed of ten times as many settings and see none of it. Five seeds told
degree 1 from degree 3 at once, and could not tell degree 5 from degree 3
at all. How small a gap do you need to see, and how many runs can you pay
for to see it?

## Break it

Give every run the same seed. In `experiments/curve.py`, in `main()`, change
`runs = [one_run(cfg, seed) for seed in range(cfg.seeds)]` to
`runs = [one_run(cfg, 0) for seed in range(cfg.seeds)]`, and run it:

<!-- verify: patch file=experiments/curve.py old="runs = [one_run(cfg, seed)" new="runs = [one_run(cfg, 0)" -->
<!-- verify: session -->
```
$ python3 -m experiments.curve
seed 0  train loss 0.0104  test loss 0.0496
seed 0  train loss 0.0104  test loss 0.0496
seed 0  train loss 0.0104  test loss 0.0496
seed 0  train loss 0.0104  test loss 0.0496
seed 0  train loss 0.0104  test loss 0.0496
test loss 0.0496 +- 0 (min 0.0496, max 0.0496, 5 seeds)
runs in runs/curve/; curves in runs/curve/train_loss.svg
```

Five runs and no spread at all, and the line still says "5 seeds". Since
00.02 a run is decided by its settings and its seed, so five runs of seed 0
are one run five times: they measure that the code is repeatable, and
nothing about chance. Worse, a comparison against these runs would see no
noise on this side, and Welch's t would count every bit of a gap as real.
Repeating a run is not the same as running another seed. Put
`one_run(cfg, seed)` back.

## Go deeper

- [Accounting for Variance in Machine Learning Benchmarks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03098)
  by Bouthillier et al., 2021 - the abstract: "variance due to data
  sampling, parameter initialization and hyperparameter choice impact
  markedly the results", across five deep learning tasks, and what that
  does to the comparisons papers make. The data sampling is this lesson's
  seed.
- [Deep Reinforcement Learning that Matters](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.06560)
  by Henderson et al., 2017 - the same problem in a field where it is worse:
  reported results that move with the randomness of the runs, and its call
  for significance measures before claiming an improvement.
- [Dive into Deep Learning, 22.10 Statistics](https://d2l.ai/chapter_appendix-mathematics-for-deep-learning/statistics.html) -
  "Statistical Bias" and "Variance and Standard Deviation" for what an
  estimate is and how far it is off, and "Conducting Hypothesis Tests" for
  the reasoning behind a test like Welch's t.
- [Bessel's correction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessel%27s_correction) -
  "Source of bias" for why the mean of the sample makes the distances too
  small, and "Proof" for the n - 1 worked out in full; "Caveats" for why the
  sd stays slightly low.
- [Welch's t-test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welch%27s_t-test) - the
  same statistic, "designed for unequal population variances", and how to
  turn it into a probability, which this track does not do.
