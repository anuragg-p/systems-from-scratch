# 10.01 RMSNorm: normalising with less

Every block of module 08's GPT reads its stream through layer norm
(04.04), which does two things to each vector: it takes the vector's mean
away, then divides by its spread. Zhang and Sennrich (2019) bet that only
the second matters, dropped the first, and claimed the same quality in
7 to 64% less time. This lesson makes the change in the GPT and measures
both halves of the claim. The quality held: 3.561 bits per character
against layer norm's 3.563, inside the noise. The time did not, on this
laptop's CPU: a whole training step is slower with RMSNorm, because
PyTorch's layer norm is one fused piece of compiled code; written out the
same way, RMSNorm takes about two thirds of layer norm's time. And in the
trained model, layer norm's mean step turns out to do almost nothing.

## The idea

### What layer norm does, and costs

04.04's layer norm takes each vector of `width` numbers, subtracts its
mean, divides by the square root of its variance (plus a small `eps`),
then multiplies each number by a learned **gain** and adds a learned
**bias**. In 08.04's GPT of 4 blocks there are nine of them, two a block
and one before the scores, and each one reads every position of every
window in every step, forward and backward. Its arithmetic is small next
to attention's and the MLP's, but it is several passes over the numbers:
one for the mean, one to subtract it, one for the squares, one to divide,
one for the gain and bias.

### Zhang and Sennrich: keep the division, drop the mean

"Root Mean Square Layer Normalization" (Zhang and Sennrich, NeurIPS 2019)
writes the new norm as its equation 4:

<!-- verify: skip reason="the paper's equation, not code" -->
```
abar_i = (a_i / RMS(a)) g_i,    RMS(a) = sqrt((1/n) sum_i a_i^2)
```

Each number divided by the **root mean square** of the vector, the root
of the mean of its squares, and times its gain. No mean is taken away and
there is no bias. Their section 4 says why it might be enough: "we
hypothesize that the re-scaling invariance is the reason for success of
LayerNorm, rather than re-centering invariance." Dividing by the size of
a vector makes a layer indifferent to how large its input is; taking the
mean away makes it indifferent to a shift of every number by the same
amount, and that, they bet, the network can do without. Where the mean
is already 0, "RMSNorm is exactly equal to LayerNorm."

The claim, from the abstract: "RMSNorm achieves comparable performance
against LayerNorm but reduces the running time by 7%~64% on different
models." The time is measured on a GPU (section 6: "one TITAN X
(Pascal)"; their table 4, a V100). The 64% is a variant that takes the
root mean square of only part of the vector, on another model. For the
model nearest ours, a Transformer base translating English to German,
their table 4 has 26.8 BLEU against layer norm's 26.6, and 231 seconds a
thousand steps against 248: 6.9% faster. Section 7 adds that "actual
speed improvements depend on the framework, hardware, neural network
architecture and relative computational cost of other components".

![What each norm does to one vector of four numbers: RMSNorm divides it by the root of the mean of its squares and keeps its direction; layer norm first takes its mean away, so 5, 6, 7, 8 comes out as low, lower, higher, highest around 0](figures/10.01-vector.svg)

### One footing for both

To compare two pieces, train two GPTs that differ in that piece alone:
the same text, the same steps, the same batches, the same seeds, the
weights counted, and the time a step measured while the two take turns,
so that whatever else the machine is doing falls on both alike. Every
lesson of this module compares its change that way, on 08.04's GPT (4
blocks of 4 heads, width 64, windows of 64) for 150 steps and 3 seeds,
with the same base row each time: 3.563 bits per character.

### What the run does

1. RMSNorm against PyTorch's own `torch.nn.RMSNorm` and the formula, and
   what both norms do to two vectors.
2. The norm alone, forward and backward, at the GPT's size and at
   SmolLM2's width (10.04): PyTorch's layer norm, layer norm written out
   with the same operations as RMSNorm, RMSNorm, and PyTorch's RMSNorm.
3. The GPT with every norm a layer norm, and with every norm an RMSNorm:
   bits per character on the test text, ms a training step, weights.
4. What the mean step was doing: how far from centred the vectors are
   that each norm reads in both trained models, and the layer-norm model
   scored again with its norms no longer taking the mean away.

### `RMSNorm`

Each vector over the root of its mean square, `eps` under the root as
layer norm has it, times a gain for each number that starts at 1. The
GPT's `norm="rms"` puts one wherever 08's GPT has a layer norm.

### `compare`

GPTs that differ in one piece, trained, timed and scored alike: for each
seed, each variant built from that seed and trained in turn, its training
timed, its bits on the test text measured, and its run written.

### `written_layer_norm`

Layer norm written out with the same operations RMSNorm uses, and one
more pass for the mean, so the two can be timed alike.

### `norm_times`

Each norm called forward and backward many times, the norms taking turns,
and the median of each.

### `centring`

For every norm of a trained GPT, how far the vectors it reads are from
centred: a vector's mean beside its root mean square, averaged.

### `uncentred`

A copy of a trained GPT whose layer norms skip the mean step and keep
everything else they learned.

**Alone, RMSNorm is cheaper than layer norm written the same way, and
both are slower than PyTorch's layer norm.** At the GPT's size, 32
windows of 64 positions of 64 numbers, RMSNorm takes 0.64 of the time of
layer norm written out with the same operations; at SmolLM2's width,
0.67. Over every run made for this module the ratio was 0.64 to 0.72:
about two thirds, the arithmetic the paper saves. But `nn.LayerNorm`
does the whole thing in 0.30 ms where RMSNorm takes 0.82, and PyTorch's
own `nn.RMSNorm` is no faster than ours on this CPU. Over the runs,

## How it works

### One line where layer norm had several

```
        return x * torch.rsqrt(x.pow(2).mean(-1, keepdim=True) + self.eps) * self.weight
```

`x.pow(2).mean(-1, keepdim=True)` is each vector's mean square, one
number per position kept as a column so it divides every number of its
own row; `torch.rsqrt` is one over its square root. Layer norm written the
same way needs one more line before it, `x = x - x.mean(-1,
keepdim=True)`, and a bias after. That is the whole saving: one pass for
the mean, one to subtract it, one to add the bias.

### Why the fused kernel wins anyway

```
    x = x - x.mean(-1, keepdim=True)
    return x * torch.rsqrt(x.pow(2).mean(-1, keepdim=True) + eps) * weight + bias
```

Each operation in a line like these is a separate call into PyTorch:
it reads its input tensor from memory, computes, and writes a new tensor
of the same size, and autograd keeps what each needs for the backward
pass, which runs as many calls again. A **kernel**, in this sense and not
06.01's filter, is one such piece of compiled code. `nn.LayerNorm` is one
kernel that reads each vector once and writes the result once, forward
and backward. On a CPU, where the
numbers here are small and each call has a cost of its own, four calls
lose to one, whatever the arithmetic inside them. The paper's 7% was
TensorFlow's layer norm against TensorFlow's RMSNorm on a GPU, both as
those frameworks ran them then.

### The default is still 08's model

```
        self.norm1 = RMSNorm(width) if norm == "rms" else nn.LayerNorm(width, bias=bias)
```

Every change of this module is an argument of `GPT` whose default is
module 08's piece, so a model built without it is built exactly as
before, draw for draw from the same seed: every earlier lesson's numbers
are unchanged, and a checkpoint 08.04 or 09.04 wrote loads as it did.
`GPT.__init__` still starts its weights by name, and an RMSNorm's gain is
called `norm1.weight` like a layer norm's, so the loop that leaves every
weight with "norm" in its name at its own start leaves it at 1.

### Taking turns

```
    for seed in range(cfg.seeds):
        for name, changes in variants.items():
            run = lab.Run(cfg, seed, label=name)
            torch.manual_seed(seed)
```

The seed's loop is outside the variants' loop, so a slow minute on the
machine lands on one seed of every variant, not on all the seeds of one.
`torch.manual_seed(seed)` before each model gives two variants whose
shapes match the same starting draws, and the batches come from the
run's own generator, the same for each. Before any of it, each variant
takes one untimed step: a process's first step of AdamW is slow while
memory and the optimiser are set up, and it would land on whichever
variant came first.

## Decide the boundary

RMSNorm's saving is real in the arithmetic and absent from this CPU's
clock, because the old piece had a fused kernel and the new one does
not. On the GPUs open models are trained on, RMSNorm has fused kernels of
its own, and the saving the paper measured can show. A change can win or
lose on speed for reasons that have nothing to do with the idea: who
wrote a kernel for it, on which hardware. When a paper's gain is time,
is it a property of the idea, or of the code someone wrote for it, and
how would you tell them apart?

## Break it

positions of a window instead of across each vector's numbers. In
`build/at/research/10.01/models/gpt.py`, in `RMSNorm.forward()`:
