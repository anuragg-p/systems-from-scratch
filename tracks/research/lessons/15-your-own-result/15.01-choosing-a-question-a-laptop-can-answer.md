# 15.01 Choosing a question a laptop can answer

A question worth a month and a question worth an hour can sound alike.
This lesson takes one that the track's own results raised, 08.03's: a GPT
without positions cannot see order with one block, and with four,
learned positions were worth only 0.065 bits, so what are they worth as
blocks are added? It checks what is already published, makes the question
small enough for an hour of a laptop's CPU, writes down seven predictions
with a rule for each before anything runs, and runs a pilot that fixes how
many seeds the experiment needs: 22 pairs of runs, about 43 minutes.

## Where this fits

![Where 15.01 fits](figures/15.01-map.svg)

This lesson starts the module that ends the core, in the research row of
the map: one small piece of research done whole, from a question to a
paper. It stands on 08.03's GPT and its order test, on 08.01's training
loop, and on module 13's tools, which it uses on a question of its own: a
hypothesis written down first (13.01 A hypothesis, and a baseline tuned as
hard as your method), paired seeds and the number of seeds a difference
needs (13.02 Error bars: is the difference real?), and choosing on one
held-out text while reporting on another (13.03 An evaluation you tried to
fool). Its code is `experiments/question.py`, which holds the question, the
predictions and what the module's three scripts share, and
`experiments/pilot.py`. After it, the experiment of 15.02 has a question,
predictions it can break, and a size.

## The idea

### Where questions come from

Richard Hamming's talk "You and Your Research" (1986): "Most great
scientists know many important problems. They have something between 10
and 20 important problems for which they are looking for an attack." And
what makes a problem important is not what an answer would be worth: "It's
not the consequence that makes a problem important, it is that you have a
reasonable attack." On a laptop the attack comes first: a question it can
settle in an hour, with the code there is.

The track has left a list of its own. Every lesson that ran a paper's
claim again said what held and what did not, and each "did not" is a
question:

| from | what was found | the question it leaves | on a laptop? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 13.01 | Adam and SGD with momentum tie when both rates are searched: 6.25 against 7.06% test error after 2 passes | does Adam pull ahead with more passes? | yes: 13.01's script runs its 31 runs in under a minute |
| 04.05 | 32 residual layers train about as well as 8 after one pass, not better | does the deeper one pull ahead with more passes? | yes: minutes of MNIST |
| 08.03 | learned positions are worth 0.065 bits at four blocks; one block cannot see order at all | what are they worth at other depths? | yes: the pilot below says 43 minutes |
| 10.03, 10.05 | SwiGLU is behind after 150 steps at width 64, and ahead from step 100 in 10.05's long run at width 128 | is it the width or the steps? | yes: a few short runs |
| 14.04 | at the paper's 50% of the equations, how large the weights start decides whether grokking appears | how does the delay grow with the start? | about half an hour: 14.04's 18 runs took 27 minutes |
| 13.04 | the exponent is 0.097 at 800 steps, against Kaplan et al.'s 0.076 at convergence | does it move with the steps? | an evening: one budget took 51 minutes |
| 09.04 | TinyStories' "nearly perfect grammar" did not hold by eye | how good is its grammar, measured? | not by this track's rules: the paper's grader is GPT-4 |

![The track's results and the questions they leave, each beside the minutes of the recorded run it would extend, read from that run's output; the hour a recorded run may take is the line](figures/15.01-questions.svg)

The minutes come from the recorded runs' own logs: a question that extends
one costs about what that run cost. This module takes 08.03's: it follows
from an exact test, its answer is one number per depth, and the code for
it is 08.03's model with one argument changed.

### Has someone answered it?

John Schulman's "An Opinionated Guide to ML Research" warns about questions
that come from reading: "One major downside of idea-driven research is
that there's a high risk of getting scooped or duplicating the work of
others. Researchers around the world are reading the same literature,
which leads them to similar ideas." So before running anything: a search.
A few searches of the web and of arXiv on 2026-11-25, and the papers they
led to, read:

- Irie, Zeyer, Schlueter and Ney (2019), "Language Modeling with Deep
  Transformers": word-level models of 12, 24 and 42 layers. Without
  positions the perplexity was lower than with sines and cosines at every
  depth (table 9: 60.5 against 63.1 at 12 layers, 54.2 against 55.0 at
  42), and "the positional encoding is not needed for multi-layer
  autoregressive self-attention models".
- Haviv, Ram, Press, Izsak and Levy (2022), 08.03's paper: models of 125
  million to 1.3 billion weights, 12 to 24 layers. No positions against
  learned ones, perplexity 22.15 against 22.04 at the smallest and 13.10
  against 13.05 at the largest; "these performance gaps diminish as the
  models scale up". A probe reads no position out of the first layer of the
  model without positions, and finds it "becomes position-aware within four
  layers".
- Kazemnejad et al. (2023): a proof that "the first layer ... can recover
  absolute positions" when every input starts with the same token, so that
  later layers can use them. Chi et al. (2023) and Zuo et al. (2024) say
  where the position comes from: an average over more earlier positions
  varies less, and neighbouring vectors are more alike.

![Where each published comparison sits on the depth axis, and where this module's runs sit: 1 to 8 blocks, below every one of them](figures/15.01-known.svg)

So it is partly known: deep models without positions do about as well as
with them, and theory says two layers are enough to recover a position.
Not found: the gap block by block from one block up, in a small model, at a
fixed budget, where the one-block case is exact. That is the question. Not
found is not the same as not done: five papers and a few searches are not
a survey, and the paper of 15.03 says so.

### Small enough for an hour

One model family, one text, one number, one budget:

- 08.03's GPT, width 64, 4 heads, windows of 32 characters, at 1, 2, 4, 6
  and 8 blocks.
- Tiny Shakespeare, which ships with the project: trained on its first
  90%, rates chosen on the next 5%, scored on the last 5%.
- The **gap**: a seed's bits per character on the test text without
  positions, minus the same seed's with learned positions. Positive when
  positions help.
- 08.03's budget: 300 steps of 32 windows, AdamW.

What this gives up is said before it runs: other widths, longer training,
other texts. The answer will be about this size and this budget.

### The predictions, written down first

![The seven predictions as experiments/question.py holds them, written before any run, each with the rule that will decide it](figures/15.01-predictions.svg)

`PREDICTIONS` in `experiments/question.py` holds seven, as 14.01's
`experiments/predictions.py` held the grokking paper's claims: what is
predicted, what will be measured, and a rule. Some rules go by the means
over seeds ("the gap shrinks at every step of depth"); the rest by the
gap's 95% interval over the pairs, as 13.02 made it, and then a third
answer is possible: "not settled", when the interval holds the line the
rule draws. One number is fixed with them: `SMALLEST`, the smallest gap
worth finding, 0.02 bits, under a third of 08.03's 0.065.

The file was committed before the pilot ran. A rule written after the
numbers can be fitted to them; written before, it cannot, and the
history of the files shows which came first.

### `pair`

The two arms of one seed. Built from the same seed, the model with
positions would draw its table of positions between its embeddings and its
blocks, and every block after would start from other numbers; so each is
built from the seed, and then every weight the model without positions has
is copied into the other. They differ only by the table. Their batches are
paired too: both runs draw their windows from a Run's generator, which the
seed makes.

### `trained`

One arm trained by 08.01's loop and scored on the validation text, and on
the test text only when it is asked to: a choice is made on validation, and
the test text is read only by runs whose settings are already fixed.

### `judge`

A prediction's rule applied to the results: held, did not hold, or not
settled.

### `budget`

The minutes a plan of runs takes, from the seconds runs took: a block adds
the same work to every step, so the time of a run is a straight line in
the number of blocks.

### `read_runs`

Every run a script wrote, read back from the files 00.02's runner leaves:
config.json and summary.json, by label and seed.

### `pack`

A recorded run made into one file: each run's three files, their text
whole, one line a run of `runs.jsonl`, checked by reading it back before
the folders go. The module's records hold hundreds of runs; packed, a
reader of the repository meets one file for each, and `unpack` gives the
folders back byte for byte. `read_runs` reads either.

### The pilot

A **pilot** is a small run made before an experiment to measure what the
experiment's design needs. Two things are not known yet: how far the gap
moves from seed to seed, which fixes the number of seeds, and how long a
run takes, which says whether the plan fits the hour. 13.02's
`seeds_needed` answers the first from a spread: how many pairs make a true
gap of `SMALLEST` come out clear of 0 four times in five. The pilot runs
both arms at 08.03's rate, at the two ends of the plan, one block and
eight, 4 pairs each, scored on validation only.

**At 08.03's rate, 0.149 bits at one block and 0.032 at eight.** The
recorded pilot's four pairs, on the validation text: positions ahead by
0.12 to 0.19 bits at one block and by 0.022 to 0.036 at eight. The pilot
was not run to answer the question, and these are one rate's numbers; it
was run to size the experiment, and it says there is something to find at
both ends.

**The largest spread decides: 22 pairs.** At one block the gaps spread
with a standard deviation of 0.0315, at eight 0.0064, and almost all of the
spread at one block is the model with positions, whose own bits moved by
0.036 from seed to seed against 0.005 without them. At 0.0315,
`seeds_needed` asks 22 pairs for a gap of 0.02 to show four times in five;
at 0.0064 it would ask 3. Not paired, at the largest spread of one model,
it would ask 52 runs of each.

**The page's pilot asks 27.** Its three pairs at one block are the
recorded pilot's first three, and at two blocks it finds 0.135 bits; but
three numbers give a rougher spread than four, here 0.0356 against
0.0315, and 27 pairs. A spread measured on a handful of seeds is itself a
measurement with an error, and a number of seeds worked out from it is
only as good: 15.02's 22 pairs turned out to spread by 0.023 to 0.035 at
the rates the search chose.

**The budget: about 43 minutes.** At the times the pilot measured, 2.7
seconds a run at one block and 16.9 at eight with other work running on
the laptop, four rates on one seed and 22 pairs at every depth, with the
control, come to about 43 minutes: inside the hour a recorded run may
take. 15.02 says what it really took.

## How it works

### One start, except the table

```
    missing, unexpected = learned.load_state_dict(none.state_dict(), strict=False)
    assert missing == ["positions.weight"] and not unexpected, (missing, unexpected)
```

`strict=False` lets a state dict that lacks a weight load anyway, and
returns the names it did not fill. The assert says the one missing is the
table of positions and nothing else, so a later change to the model that
gave the two arms other shapes would stop the run rather than pair it
wrongly.

### The widest spread decides

```
    paired = max(3, lab.seeds_needed(q.SMALLEST, worst, q.LEVEL, q.POWER, paired=True))
```

`worst` is the largest standard deviation of a depth's gaps in the pilot.
Every depth runs the same number of pairs, so the number is the one the
noisiest depth needs. At least 3: with 2, an interval has one degree of
freedom and its t is 12.7.

### Validation only

```
    if test:
        out["test_bits"] = window_bits(model, texts["test"], cfg.context - warm + 1, warm)
```

The pilot's runs never pass `test=True`, and their summary.json files hold
no test bits. The pilot's numbers sized the experiment, so they are part
of the choosing, and the test text stays out of it.

## Decide the boundary

The rule took the largest spread, one block's, and asks 22 pairs of every
depth. Per depth, the eight blocks' spread would ask 3, and the experiment
would take a fraction of the time. The rule was written before the pilot,
so 22 it is; changing it now would be a choice made with the numbers in
view, the thing the rules are there to stop. As it happened, the eight
blocks' spread at the rate its search chose was 0.025, not 0.0064, which
asks 15: a pilot at one rate had measured the spread of another
experiment. Which costs more: an experiment that spends minutes it did not
need, or a rule changed after the data came in?

## Go deeper

- [You and Your Research](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~robins/YouAndYourResearch.html) by Richard Hamming, 1986 - the part on important problems, "a reasonable attack", and the 10 to 20 problems a scientist carries; and "Great Thoughts Time", the Friday afternoons he kept for asking which problems matter.
- [An Opinionated Guide to ML Research](http://joschu.net/blog/opinionated-guide-ml-research.html) by John Schulman - "Choosing Problems": idea-driven against goal-driven research, and the risk of duplicating work; "Aim High": the 10% and the 10X improvement, by which measure this module's question is small.
- [Transformer Language Models without Positional Encodings Still Learn Positional Information](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.16634) by Haviv et al., 2022 - table 2 for the gap at four sizes, figure 2 for the probe that finds position within four layers.
- [Language Modeling with Deep Transformers](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.04226) by Irie et al., 2019 - section 7 and table 9: models of 12 to 42 layers without positions, and what their first layer attends to.
- [The Impact of Positional Encoding on Length Generalization in Transformers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19466) by Kazemnejad et al., 2023 - section 5, theorem 1: how a first layer can work out a position, given a token that marks the start.
