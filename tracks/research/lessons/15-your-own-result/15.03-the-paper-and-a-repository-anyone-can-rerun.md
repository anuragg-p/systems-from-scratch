# 15.03 The paper, and a repository anyone can rerun

A result that nobody else can read, or run again, is a result only for the
one who ran it. This lesson writes 15.02's up as a short paper, made by a
script from the recorded logs so that no number in it is typed by hand,
and reads it section by section against Simon Peyton Jones's advice on
writing papers. Then it looks at what makes the track's own project
something anyone can run again, shows that the paper's commands really
do make the same bytes in a clean copy, and ends with a checklist for
publishing a result of your own.

## Where this fits

![Where 15.03 fits](figures/15.03-map.svg)

This lesson ends the module and the core of the track, in the research
row of the map. Its code is `experiments/paper.py`, which reads the two
recorded runs through `experiments/question.py`'s readers (15.01, 15.02),
judges the predictions with its `judge`, and writes text; it trains
nothing. Under it are 00.02's runner, whose config.json and summary.json
files hold every number the paper prints, and data/fetch.py, which pins
every download. After it the module's result exists as a paper, and the
track has shown, on its own files, what a repository needs for someone
else to run it again.

## The idea

### Writing it is part of the work

Hamming, in "You and Your Research": "it is not sufficient to do a job,
you have to sell it", and the first of his three ways: "You have to learn
to write clearly and well so that people will read it". Peyton Jones's
slides "How to Write a Great Research Paper" go further: "Writing papers
is a primary mechanism for doing research (not just for reporting it)",
and the slide headed "Do not be intimidated" answers the fear of a small
result: "Write a paper, and give a talk, about any idea, no matter how
weedy and insignificant it may seem to you".

A paper typed by hand copies its numbers from a terminal, and sooner or
later copies one wrong, or keeps one from an older run. This one is
written by `experiments/paper.py` from the files the runs left: every
number in it is read from a log, computed from one, or taken from
`experiments/question.py`, where the predictions and other people's
numbers were written down with their sources before the runs. The words
around the numbers are fixed; where a word depends on a number (whether
the gap closed, which predictions failed), the number chooses it.

![The paper, section by section, beside where each section's numbers come from: the recorded runs' config.json, summary.json and output.txt, question.py, and the text's own bytes](figures/15.03-sources.svg)

### `defaults`

The settings an experiment declares and their defaults, read from its
`CONFIG = dict(...)` with Python's own parser rather than by importing
it: an experiment never imports another, and the paper needs depth.py's
settings without running anything of depth.py.

### `command`

The command that made a recorded run, from its config.json alone: every
setting whose recorded value is not the script's default, lists quoted so
that no shell reads their brackets. If the paper's commands are made from
the record, they cannot drift from it.

### `abstract`

Four sentences, as Peyton Jones's structure asks: the problem, what was
done, what was found with its interval, what follows.

### `verdicts`

Each prediction beside its outcome: its words, the rule written before
any run, our number, and the verdict `judge` gives.

### The shape it takes

Peyton Jones's structure for a conference paper: "Title (1000 readers)",
"Abstract (4 sentences, 100 readers)", "Introduction (1 page, 100
readers)", "The problem (1 page, 10 readers)", "My idea (2 pages, 10
readers)", "The details (5 pages, 3 readers)", "Related work (1-2 pages,
10 readers)", "Conclusions and further work (0.5 pages)". The paper here
follows it, with two sections the slides do not have and module 13 and 14
taught: the predictions beside their outcomes, and what was not expected.

![Peyton Jones's structure, and the paper's sections against it: the same order, related work late, and two sections of the track's own](figures/15.03-structure.svg)

### A repository anyone can rerun

The paper's last section gives commands; they are only worth something if
they work on someone else's machine. The track's own project has been
built that way since 00.02, and it is the example:

1. **Every run keeps its settings and its seed.** 00.02's runner writes
   config.json beside every log: every setting, the seed, the label. The
   commands in the paper are made from those files, and run again they
   give the same bytes (below). A record of hundreds of runs is kept
   packed into one file, every file's text in it, so that the repository
   stays readable; nothing is lost in the packing.
2. **The data comes from pinned commands.** What is too big or not ours
   to ship is fetched by `data/fetch.py`, which keeps a file only if its
   size and SHA-256 match the ones written in it; what ships, Tiny
   Shakespeare, has its SHA-256 printed in the paper.
3. **Each licence is read and written down.** NOTICE, at the top of the
   repository, says for every data set and set of weights where it came
   from, under which terms as read at the source on which day, and whether
   any of it is in the repository.
4. **One command per figure.** `depth.py` writes `bits.svg` and
   `gap.svg` from its own runs; the paper names both and the command.
5. **A short default beside the recorded run.** Every experiment's default
   runs in about a minute and shows the method; the long run is recorded
   beside it, its files committed, its command in its config.json files.
6. **No weights.** A trained model is made again by its run's command from
   its config.json and seed; a checkpoint is large, and would carry the
   data's terms with it. 08.04's long run saved one, 10.9 MB, and it was
   left out of the repository.
7. **What it does not do.** The records do not say which machine or which
   version of PyTorch made them, and the last digits of a number can
   differ on another machine; the paper's limits say so.

![What the track's project ships, what it fetches by a pinned command, and what it never ships, with their sizes: from the files on disk and from data/fetch.py's pins](figures/15.03-repo.svg)

### Publishing your own

A result of your own, made public so that someone else can check it. In
order:

- One folder for the project, under version control, with a README that
  says what the question is, what the answer was, and the one command for
  each number, figure and table, with how long each took on what machine.
- The predictions in a file of their own, committed before the first run;
  the history shows it.
- Every run writing its settings and its seed beside its numbers, and a
  short default under a minute that shows the method.
- The long runs recorded: their settings, logs and what they printed,
  committed; their weights not.
- The data: shipped with its hash if it is small and its terms allow, else
  fetched by a command that checks the size and the hash; its licence
  read at the source and written down with the date. Your own code under
  a licence you chose.
- The paper made from the logs, or checked against a version that was.
- Before calling it done: a clean copy of the repository, in a new folder,
  runs the short default to the same bytes, and the paper's numbers come
  out of the commands it gives. Nothing private in it: no keys, no
  personal paths, no data you may not share.

## Run it

The paper, from the recorded runs the project has; it trains nothing:

```
$ ./learn run research --at 15.03 -- paper
Running research as it stands at 15.03 (The paper, and a repository anyone can rerun). Its files are in build/at/research/15.03/
What learned positions are worth to a small GPT, block by block

Written by experiments/paper.py from the logs of experiments/recorded/pilot and
experiments/recorded/depth: runs on one laptop's CPU.

Abstract

Without position embeddings a GPT of one block cannot tell one order of its window from another, and
from the second block on its causal mask lets order in; at 4 blocks learned positions were worth
0.065 bits per character in an earlier run, at one learning rate for both models. We measure what
they are worth at 1, 2, 4, 6 and 8 blocks of a character-level GPT 64 wide on Tiny Shakespeare, each
model's learning rate chosen by the same search on held-out text, each depth on 22 pairs of runs
that share their batches and starting weights, a number fixed by a pilot before the experiment ran.
The gap falls from 0.288 bits (0.278 to 0.298) at one block to 0.124 bits (0.113 to 0.135) at 8 (95%
intervals), its interval stays above 0 at every depth, and no depth without positions reaches even
one block with them (3.384 bits at best, against 3.196). At 4 blocks it is 0.201, 3.1 times the
earlier run's, because the model with positions gains most from a larger rate; and the weights
positions add, given to the model without them, close none of it (+0.004 bits).

1. The problem

Take 32 characters of Shakespeare and a GPT of one block without position embeddings, and put the 31
characters before the last in another order. Its scores for the next character do not move: in the
earlier run the largest change over five orders was 3.55e-15, rounding. With four blocks they moved
by up to 2.44. In one block the last position weighs a set of characters, and a weighted sum does
not care in what order its terms come; from the second block on, each position reads what the first
block saw at the positions up to it, and the causal mask made those views differ. So a GPT without
positions sees some of the order, and at four blocks learned positions were worth only 0.065 bits
per character (3.407 against 3.342, 3 seeds). How much are they worth as blocks are added, and how
many blocks does a GPT without them need to match one with them?

2. What this paper shows

- The gap at 1, 2, 4, 6, 8 blocks, each on 22 pairs of runs, each model's rate searched alike
  (section 4): 0.288 bits (0.278 to 0.298) at one block, 0.124 bits (0.113 to 0.135) at 8; at no
  depth tried does its 95% interval reach 0.
- How many blocks without positions match one with them: none of 1 to 8. The best without, 8 blocks,
  scores 3.384 bits; the worst with, 8 blocks, 3.260 (section 4).
- The rate matters more than the depth: at 0.003, the earlier run's rate, the search put the gap at
  4 blocks at 0.076 on validation, at the rates it chose at 0.169 (sections 4 and 6).
- Not the weights: the 2048 weights positions add at 4 blocks, put into the MLPs of the model
  without them instead, close none of it (+0.004 bits) (section 4).
- Seven predictions written before any run: 6 held (section 5).

3. Method

Model. The GPT of the track's module 08: pre-norm blocks of attention and an MLP, GELU, a last layer
norm, the output tied to the embeddings, GPT-2's start (every weight drawn with sd 0.02); width 64,
4 heads, windows of 32 characters, 1 to 8 blocks. With learned positions it has a table of 32 x 64 =
2048 more weights, added to the embeddings.

Data. Tiny Shakespeare, 1,115,394 bytes, one byte a character: the first 90% to train on, the next
5% to choose on (validation), the last 5% to report on (test), split by position. Scores are bits
per character, each character read with 16 or more before it.

Training. AdamW, weight decay 0.1 on the matrices and embeddings, the rate warmed up over 50 steps
then a cosine to 0, the gradient clipped to 1.0; 300 steps of 32 windows.

Search. At every depth both models ran at each of the rates 0.0015, 0.003, 0.006, 0.012 on seed 0,
and each kept the rate with the lowest validation bits.

Pairs. Seeds 1 to 22 at the rates chosen: one seed gives both models the same batches and the same
start for every weight they share; only the table of positions is not shared.

Seeds. A pilot before the experiment ran 4 pairs at 1 block and 8 blocks, rate 0.003, scored on
validation only. Its largest standard deviation of a depth's gaps, 0.0315, and the smallest gap
worth finding, 0.02 bits, ask for 22 pairs for a true gap of that size to come out clear of 0 four
times in five.

Control. At 4 blocks the model without positions again, with every MLP 4 hidden units wider (2064
weights, as many as positions add, within a few), at the rate chosen for the model without
positions, on the same seeds.

Statistics. Each pair's gap, no positions minus learned; the mean with a 95% paired t interval, and
Welch's interval on the same runs beside it.

Cost. The pilot took 157 s (3 minutes) and the experiment 2817 s (47 minutes) on one laptop's CPU,
with other work running.

6. What was not expected

The predictions that did not hold: again (did not hold).

What follows was seen in the numbers after the runs; no rule was written for it before.

The rate, more than the depth. At 4 blocks the gap on validation (seed 0, the search) is 0.016 at
0.0015, 0.076 at 0.003, 0.167 at 0.006, 0.169 at 0.012. From the earlier run's 0.003 to the rate
each model's search chose, the model with positions gained 0.117 bits and the model without them
0.025. One rate for both, chosen for neither, measured 45% of the gap at the rates the searches
chose; the prediction 'again' failed for that reason.

Why the gap shrinks. With positions the best depth is 2 blocks (3.164 bits); from there to 8 blocks
the model with positions gets worse by 0.096, and the model without gets better by 0.051. Of the
gap's fall of 0.147, 65% is the deeper model with positions doing worse in 300 steps, not the deeper
model without them catching up: 'shrinks' held by its rule, and not for the reason it gave.

Pairing bought nothing. The paired intervals are 0.97 to 1.11 times as wide as Welch's on the same
runs: the two models of a seed do not move together (their correlation over seeds is -0.17 to
+0.15), though they share their batches and every weight but the table of positions.

Validation and test agree: at no depth do the two texts' gaps differ by more than 0.007 bits, so the
test text's own sampling is not what the numbers show.

7 of the 10 choices of the search are an end of its range (learned at 2 blocks, none at 2 blocks,
learned at 4 blocks, none at 4 blocks, none at 6 blocks, learned at 8 blocks, none at 8 blocks): a
search whose best is at its edge may have stopped short of the best.

A follow-up past the top of the search, at 4 blocks: both models at 0.012, 0.024, 0.048 on seed 0,
validation bits 3.379, 3.415, 3.867 without positions and 3.210, 3.235, 4.045 with them, so the
search keeps 0.012 and 0.012, the experiment's own: there its edge was the best rate, not a limit.
Its 3 pairs give a gap of 0.211 (0.133 to 0.290), against 0.201 in the experiment; run at the same
rates and seeds by another command, they are the experiment's seeds 1 to 3 again, to the last digit.
It took 96 s (2 minutes).

7. What others found

- Irie, Zeyer, Schlueter and Ney (2019), Language Modeling with Deep Transformers, Interspeech 2019,
  arXiv 1905.04226 (section 7.1 and table 9): word-level models of 12, 24 and 42 layers on
  LibriSpeech: without positions the perplexity is lower than with sinusoidal ones at every depth
  (development set 60.5 against 63.1 at 12 layers, 56.6 against 58.0 at 24, 54.2 against 55.0 at
  42); 'the positional encoding is not needed for multi-layer autoregressive self-attention models'.
- Haviv, Ram, Press, Izsak and Levy (2022), Transformer Language Models without Positional Encodings
  Still Learn Positional Information, Findings of EMNLP 2022, arXiv 2203.16634 (table 2 and section
  5): on the Pile, 125 million to 1.3 billion weights (12 to 24 layers): no positions against
  learned, perplexity 22.15 against 22.04 at 125M and 13.10 against 13.05 at 1.3B, 'these
  performance gaps diminish as the models scale up'; a probe finds the model without positions has
  no position in its first layer and 'becomes position-aware within four layers'.
- Kazemnejad, Padhi, Natesan Ramamurthy, Das and Reddy (2023), The Impact of Positional Encoding on
  Length Generalization in Transformers, NeurIPS 2023, arXiv 2305.19466 (theorem 1): 'the first
  layer of f_theta can recover absolute positions' (with a beginning-of-sequence token at the start
  of every input), so that later layers can use them; models without positions generalise to longer
  inputs better than with any explicit positions, on reasoning and arithmetic tasks.
- Chi, Fan, Chen, Rudnicky and Ramadge (2023), Latent Positional Information is in the Self-
  Attention Variance of Transformer Language Models Without Positional Embeddings, ACL 2023, arXiv
  2305.13571 (the abstract): a model without positions, even untrained, 'inherently encodes strong
  positional information through the shrinkage of self-attention variance': the average over more
  earlier positions varies less.
- Zuo, Guerzhoy and Guerzhoy (2024), Position Information Emerges in Causal Transformers Without
  Positional Encodings via Similarity of Nearby Embeddings, COLING 2025, arXiv 2501.00073 (the
  abstract): 'nearby embeddings are more similar to each other than faraway embeddings, allowing the
  transformer to potentially reconstruct the positions of tokens', in trained and untrained models.

Not found in that search: the gap block by block from one block up, in a small model, at a fixed
budget. The search was a few queries of the web and arXiv and the papers they led to, not a survey.

8. Limits

- One size (64 wide, 4 heads, windows of 32), one text, one budget (300 steps): a model trained
  longer, or wider, or on another text may give another curve.
- Each rate was chosen on one seed; a rate that was lucky on seed 0 runs on every later seed.
- The windows start at random places in the text and have no token that marks a start, which the
  proof of Kazemnejad et al. uses.
- The runs record their settings and seeds, not the machine or the library's version; the last
  digits can differ on another machine.
```

### Read against Peyton Jones

**One ping.** "Your paper should have just one 'ping': one clear, sharp
idea". This one's is in the title and the abstract's third sentence: what
positions are worth falls with depth and never reaches 0 by eight blocks.
The rate finding competes with it for the reader; the abstract gives it
the fourth sentence, the reason a second paper could be written.

**The abstract, four sentences.** The problem (one block cannot see
order, the mask lets it in, 0.065 at four blocks), what was done (five
depths, a search for each model, 22 pairs fixed by a pilot), what was
found (0.288 falling to 0.124, never 0, and no depth without positions
reaching one block with them), what follows (the rate, three times; not
the weights).

**The problem, with an example.** "Use an example to introduce the
problem": the paper opens on 32 characters of Shakespeare shuffled, and
the number 08.03 measured, before any general statement.

**Contributions that can be refuted.** "Contributions should be
refutable": each point of section 2 is a number with its interval and the
section that holds the evidence, the forward references the slides ask
for in place of a "rest of this paper is" paragraph.

**Related work after the idea.** The slides put it late because "the
reader knows nothing about the problem yet": section 7, after the
results, and generous: "Be generous to the competition". Irie et al.
found models without positions as good or better at 12 to 42 layers; the
paper says so in their words and numbers.

**Weaknesses said.** "Acknowledge weaknesses in your approach": the
limits, and section 6 above them, where everything not predicted is set
out, the prediction that failed first.

**What it does not follow.** The slides' "Use the active voice": the
generated sentences use "we" in the abstract, and elsewhere often state
numbers without a subject. And the intuition before the details: the
paper explains the mask's order in section 1 in words, with no figure;
the figures are files beside it.

The commands at its end are true. The record keeps its 282 runs packed,
one line each in `experiments/recorded/depth/runs.jsonl` with every file's
text (15.01's `pack`), and `unpack` writes the folders back. Take the
recorded experiment's settings for one depth, one rate and one seed, run
them in the project, and compare with what the recorded run left:

## How it works

### Read, not run

```
        tree = ast.parse(f.read())
```

`ast.parse` turns depth.py's text into Python's own tree of it without
running a line, and `ast.literal_eval` turns each value of `CONFIG` into a
number, a list or a string, and refuses anything that is not written out
as one. Importing depth.py would also work today; it would also run its
imports, and an experiment never imports another.

### A setting is what differs from the default

```
        if key in NOT_SETTINGS or value == default:
            continue
```

The record holds every setting, the defaults too; the command gives only
those the run changed, so that it reads as a person would type it. The
comparison is with the defaults as they are now, so if a default moves,
the command gains the setting it needs to make the old run again.

### Words the numbers choose

```
    closes = (f"its interval reaches 0 from {blocks(shut)} on" if shut else
              "its interval stays above 0 at every depth")
```

The abstract's third sentence must be true whatever the runs found, so the
numbers pick between two ways of saying it. This is how the whole paper is
written, and its cost: the sentences are the ones someone thought of
before reading the result.

## Decide the boundary

The paper is made by code: every number is right, and the sentences are
stiff, since a sentence that must hold whatever the numbers are cannot
say much that only these numbers allow. A paper written by hand reads
better and can say what the numbers mean, and every number in it is a
copy. Between them: generate the numbers and the tables, and write the
prose around them by hand, checked against the generated text before it
goes out. Which would you rather defend to a reviewer who reruns your
commands: a dull sentence that is true, or a good one with a number that
is not?

## Break it

Move a default out from under the record: someone shortens depth.py's
warmup, a setting the recorded run never gave on its command line. In
`build/at/research/15.03/experiments/depth.py`, make `CONFIG`'s
`steps=150, warmup=40,` (it was 50), and make the paper again:

```
$ ./learn run research --at 15.03 -- paper | grep -F "experiments.depth"

```

The command has gained `warmup=50`: the recorded runs warmed up over 50
steps, the default now says 40, so the command must say 50 to make them
again. A command typed into the paper when it was written would still
read as it did, would now run something else, and nothing would say so.
Put `warmup=50` back.

## Go deeper

- [How to Write a Great Research Paper](https://simon.peytonjones.org/great-research-paper/) by Simon Peyton Jones - the slides (PDF on that page): the one "ping", the four-sentence abstract, contributions that are "refutable", related work after the idea, examples before the general case, and "Use the active voice".
- [You and Your Research](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~robins/YouAndYourResearch.html) by Richard Hamming, 1986 - the part on selling: writing clearly, formal talks and informal ones, and why "the tendency is to give a highly restricted, safe talk".
- [An Opinionated Guide to ML Research](http://joschu.net/blog/opinionated-guide-ml-research.html) by John Schulman - "Keep a Notebook, and Review It": the daily record the recorded runs and the paper are a small form of.
- [Deep Learning Tuning Playbook](https://github.com/google-research/tuning_playbook) by Godbole, Dahl, Gilmer, Shallue and Nado - "Identifying bad search space boundaries", the step 15.02's follow-up took.
