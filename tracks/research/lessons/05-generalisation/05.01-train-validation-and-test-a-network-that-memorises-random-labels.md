# 05.01 Train, validation and test: a network that memorises random labels

A network can get every one of its training examples right and still have
learned nothing. This lesson cuts MNIST into three sets - one to train on,
one to choose with, one to score once at the end - and uses them to catch a
network that memorises 1000 labels drawn at random.

## Where this fits

![Where 05.01 fits](figures/05.01-map.svg)

This lesson opens module 05, on the experiments row of the map:
generalisation. It adds nothing to the engine or the lab. The network, the
training loop, Adam and MNIST are the ones modules 02 and 03 built; what is
new is which digits each number is measured on. Until now a model was
scored on the data it trained on, or on MNIST's test set, and a choice such
as the best learning rate of a grid was made on whichever was printed. From
here an experiment trains on one set, makes its choices on a second, and
reports a third, once. The rest of the module measures every fix against
those sets.

## The idea

Here is the experiment of this lesson. A network of two hidden layers of
512 relu units is trained with Adam, at a rate of 1e-3, on the first 1000
digits of MNIST: 60 passes over them (**epochs**), in batches of 100. Then
the same network is trained on the same 1000 images with their labels
replaced by digits drawn at random, so that one 7 may be labelled 3 and the
next 7 labelled 0. There is nothing to learn from those labels: no rule
takes a picture to its random label. Three seeds each, under a minute in
all:

Read only the `train accuracy` lines first: 1 and 1. By the score on its
own training set, the network with random labels is as good as the one with
real labels: every one of the 1000 labels right. It only takes longer, to
epoch 40 or 45 instead of 10 (the run checks every 5 epochs). A network
told that this 7 is a 3 and that 7 is a 0 can still get every one right: it
**memorises** them, keeping whatever is particular to each image, a stray
stroke or a slant, that tells it from the others. The score on the training
set cannot tell that apart from **learning**, finding what all 7s have in
common.

The other two lines can. They score each network on digits it never
trained on, with their real labels. The network that learned real labels
gets 90% of them right; the one that memorised random labels gets 10%,
what guessing gets with ten digits. Doing well on examples the model was
not trained on is **generalisation**, and only a score on such examples
says whether a model learned anything. Fitting what is particular to the
training images, which does not carry over to new ones, is
**overfitting**; the rest of this module is about having less of it.

### The paper

This is the experiment of Zhang, Bengio, Hardt, Recht and Vinyals,
"Understanding deep learning requires rethinking generalization" (2016).
Their central finding, in their words: "Deep neural networks easily fit
random labels." Their figure 1(a) shows the training loss of an Inception
network on CIFAR10's images going to zero with true labels and with random
labels; with random labels the test error is "no better than random
chance", and training takes longer "only by a small constant factor". Here,
at laptop size, on 1000 MNIST digits and a network of two hidden layers:
every training label right with random labels too, at epoch 40 to 45
against 10, and a test accuracy of 0.104 where guessing gets 0.1. The claim
held. It is not their experiment repeated: theirs fits CIFAR10's 50,000
photographs with networks that train for hours; this is the size a laptop
finishes in under a minute.

### Three sets

![MNIST cut three ways: the first 1000 training digits to learn from, the last 10,000 to validate on, and MNIST's own test set, scored once at the end](figures/05.01-splits.svg)

The two held-out lines come from two different sets, and they have
different jobs. The **training set** is what the weights learn from: here
the first 1000 of MNIST's 60,000 training digits. The **validation set** is
held back from the weights but not from you: the last 10,000 of the same
60,000. It is scored as often as you like, here every 5 epochs, and every
choice an experiment makes - a learning rate, a width, when to stop, which
of four ways to train - is made on it. The **test set** is held back from
both: MNIST's own 10,000 test digits. It is scored once, at the end, after
every choice is made, and its number is the one reported.

Two sets are not enough, because choosing uses up a set. A held-out score
is a fair guess at how a model does on new digits only while nothing has
been picked by looking at it. Train twelve settings and keep the one whose
score was best, and part of why it was best is luck: these particular
10,000 images happened to suit it. Its score is then a little better than
what it will do on the next 10,000. The validation set pays that price, and
is meant to. The test set is kept clean so that one number has not paid it.
The moment anything is changed because of a test score, the test set has
become a second validation set, and nothing clean is left to report. From
here the track keeps this rule: choices are made on validation; the test
set is scored once per run, printed, and never used to choose.

### `split`

The experiment's data come from one function. It loads MNIST's training
set, takes the first `n_train` images to train on and the last `n_val` to
validate on, and returns MNIST's test set as it is, third. With
`random=True` it replaces the training labels, and only those, with digits
drawn from the run's generator, so the seed decides them. `main()` trains
each network `every` epochs at a time and scores the training and
validation sets after each stretch, into the run's log; the test set is
scored once, after the last epoch, into the run's summary.

## How it works

### Only the training labels are random

```
    if random:
        train_y = rng.integers(0, 10, n_train)
```

The validation and test sets keep their real labels, because the question
put to them is whether the network learned digits: they have to say which
digit each image is. Draw their labels at random too and every network,
whatever it learned, would score about 0.1 on them.

<!-- verify: fact cmd="python3 -c 'import numpy as np; from data import mnist; from experiments.random_labels import split; y = mnist.load()[1][:1000]; print(\"{}, {} and {}\".format(*[int((split(1000, 10000, True, np.random.default_rng(s))[0][1] == y).sum()) for s in range(3)]))'" expect="120, 90 and 95" -->

That also explains why the random-label networks are not all at exactly
0.1. A label drawn from ten digits is the right one a tenth of the time, so
some of the 1000 random labels are true by chance, and the network
memorises those along with the rest. Seeds 0, 1 and 2 drew 120, 90 and 95
right labels of the 1000, and their validation accuracies are 0.122, 0.094
and 0.089, in the same order. The held-out
score measures what the training labels carried, and a few chance-right
labels carry a little.

### The two sets never share an image

```
    return (train_x, train_y), (x[-n_val:], y[-n_val:]), mnist.load("test")
```

`x[:n_train]` and `x[-n_val:]` are the two ends of the same 60,000 images.
They have no image in common as long as `n_train + n_val` is at most
60,000, 11,000 with the defaults: that is the **invariant** a validation
set rests on, and nothing in the code checks it for you. Set
`n_train=55000` and the last 5,000 training images are validated on too,
with no error; the score would be partly the training set's own.

The validation set comes out of MNIST's training files, not its test file,
so that the test set stays whole, the 10,000 images everyone else reports
on. The two held-out sets do not give the same number even here, where
nothing was chosen on either: 0.902 against 0.894 with real labels. They
are different digits. Compare a validation score with another model's
validation score, and a test score with a test score, never one with the
other.

### The test score, taken once

```
            test_acc = evaluate(torch, model, xt, yt)[1]
```

This line sits after the training loop, not in it: the network is scored
on the test set once per run, after its last epoch, and the number goes to
the run's `summary.json` and to the printout. Nothing in the script reads
it back. The validation score, inside the loop, is logged twelve times a
run, and could be used to choose an epoch to stop at; the test score could
not, because it does not exist until training is over.

## Go deeper

- [Understanding deep learning requires rethinking generalization](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.03530) by Zhang, Bengio, Hardt, Recht and Vinyals - section 2, the randomization tests: figure 1(a) for the training loss with true labels, random labels, shuffled pixels, random pixels and Gaussian noise; 1(b) for how much longer training takes as more labels are corrupted; 1(c) for the test error climbing to 90%, guessing on ten classes.
- [Understanding Deep Learning](https://udlbook.github.io/udlbook/) by Simon Prince, chapter 8, "Measuring performance" - 8.1 trains a network until it classifies its training set perfectly and asks whether it memorised; 8.5, "Choosing hyperparameters", says why choices are made on a validation set and not on the test set.
- [Deep Learning](https://www.deeplearningbook.org) by Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, section 5.3, "Hyperparameters and Validation Sets" - the same three sets, with the reasoning in the language of statistics.
- [Deep Learning Tuning Playbook](https://github.com/google-research/tuning_playbook) by Godbole, Dahl, Gilmer, Shallue and Nado - "Choosing a sample for periodic evaluation" on how often a validation set can be looked at before it is "fitted", and "After exploration concludes" on when the test set is finally scored.
