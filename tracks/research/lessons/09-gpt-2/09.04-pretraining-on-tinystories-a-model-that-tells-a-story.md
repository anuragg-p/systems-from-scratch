# 09.04 Pretraining on TinyStories: a model that tells a story

08.04's GPT, trained for an hour on Shakespeare, writes lines that scan
and words that are not words. GPT-2 writes English because it was
trained on 40 GB of the web, which a laptop cannot do again. Eldan and Li
(2023) asked how small a model can be and still write coherent English,
and answered by making the text simple instead of the model big: millions
of short stories, written by GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, in the words a small
child knows. This lesson pretrains module 08's GPT on 64 MB of their
stories, with 09.01's byte pair encoding learned on them, and measures
what code can measure. A minute of training gives words in the shape of
a story. The module's long run, a model of 4.3 million weights trained
for 45 minutes, writes whole stories of several paragraphs that end on
their own, without copying its training text: part of the paper's claim.
Coherent they are not.

## Where this fits

![Where 09.04 fits](figures/09.04-map.svg)

This lesson's piece is in the data row: TinyStories, fetched once, its
stories cut apart and made tokens by 09.01's byte pair encoding, learned
on the stories themselves, and kept in the data cache. Above it module
08's GPT trains on them with module 08's loop, 08.05's sampler writes,
and 07.03's scoring reads the held-out stories back; 09.01 makes its
bits per token bits per byte. After this lesson the track has a model it
pretrained from nothing that writes a whole story, and a way to check
what it wrote against everything it was trained on.

## The idea

### Shakespeare is too hard for a laptop

08.04's long run scored 2.226 bits per character on the test text, below
everything module 07 built, and its text is still mostly not English:
"My lord of night, but I cannot not from myself?". GPT-2 scores 1.851 on
the same text (09.03), after training on more text than a laptop could
read in a year. Between them is the question Eldan and Li asked: is it
the size of the model that makes text coherent, or the difficulty of the
text it learns from?

### Their dataset, and their claim

TinyStories is, in their abstract's words, "a synthetic dataset of short
stories that only contain words that a typical 3 to 4-year-olds usually
understand", generated by GPT-3.5 and GPT-4. Their claim, from the same
abstract: it "can be used to train and evaluate LMs that are much smaller
than the state-of-the-art models (below 10 million total parameters) ...
yet still produce fluent and consistent stories with several paragraphs
that are diverse and have almost perfect grammar, and demonstrate
reasoning capabilities". Their models are GPT-Neo's architecture, with
"GPT-Neo tokenizer but only keep the top 10K most common tokens"
(footnote 2), trained on the whole dataset, "on a single V100 GPU within
at most 30 hours" (section 4). Their stories are graded by GPT-4, the method of their section 3,
GPT-Eval: a model completes the beginning of a story, and GPT-4 grades
the completion on "grammar, creativity, and its consistency with the
beginning of the story".

Here: the paper's own training file, 64 MiB of it, module 08's GPT, the
project's own tokenizer, under an hour of a laptop's CPU. GPT-4's grading
is not run. What is measured instead is what code can check:

- **Bits per byte** on held-out stories, the validation file no model
  trains on: 09.01's bits per character, for a text that is not all ASCII.
- Whether a story **ends on its own**: the model writes END, the symbol
  that stands between two stories, within a limit of tokens.
- Its **length**, in tokens and words, and whether it ends in a **loop**,
  08.05's measure.
- **Copying**: the longest run of tokens a story shares with the training
  text, beside the same for real held-out stories, which no model saw.
  The paper measured copying another way, by Rouge scores against the
  training set (its section 4.4.1); the longest shared run is a simpler
  check of the same thing.

### The download

`data/fetch.py` gains TinyStories, from the roneneldan/TinyStories
dataset on Hugging Face at one fixed commit, under the CDLA-Sharing-1.0
licence its card gives. Not all of it: `TinyStories-train.txt`, the file
the card says the paper's models were trained on, is 1.9 GB. The fetch
takes its first 64 MiB, 67,108,864 bytes, as one range of bytes, and the
whole validation file, 19,447,282 bytes: 87 MB in all, 11 seconds here.
In the files every story is followed by a line `<|endoftext|>`.

### Stories as tokens

The first 64 MiB hold 74,394 whole stories (the range ends in the middle
of one, which is dropped); the validation file 21,990. 09.01's `train()`
learns 3,839 merges on the first 10,000 training stories, which with the
256 bytes and END make a vocabulary of 4,096. END is the last symbol,
4095: every story's tokens are followed by it, so a model learns where a
story ends as it learns everything else, by predicting the next symbol.
A token is 3.95 bytes of a story. Making the tokens takes about 9 seconds;
`data/tinystories.py` keeps the merges and the tokens in the data cache
beside the files and reads them back in milliseconds after that.

![TinyStories' 74,394 training stories by their length in tokens: median 194; 80 percent fit, with their END, in a window of 256](figures/09.04-data.svg)

### Copying, counted

A small model trained on a few million stories might write stories by
gluing together pieces of the ones it read. To check, the run looks up
each story it writes in the 16.7 million training tokens: for every
place in the story, the longest run of tokens from there that also occurs
somewhere in the training text. A list of every run of 8 training tokens,
each as one 64-bit number (its tokens as the digits of a number in a
large base), sorted, finds the places where a run of 8 occurs by a binary
search; each is then checked token by token and followed as long as it
agrees. Runs shorter than 8 count 0: a few words in common, "Once upon a
time" say, are not copying.

The same is measured for real stories of the validation file. They come
from the same source as the training stories, and no model saw them: a
model's stories should share about as much with the training text as
they do, and much more would be copying.

### What the run does

1. Reads the stories and their tokens.
2. Trains module 08's GPT on the training tokens, two seeds, logging its
   bits per token on 32 held-out windows, and scores each seed in bits
   per byte on the first held-out stories.
3. The better seed writes stories, each started from END at temperature
   1, and they are measured as above, beside the first held-out stories.

### `stories`

The stories of a split, cut at each `<|endoftext|>`, as text.

### `tokenizer`

09.01's merges learned on the first 10,000 training stories, and their
table of bytes; kept in the data cache.

### `tokens`

Every story of a split as tokens, END after each, as one array; each
different piece merged once and remembered; kept in the data cache.

### `tell`

Stories from a model: 08.05's `generate()`, 32 tokens at a time, stopped
once every story has written END, each story its tokens before END.

### `kgram_hashes`

A number for every run of k tokens of a list: its tokens as the digits of
a number in base `MULTIPLIER`, kept to 64 bits. Equal runs, equal numbers.

### `shared_run`

The longest run of a story's tokens that the training tokens also have,
found through the sorted numbers and followed token by token.

says what a laptop's hour gives, not where their models end.

## How it works

### A story ends with a symbol

```
            out.extend(ids)
        out.append(size - 1)
```

Each story's tokens, then END. A model trained on these windows sees END
after every story's last full stop, about once in 225 tokens, and the
first token after END is always the start of a new story. So END is both
the way a story stops and the way one starts: the run asks for a story by
giving the model END alone, and takes the story to be everything before
the next END it writes. In bits per byte END counts as one byte, the line
break of the `<|endoftext|>` line it stands for.

### Stopping once every story has ended

```
    while drawn.shape[1] < length and not (drawn == end).any(1).all():
```

`generate()` writes a whole batch of stories at once, one row each, and
cannot stop one row early. `tell()` asks it for 32 tokens at a time and
stops when every row holds an END; whatever a row wrote after its END is
thrown away. A batch of stories whose longest is 300 tokens costs 320
steps, not the 600 the limit allows.

### Every run of 8, as one number

```
    for j in range(k):
        h = h * np.uint64(MULTIPLIER) + ids[j:j + n].astype(np.uint64)
```

`h` starts as zeros, one for every place in the training tokens; each
pass multiplies all of them by the same large odd number and adds the
next token, numpy's uint64 wrapping around past 2^64 as the arithmetic
of a 64-bit machine does. After k passes each place holds its k tokens
as a number in base `MULTIPLIER`, cut to 64 bits: 16.7 million numbers in
8 passes over an array, then one sort. Two different runs can share a
number, rarely; `shared_run()` checks every candidate token by token, so
a collision costs time, never a wrong answer.

## Decide the boundary

The stories this model learned from were written by GPT-3.5 and GPT-4.
Its language is their language for small children: a few hundred
characters with a few names, the same openings and endings, simple
sentences. That is what made a laptop's hour enough, and it is also what
the model learned: not English, but a narrow and regular part of it that
another model chose. Data made by a larger model is cheap and can be
shaped to the question; it also carries that model's habits and limits
into the smaller one. When is a dataset written by a model a fair ground
for a claim about what small models can learn?
