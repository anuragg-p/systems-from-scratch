# 07.02 Embeddings and a network: a neural language model

07.01's pairs look one character back. The obvious way to do better is
to look further back and count again. This lesson counts with contexts of
up to seven characters and watches counting fail, gives counting its best
chance with the paper's own baseline, and then runs Bengio et al.'s
network: each character a vector of learned numbers, read by a small
network. In a minute's training it is far ahead of plain counting and
still behind the baseline; a recorded run of twenty minutes is ahead of
both.

## Where this fits

![Where 07.02 fits](figures/07.02-map.svg)

This lesson's piece is the first of the language models in the models
row: count models of any length of context, and beside them a network
that reads characters. Below them sit 07.01's text and its way of adding
a little to every count, PyTorch's layers from 06.02, and module 03's Adam
and cosine schedule, which train the network. The module's experiments
gain a small file of their own: the loss in bits, a batch of contexts cut
out of the text, a score over a whole text, and drawing text from any
model. After this lesson the track has a network whose inputs are
symbols, each turned into a vector it learns: the first layer of every
language model that follows.

## The idea

### Counting with more context

A context of n characters is n - 1 more than a pair, and the counting is
the same: how often each character came after each context of n
characters in the training text, divided by how often that context came.
The run counts with contexts of 0 to 7 characters:

![Counting with longer contexts: bits per character on the training text (k = 0) and on the test text, add-k and deleted interpolation; and how much of the test text has a context the training text never had](figures/07.02-counts.svg)

On the training text itself the score falls with every character of
context, from 3.537 bits at one to 0.804 at seven. On the test text it
falls to 2.629 at four characters and then rises, to 4.109 at seven,
worse than 07.01's pairs, with k chosen on the validation text at every
length. The reason is in the right panel. There are 65^5, about 1.16
billion, possible contexts of five characters, and the training text has
133,293 of them. Of the test text's contexts of five characters, 11.7%
never occur in the training text; of seven, 37.3%. After a context never
seen, a count model knows nothing: every character gets 1/65, the 6.022
bits of a blind guess. The longer the context, the more of the text is
scored by blind guesses, and the more the counts it does have are
memorised detail of one text: at seven characters the training text
costs 0.804 bits a character and the test text five times that.

Bengio, Ducharme, Vincent and Jauvin (2003) name this the **curse of
dimensionality**: "a word sequence on which the model will be tested is
likely to be different from all the word sequences seen during training."
The number of possible contexts grows as the number of symbols to the
power of the context's length, and no text fills more than a sliver of
them.

### The baseline: deleted interpolation

Counting has an answer of its own, and the paper compares against it:
"an interpolated or smoothed trigram model (Jelinek and Mercer, 1980)".
**Deleted interpolation** mixes the count models of every context length
up to n: the chance of a character is w0 p0 + w1 p1 + ... + wn pn, each p
the chance one count model gives it, each weight at least 0, the weights
adding up to 1. Where the longest context was never seen its p is 1/65,
but the shorter ones still know something. The weights are fit on the
validation text, to give it the highest chance. Up to five characters the
weights are 0.001, 0.013, 0.080, 0.254, 0.365 and 0.288, and the test
text costs 2.416 bits; up to seven, 2.409. Counting can do that well.

### The paper's idea: embeddings

The paper's answer, in its own words: "associate with each word in the
vocabulary a distributed word feature vector", and learn those vectors
and a network that reads them, together. Here the symbols are characters.
Each of the 65 gets a row of m numbers, 24 here, in one table: its
**embedding**. A context of five characters becomes the five rows the
characters pick out, side by side, 120 numbers. A hidden layer of 512
tanh units reads them, and a Linear layer gives 65 scores, whose softmax
(02.04) is the chance of each character that could come next. The table
is a weight like any other: training moves each row along its gradient,
with the rest of the network, by cross-entropy (here in bits) and Adam.

![Bengio et al.'s network for a context of 5 characters, at the page's sizes: each character's embedding looked up in one table, the 5 side by side, 512 tanh units, 65 scores](figures/07.02-nplm.svg)

Why that could beat counting: "Generalization is obtained because a
sequence of words that has never been seen before gets high probability
if it is made of words that are similar (in the sense of having a nearby
representation) to words forming an already seen sentence." A count model
treats two contexts that differ in one character as strangers. The
network gives similar characters similar rows, and a context it never saw
is near ones it did.

### The paper's claim, in its terms

Their table 1, on the Brown corpus (about a million words, 800,000 to
train on), is the result: test perplexity 336 for deleted interpolation of
trigrams; 312 for the best count model they had, a back-off model over
classes of words; 268 for the best of their networks alone; and 252 for a
network mixed half and half with the interpolated trigram, the network
with the lowest validation perplexity. Its caption: "The deleted
interpolation trigram has a test perplexity that is 33% above that of the
neural network with the lowest validation perplexity." That is the claim
this lesson runs again: the network, against deleted interpolation of the
same context, scored on held-out text, several seeds.

### `context_codes`

Every context of n characters in a text as one number, its characters
the digits of a number in base 65, and the character after each.

### `lookup`

The count of each of many numbers in a sorted list of numbers with
counts, by binary search; 0 for a number not in the list.

### `NGram`

A count model with a context of n characters. It keeps only what occurs:
the distinct contexts with their counts, and the distinct pairs of a
context and the character after it with theirs. Its chances are 07.01's,
every count plus k, and 1/65 for every character after a context it never
saw.

### `mixture_weights`

The weights of deleted interpolation: given each model's chance of each
character of the validation text, the weights that give the text its
highest chance, found by repeating one step that can only raise it.

### `bits`

The cross-entropy of a batch of scores, in bits: PyTorch's, divided by
log 2.

### `contexts`

A batch of contexts: for each position asked for, the n character
numbers before it and the character at it, picked out of the text in one
indexing.

### `context_bits`

A model of the next character from the n before it, scored over every
position of a text, in batches, without a graph.

### `sample`

Text drawn from any model that scores the next symbol, one symbol at a
time, at a temperature, with a seeded generator.

### `NPLM`

The network: the embedding table, the hidden layer of tanh units with
dropout when asked for, and the Linear layer to 65 scores. The paper's
direct connections from the embeddings to the scores are left out.

### `train`

One network, one seed: 2000 steps of 1024 positions of the training text
drawn at random, Adam at 0.01 falling to 0 along a cosine (03.05), the
batch's bits logged every 100 steps.

### `nearest`

Which characters' embeddings point the most nearly the same way as a
given one's: the cosine of the angle between two rows, 1 for the same
direction, 0 for none in common.

**At one character, the pair model again.** 3.594 bits against add-k's
3.599 and interpolation's 3.612. With one character of context there is
nothing to share between contexts: the network can only learn the 65
rows of the pair table, and it does.

**At three characters, the network is ahead of both,** 2.606 against
2.659 and 2.632, in every seed.

**At five, far ahead of add-k and behind interpolation.** 2.474 bits
against add-k's 2.979, half a bit better than counting with the same
context. But deleted interpolation scores 2.416, and the network is 0.058
bits behind it in every seed. In a minute's training, the paper's claim
does not show: the network has not caught up with the paper's own
baseline. Mixed half and half with interpolation, as the paper's best row
is, it scores 2.330, 0.086 below interpolation. The paper saw the same,
"mixing the output probabilities of the neural network with the
interpolated trigram always helps to reduce perplexity", and read it as
"the neural network and the trigram make errors (i.e. low probability
given to an observed word) in different places".

**The embeddings, part 4.** Among the 53 characters seen at least 1000
times, the full stop's nearest are the question mark (0.75) and the
exclamation mark (0.72), and the exclamation mark's the full stop and the
question mark: the marks that end a sentence have rows that point the
same way. a is nearest A at 0.32, s nearest S at 0.35. e has nothing
above 0.22. Two random vectors of 24 numbers have cosines spread with a
standard deviation of 0.20, so only the punctuation is clearly grouped;
capitals with their small letters, weakly; the rest is the noise of where
the rows started.

**The sample** has whole words, a speaker's name on a line of its own
(INCENTIO, a letter short of the duke of Measure for Measure, who speaks
193 times in the training text), and lines that run on: five characters
are too few to know where a line of verse ends.

## How it works

### A context as one number

```
    for j in range(n):
        code = code * V + ids[j:len(ids) - n + j]
```

A table with a row for every context of five characters would have 1.16
billion rows. So each context is turned into one number, its characters
as the digits of a number in base 65, for every position at once: each
pass of the loop shifts what is there one digit up and adds the next
character's column. `np.unique` then sorts the numbers that occur and
counts each, and `lookup()` finds a context among them by binary search.
133,293 contexts of five characters occur, so that is all the model
keeps. A context and the character after it, coded the same way, fit in
an int64 for contexts of up to nine characters; the function refuses
longer ones.

### The weights, by expectation-maximisation

```
    for _ in range(steps):
        share = w[:, None] * p
        w = (share / share.sum(axis=0)).mean(axis=1)
```

`p` has a row for each count model and a column for each character of
the validation text: that model's chance of it. For one character, each
model's share of the mixed chance is its weight times its chance, over
the sum. Averaged over the text, a model's share is its new weight.
Repeated, this is **expectation-maximisation**: each step gives the text
a chance at least as high as the step before, so the weights climb to the
best mixture, here in 200 steps. The weights come from the validation
text, never the test text: fit on the test text they would score it
better than any new text.

### The loss in base 2

```
    return F.cross_entropy(scores.reshape(-1, scores.shape[-1]), targets.reshape(-1)) / math.log(2)
```

PyTorch's cross-entropy is in nats; dividing by log 2 makes it bits
(07.01), so the training loss and the scores printed are in the same
unit. The network trains on bits as it would on nats: the gradient is
only scaled by 1 / 0.693, and 03.03 showed that Adam takes the same steps
whatever constant the gradient is multiplied by, but for its eps.

### An unseen context is a blind guess

```
        p = np.full(len(pair), 1.0 / self.V)
        p[seen] = (pair[seen] + k) / (context[seen] + k * self.V)
```

07.01's smoothing gives a pair never seen after a seen context some
chance. It has nothing to say after a context never seen, which has no
row to add k to. Every character equally likely is what the formula gives
there for any k above 0, and it is a model of its own, the bottom rung of
07.01's ladder: interpolation then has the shorter contexts to fall back
on. Break it takes that away.

## Decide the boundary

Counting is exact, takes a few seconds, and needs nothing but the text.
The network took a minute to come within 0.06 bits of the best count
model and twenty minutes to pass it by 0.07; mixed half and half, the
two beat either alone, because they are wrong in different places. A
model of words, where counting is starved, would show the network's
advantage at once; a model of characters shows it only with training.
When is a learned model worth its training, if counting is nearly free?

From two characters on, add-k is infinite: 0.3% of the test text's
contexts of two characters were never seen, and each of those characters
now has the chance 0, as 07.01's unsmoothed pairs had. numpy warns about
the log of 0, once for each line that takes one. Interpolation survives,
because the shorter contexts still give every character some chance, but
it is worse: 2.525 bits at five characters instead of 2.416, and the
longer contexts' weights shrink, since a context that can say 0 is a risk
the validation text punishes.

The network is untouched, 2.482 bits, the same as seed 0 in Run it. And
now it beats interpolation, by 0.043 bits, where with the right baseline
it was 0.066 behind. One line in the baseline turned a loss into a win.
That is how a new method can seem to beat an old one: by comparing it
with the old one done less well than it can be done. Module 13 comes
back to it. Put `np.full(len(pair), 1.0 / self.V)` back.
