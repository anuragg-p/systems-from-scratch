# 07.01 Counting pairs: a bigram model, and the loss to beat

Every model of this module and the next does one thing: given the text so
far, it gives each character that could come next a chance. This lesson
sets out how such a model is scored, in bits per character on text it
never saw. Then it builds the simplest model that does better than a blind
guess, a table of how often each character follows each other one, and
finds the three numbers every later model has to beat.

## Where this fits

![Where 07.01 fits](figures/07.01-map.svg)

This lesson's piece is in the data row: the text, a million characters of
Shakespeare's plays, shipped with the project so that nothing has to be
fetched. Beside it the experiments row gains language, the scripts of this
module. There is no network yet and nothing to train: the model is a table
of counts, made with numpy alone, and its score is 02.04's cross-entropy
in another unit. After this lesson the track has a text cut into
training, validation and test parts, a way to score any model of it, and
three scores that the networks of the rest of the module are measured
against.

It is 1,115,394 characters, 65 different ones: the newline, the space, ten
punctuation marks, the digit 3, and the 26 letters in both cases. It is
cut by position into 05.01's three sets: the first 90% to train on, the
next 5% to choose settings by (the validation text, The Taming of the
Shrew), and the last 5% to score on (the test text: the Shrew's end, then
The Tempest). Nothing is shuffled, so a held-out passage is whole, as a
new text would be. The Tempest's people, Prospero, Miranda and Ariel,
never appear in the training text: the test text is a little harder than
the validation text, and every score reported here is on it.

A **language model** guesses what comes next. Given the characters so far,
it gives each of the 65 characters a **probability**: a chance, a number
from 0 to 1, where the chances of all 65 add up to 1. 02.04's softmax made
such chances out of a network's scores; here they come from counting.

### Counting pairs

The obvious first model looks one character back. In the training text
the letter r occurs 44,271 times, and 2375 of those times an a comes next.
So after r, the chance of a is 2375 / 44,271 = 0.0536. Do that for every
pair: a table of 65 rows, the character before, by 65 columns, the
character after, each cell the number of times that pair occurs. Divide
each row by its total and the row is a set of chances that add up to 1.
That is the **pair model**, or **bigram model**.

![The pair table of the training text: a row for the character before, a column for the one after, darker for more; blank where the pair never occurs](figures/07.01-table.svg)

The characters a model looks at before it guesses are its **context**:
the pair model's context is one character. A model whose context is the
n - 1 characters before is called an **n-gram** model, after Shannon's
N-grams of N adjacent letters, and the pair model is the case n = 2. Of the 4225 pairs that 65 characters
can make, 1380 occur in the training text. The other 2845 cells are 0.

### How surprised a model is

A model is good when it gives a high chance to the character that actually
comes. Over a text, the chance the model gives the whole text is the
product of its chances for each character in turn, each given what came
before. That is a product of 55,769 numbers, most of them below 0.3: far
too small for any float (02.04 met a float32 that cannot hold e to the
-200). So the score is built from logarithms instead, in base 2.

The **logarithm** in base 2 of x, log2 x, is the power 2 must be raised to
to give x: log2 8 = 3, since 2 x 2 x 2 = 8; log2 1 = 0; log2 0.5 = -1.
Three steps take it to a score:

1. A product's logarithm is the sum of the logarithms, since 2^a x 2^b =
   2^(a + b). So the log2 of the text's chance is the sum, character by
   character, of log2 p, where p is the chance the model gave the
   character that came.
2. For any p below 1, log2 p is below 0, so -log2 p is a positive number:
   how surprised the model was. It is 0 when the model gave the character
   that came a chance of 1, 1 for a fair coin's 0.5, and, when all 65
   characters were equally likely, log2 65:
   <!-- verify: fact cmd="python3 -c \"import math; print(f'{math.log2(65):.3f}')\"" expect=6.022 -->
   6.022.
3. As p goes to 0, -log2 p grows without limit. At p = 0 it is infinite.

![Surprise, -log2 p, against the chance p a model gave the character that came; and the pair model's surprise at each of the first 12 characters of the test text](figures/07.01-surprise.svg)

Divide the sum by the number of characters, and the mean of -log2 p over
the text is the model's score in **bits per character**: lower is better,
and a text the model knew for certain would cost 0. The name is Shannon's:
in his words, "the entropy H is the average number of binary digits
required per letter of the original language". It is 02.04's
**cross-entropy** in another unit. 02.04 took the natural logarithm, log,
whose unit is the nat, and log2 p = log p / log 2: bits are nats divided
by log 2 = 0.693. Equal scores over 65 classes, put through 02.04's
cross-entropy, lose
<!-- verify: fact cmd="python3 -B -c \"import engine as torch; print(f'{torch.nn.functional.cross_entropy(torch.zeros(1, 65), torch.tensor([0])).item():.3f}')\"" expect=4.174 -->
4.174 nats, and
<!-- verify: fact cmd="python3 -B -c \"import math, engine as torch; print(f'{torch.nn.functional.cross_entropy(torch.zeros(1, 65), torch.tensor([0])).item() / math.log(2):.3f}')\"" expect=6.022 -->
4.174 / 0.693 is 6.022 bits, the score of equal chances.

The same number read another way: 2 to the power of the bits is the
**perplexity**, the number of equally likely characters the model was, on
average, as unsure as. Equal chances have 6.022 bits and a perplexity of
2^6.022 = 65, exactly the number of characters.

### One pair never seen

Run the pair model over the start of the test text, "rance ta'en" (the
test text starts mid-word): after r it gave a a chance of 0.0536, a
surprise of 4.220 bits; after a, the apostrophe got 58 / 49,718 = 0.0012,
9.743 bits. Over those 12 characters the mean is 4.282 bits, a perplexity
of 2^4.282 = 19.45. Over the whole test text the score is infinite. In 176
places the test text has a pair the training text never had, 12 different
pairs such as -D, NZ and Sy. The table gives each of them the chance 0, a
surprise of infinity, and one infinite term makes the mean infinite. A
model that calls something impossible, and is wrong, cannot be scored.

**Smoothing** is the cure: take a little chance from what was seen and
give it to what was not. The simplest way adds k to every count before
dividing, (count + k) / (total + 65 k), as if every pair had been seen k
more times. The run tries five values of k on the validation text, never
the test text, and keeps the best, k = 0.1. Here it hardly matters: from
k = 1 to k = 0.001 the validation score moves by 0.002 bits, because a
million characters fill a table of 4225 cells well. 07.02's longer
contexts are another matter.

### Three rungs

Three models, each counted on the training text with k = 0.1 added and
scored on the test text, make the first rungs of a ladder that every
later model of this module has to climb:

![The ladder: bits per character of three count models on the test text (counted on the training text, plus k = 0.1), beside their bits on the training text itself](figures/07.01-ladder.svg)

Every character equally likely: 6.022 bits, a perplexity of 65. Single
characters by how often each occurs, a context of nothing: 4.850 bits,
28.8. Pairs: 3.599 bits, 12.1. The pair model is as unsure as a choice
among 12 characters where the blind guess is a choice among 65.

### The paper: Shannon (1951)

Claude Shannon's "Prediction and Entropy of Printed English" defines these
numbers for English: "F_N may be called the N-gram entropy; it measures
the amount of information or entropy due to statistics extending over N
adjacent letters of text." He computed F_N from published tables of how
often letters, pairs and triples occur in English, so each F_N is a
model's score on the very text it was counted from. That is a text's
**entropy** under the model: its cross-entropy, with no held-out text in
it. It goes beside our scores on the training text, with k = 0.

The two are the same sum, grouped two ways. The mean over the text of
-log2 p(b after a) has one term for every place a pair occurs. Gather the
places by pair: a pair that occurs c times contributes c equal terms, so
the mean is the sum over pairs of (c / N) times -log2 p, where N is the
number of pairs in the text. That is Shannon's formula, -sum p(i, j) log2
p_i(j), with the counts' own proportions for p(i, j). On the training text,
both ways give
<!-- verify: fact cmd="python3 -B -c \"import numpy as np; from data import shakespeare; from experiments import bigram; t = shakespeare.split('train'); c = bigram.pair_counts(t, 65); p = bigram.probabilities(c); s = c[c > 0] / c.sum(); a, b = f'{bigram.bits(p[t[:-1], t[1:]]):.3f}', f'{-(s * np.log2(p[c > 0])).sum():.3f}'; print(a if a == b else a + ' and ' + b)\"" expect=3.537 -->
3.537 bits.

His table (page 54 of the paper) gives F0, F1 and F2 for 27 symbols, the
26 letters and the space: 4.76, 4.03 and 3.32 bits. F0 is equal chances,
log2 27; F1 single letters; F2 pairs. Our alphabet is not his, so the run
also folds the training text to his 27 symbols (letters made small,
everything else a space, a run of spaces made one):

| | F0 | F1 | F2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shannon, English, 27 symbols | 4.76 | 4.03 | 3.32 |
| the training text folded to 27 symbols | 4.75 | 4.09 | 3.32 |
| the training text, its 65 characters | 6.02 | 4.77 | 3.54 |

The folded text's F0 is
<!-- verify: fact cmd="python3 -c \"import math; print(f'{math.log2(27):.3f}')\"" expect=4.755 -->
log2 27 = 4.755, which Shannon rounded to 4.76. Its F1 and F2 are close to
his, and F2 is the same to three digits. That is two different texts, his
counted from tables of general English and ours a selection of plays,
coming out close: a coincidence of two texts, not a reproduction. What the
65 characters add, capitals and punctuation and line breaks, costs 0.68
bits a character to the single characters and 0.22 to the pairs. His
other experiments, people guessing the next letter with up to 100 letters
known, put English at "something of the order of one bit per letter".
Nothing here runs them; they are a mark far below the pairs, for the
models to come.

### `data/shakespeare.py`

The text and its numbers. `load()` reads the file as one string; `chars()`
is its 65 different characters, sorted, and a character's number is its
place there; `encode()` and `decode()` turn text into those numbers and
back. `split()` gives one of the three parts as numbers, cut at 90% and
95% of the text, and keeps each part once it is made.

### `pair_counts`

The pair table of a text given as character numbers: every place where
one character follows another adds 1 to the cell of that pair, in one
numpy call.

### `probabilities`

Counts made into chances along the last axis, every count plus k first:
each row of a pair table, or one row of single counts. A row that has no
counts at all stays 0 with k = 0, rather than dividing by 0.

### `bits`

A model's score on a text, from the chance it gave each character that
came: the mean of -log2 p, infinite if any p is 0.

### `sample`

Text from the pair model: each character drawn from the row of the one
before it, with the run's generator.

## How it works

### Counting with one call

```
    np.add.at(table, (ids[:-1], ids[1:]), 1)
```

`ids[:-1]` is every character but the last and `ids[1:]` every one but the
first, so position by position the two are each pair of neighbours, a
million of them. `np.add.at` adds 1 to the cell of each pair, once for
every time the pair occurs. The line that looks the same,
`table[ids[:-1], ids[1:]] += 1`, is not: numpy reads the cells, adds 1,
and writes them back once, so a pair that occurs 25,010 times is counted
once. Break it shows what that does.

### Infinite without a warning

```
    with np.errstate(divide="ignore"):
        return float(-np.log2(p).mean())
```

numpy's log2 of 0 is minus infinity, with a warning printed. The warning
is switched off here because an infinite score is a result this lesson
wants to print, not an accident: `inf` in part 1 and in part 2's first
line is the model saying "impossible" and being wrong.

## Decide the boundary

This module's symbols are characters: 65 of them, no word that is
unknown, and every text spelled out. A model of words would have tens of
thousands of symbols, a context of three words would reach much further
back than three characters, and a word never seen in training would need
a symbol of its own. Bengio et al.'s paper in 07.02 works on words; module
09 builds the compromise every current model uses, pieces of words found
by counting pairs again. What should a model's symbols be: the ones that
make the text short, or the ones that leave nothing unknown?

## Go deeper

- [Prediction and Entropy of Printed English](https://archive.org/details/bstj30-1-50) by Claude Shannon, The Bell System Technical Journal, 1951 - section 2 for F_N, the N-gram entropy, computed from tables of letter, pair and triple frequencies, and the table of F0 to Fword for 26 and 27 letters on page 54; section 3 on for the prediction experiments with a person guessing the next letter, which put English near one bit per letter.
- [N-gram Language Models](https://web.stanford.edu/~jurafsky/slp3/3.pdf), chapter 3 of Speech and Language Processing by Jurafsky and Martin - n-gram models from counts, perplexity as the way to score them on held-out text, and smoothing, add-one and add-k first.
- [Neural Networks: Zero to Hero](https://karpathy.ai/zero-to-hero.html) by Andrej Karpathy - its second lecture, "The spelled-out intro to language modeling: building makemore", builds a bigram character-level language model on a list of names: the counts, sampling, and the loss it is scored by.
