# 11.04 Lower precision: sixteen bits, and what breaks

Every number the track has trained with is a float32, four bytes. Two
kinds of number take half the room: float16, and bfloat16, which keeps
float32's range and gives up precision. Half the bytes to move and, on
hardware made for them, much faster products: Micikevicius et al.
(2018) train in float16 without losing accuracy, with three changes
that keep what sixteen bits cannot hold. This lesson measures what they
cannot hold on this model's own numbers, and every fact the paper gives
holds: an update late in a run is too small to change its weight in
sixteen bits, a quarter of them in float16 and most of them in bfloat16;
Adam's eps is 0 in float16; some gradients are lost without loss
scaling. Then it trains under PyTorch's autocast, and the speed does not
hold: this build of PyTorch has no fast 16-bit products on this CPU, and
a step is 8 to 29 times slower. Autocast also found a bug in 10.02's
rotary positions, which this lesson fixes.

## The idea

### Fewer bits

A floating-point number is a sign, an exponent that says how large it
is, and a fraction that says it to some number of bits, its
**precision**. float32 has 8 bits of exponent and 24 of precision:
numbers from about 1e-38 to 3e38, to about seven digits. **float16** has
5 and 11: the largest is 65,504; below 2^-14 = 6.1e-5 it has fewer bits
of precision, and below 2^-24 = 6e-8 nothing but 0; near 1 its numbers
are 2^-10 apart, so 1 + 2^-11 is 1. **bfloat16** has float32's 8 bits of
exponent and 8 of precision: the same range, 1.2e-38 to 3.4e38, but
near 1 its numbers are 2^-7 apart, a hundredth. Either way the spacing
grows with the number: between 16 and 32, float16's numbers are a 64th
apart and bfloat16's an eighth.

### What breaks, measured on this model

The run takes the page's GPT after 60 steps of training and looks at
the numbers it is made of. Its median weight, in size, is 0.017; there
float16's numbers are 1.5e-5 apart. One AdamW step at the full rate,
0.003, moves the median weight by 0.003, far more than the spacing. One
step at 1.8e-5, the rate the cosine schedule has reached 95% of the way
through a run, moves it by 1.3e-5: less than the spacing, and an update
under half of it, added to its weight in sixteen bits, rounds back to
the weight. Adam's eps, 1e-8, is under float16's smallest number, so it
is 0. A softmax written as e^s over the sum of e^s overflows: e^12 is
already past 65,504.

![What sixteen bits hold, on a log scale, with this model's own numbers after 60 steps: float16's range, normal, then fewer bits, then 0, and bfloat16's, float32's; where Adam's eps, an update, a weight, a score and e^12 fall](figures/11.04-line.svg)

### Micikevicius et al.: three changes

Micikevicius et al., "Mixed Precision Training" (ICLR 2018):
"Firstly, we recommend maintaining a single-precision copy of weights
that accumulates the gradients after each optimizer step (this copy is
rounded to half-precision for the forward- and back-propagation).
Secondly, we propose loss-scaling to preserve gradient values with
small magnitudes. Thirdly, we use half-precision arithmetic that
accumulates into single-precision outputs." Their reasons, from section
3: "any value whose magnitude is smaller than 2^-24 becomes zero in
FP16"; an update "could still become zero when addition operation
right-shifts it to align the binary point with the weight. This can
happen when the magnitude of a normalized weight value is at least 2048
times larger that of the weight update" (2048 is 2^11, float16's bits of
precision); and on a detector's gradients, "67% of values are zero" in
float16. **Loss scaling** multiplies the loss by a large number before
the backward pass, so every gradient is that much larger and fewer fall
under float16's smallest; they are divided by it again before the step.
"There is no downside to choosing a large scaling factor as long as it
does not cause overflow." What it buys: "This nearly halves memory
requirements and, on recent GPUs, speeds up arithmetic." bfloat16 is
not in the paper.

### Autocast

PyTorch's `torch.autocast(device, dtype)` does the third change for
whatever runs inside it: operations on its list of those that gain from
sixteen bits (on the CPU, `linear`, `matmul` and the like) get their
inputs rounded to sixteen bits; operations on its list of those that
need float32 (the losses) get them widened back; the rest run in
whatever type their inputs have. The weights stay float32, the master
copy, and the optimiser steps them in float32. PyTorch's documentation:
the default type is "torch.float16 for CUDA and torch.bfloat16 for
CPU", and on the CPU, bfloat16 "only uses torch.autocast": no loss
scaling, since bfloat16's range is float32's. `cfg.precision` in 08.04's
loop runs the forward pass and the loss under autocast in bfloat16 or
float16; "float32", or no such setting, does not.

### What the run does

1. What sixteen bits hold, and on this model's numbers after 60 steps:
   the updates of one AdamW step at the full rate and at the late one,
   the share that would change nothing in float16 and in bfloat16;
   Adam's eps and its averages of squared gradients; the gradients of
   the weights and of the residual stream; a softmax; rotary's angles in
   bfloat16.
2. Autocast in bfloat16 on this CPU: which functions of the forward pass
   ran in which type; one matrix product and a training step in each
   type; the GPT trained 60 steps both ways, 2 seeds.
3. One backward pass under float16 autocast with the loss as it is and
   scaled by 1024 and 65,536: the gradients lost, against float32's.

### `lost`

The share of updates that would change nothing if the weights were kept
in float16: the weight and the update rounded to float16 and added
there.

### `Kinds`

A hook on every PyTorch function called inside it, counting each by the
types it took and the type it gave back.

The times move with the load (this run's was 9); every other number is
the same in every run.

**The master copy, in numbers.** At the full rate no update is lost in
float16 and 0.1% in bfloat16. At the late rate, 24.6% of the updates
would change nothing in float16 and 86.2% in bfloat16: near the end of
a run a model kept in sixteen bits stops learning, most of all in
bfloat16, whose 8 bits of precision put the line at about 2^8 where
float16's 11 put it at about 2^11, the paper's 2048. Keeping the weights
in float32 and only computing in sixteen bits, as autocast does, is not
an option to skip.

![One AdamW step after 60 steps, at the full rate and at the late one: how many times smaller each update is than its weight, on a log2 scale, and the share sixteen bits would lose](figures/11.04-lost.svg)

**Adam in float16 divides by 0.** eps is 0.0 in float16, and 91.5% of
Adam's averages of squared gradients are under float16's smallest
number, where in float32 none is 0: their root plus eps is 0, and the
update 1e-3 / 0 is infinite. The optimiser's state has to stay in
float32, as the paper's master copy and PyTorch's autocast keep it.

**Gradients: the stream's are small.** Of the weights' gradients, 0.01%
are under 2^-24 and would be 0 in float16, and 10.7% under 2^-14, where
float16 has fewer bits. The gradients of the residual stream at each
block's output are smaller: 84.6% under 2^-14, the largest 0.0038. They
are the paper's "activation gradients", where float16 runs short.

**Loss scaling brings them back.** One backward pass under float16
autocast with the loss as it is: 0.007% of the weights' gradients come
out 0 where float32's are not, and the worst is off by 0.15 of its
tensor's largest gradient. Times 1024: none lost, the worst 0.00073 off;
times 65,536, PyTorch's starting scale, the same, and every gradient
finite. This model needs it in float16, a scale of 1024 is enough, and
bfloat16 needs none: its range is float32's.

**The softmax is written to survive.** e^12 is infinite in float16, so
[12, 0, -3] written as e^s over its sum is [nan, 0, 0]; with the largest
score taken away first, as `F.softmax` does (02.04), it is [1, 0, 0].
This model's largest attention score is 2.27, nowhere near. Under
autocast on this CPU the softmax runs in bfloat16: the CPU's list of
functions kept in float32 does not have it, CUDA's does, and the paper
asks for "large reductions" like a softmax's sum in float32.

**Which functions ran in sixteen bits.** The Linear layers and the
products took float32 and gave bfloat16; the loss took bfloat16 and gave
float32; attention's scores, the mask, the softmax, rotary's turning and
the MLP's gate ran in bfloat16 throughout, because their inputs were;
the embedding, the norms (which read the float32 stream) and rotary's
angles stayed in float32.

**Slower, not faster.** One product of 2048 x 256 by 256 x 256: 0.10 ms
in float32 and 42.10 in bfloat16 at load 6.9, 420 times slower (float16,
48 ms). A training step: 28.8 ms in float32, 225 in bfloat16, 7.8 times
slower (load 7.4); trained 60 steps, 8.1 times. In the recorded
benchmark (11.06), with a GPT of width 128, a step took 1,465 ms against
52.5 and the target 659 seconds against 22.7, 29 times slower, the
larger model losing more. This build of PyTorch says "MKLDNN not found"
(`torch.__config__.parallel_info()`): its float32 products are fast on
this CPU and its 16-bit ones go to a slow loop. On this CPU, sixteen
bits is slower. That is the result.

![Sixteen bits on this CPU against float32, on a log scale: one matrix product, a training step, the page's runs trained alike, and the recorded benchmark's step](figures/11.04-speed.svg)

**And the same bits.** Trained 60 steps, bfloat16 reaches 3.83 bits on
the validation windows against float32's 3.87, t = -0.5: the same, this
early, by the noise of two seeds. The recorded benchmark's bfloat16
curves stay within 0.02 to 0.04 bits of float32's and reach 2.4 bits in
451 steps against 433.

**On the Apple GPU, the same or slower.** With `device=mps` at load 16 to
18, a step of the page's GPT took 17.8 ms in float32, 22.8 in bfloat16
and 21.9 in float16; at width 128 and windows of 256, 45.4, 42.6 and
42.8, 7% faster at best. A step of a model this small on that GPU is
mostly PyTorch's work around each operation, not arithmetic (Horace
He's "overhead", 11.02).

### A bug autocast found

Under autocast, 10.02's `Rotary` made its angles in the type of the
queries it was given, bfloat16, which holds every whole number only up
to 256 and every second one up to 512: place 257 became 256, and an
angle of 60 radians was out by up to an eighth of a radian. The page's
windows of 64 places hide it; SmolLM2's 8,192 would not. From this
lesson `Rotary` makes its angles in float32 when it is asked for sixteen
bits and rounds only their cos and sin, as transformers' Llama does;
float32 and float64 are unchanged, to the last bit.

![Rotary's places and angles in bfloat16, as 10.02's Rotary made them under autocast: each true value and the bfloat16 number it becomes, with bfloat16's numbers there as ticks](figures/11.04-angles.svg)

| tiny values | "any value whose magnitude is smaller than 2^-24 becomes zero in FP16" | Adam's eps 0; 91.5% of its averages 0; 0.01% of the gradients |
| lost updates | when a weight is "at least 2048 times larger" than its update | 24.6% of updates late in a run in float16, 86.2% in bfloat16 |

Every fact about what sixteen bits cannot hold held, on this model's own
numbers. The speed did not, and the paper never claimed it would here:
its speed is the hardware's, products in sixteen bits that a V100 has
and this build of PyTorch on this CPU does not.

## How it works

### Autocast around the forward pass only

```
        with torch.autocast(dev.type, dtype=sixteen, enabled=sixteen is not None):
            loss = bits(model(x), y)
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss.backward()
```

The forward pass and the loss run under autocast; the backward pass
does not, as PyTorch's documentation advises ("Backward passes under
autocast are not recommended"): it runs each gradient in the type the
forward operation used. With no precision setting `sixteen` is `None`
and autocast is off, so every earlier lesson's run is the same to the
last bit.

### The angles in float32, the turning in sixteen bits

```
        work = dtype if dtype in (torch.float32, torch.float64) else torch.float32
```

The places and the angles are made in `work`, float32 for any 16-bit
type, and only the cos and sin are rounded to the queries' type: a cos
is between -1 and 1, where bfloat16's spacing is a hundredth or less,
while a place or an angle of 60 radians needs the bits it does not
have.

### An update that changes nothing, counted

```
    return float(((w16 + u.astype(np.float16)) == w16)[keep].mean())
```

numpy's float16 adds two float16 numbers and rounds the sum to float16,
as a float16 weight would be stepped: an update is lost exactly when the
sum comes back as the weight. Weights of 0 are left out, since any
update changes them.

## Decide the boundary

On a GPU with fast 16-bit products, autocast in bfloat16 is a setting
that costs nothing in quality here and buys speed and memory; on this
CPU it costs 8 to 29 times the time. A run's precision is therefore a
property of the machine it runs on, not of the experiment, and the same
script reports different speeds, and in the last digits different
losses, on two machines. This track keeps float32 by default so that
every page's numbers replay on any laptop. If you moved your
experiments to a GPU, which would you change: the default, so the GPU
pays, or nothing, so the numbers stay the ones your pages print?
