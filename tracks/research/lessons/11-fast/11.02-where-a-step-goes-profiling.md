# 11.02 Where a step goes: profiling

A training step is many small pieces of work: the batch, the forward
pass through the embedding, the norms, attention and the MLP, the loss,
the backward pass, the clipping and the optimiser. Before making any of
it faster, find out where the time goes. This lesson takes one step of
the module's GPT apart two ways, with plain timers on its layers and
with PyTorch's profiler, which records every operation, and sees where
they agree. Module 10 expected the answer: 10.02 found the rotary model's
step slower and suspected the angles it makes again on every call. The
profile says otherwise: the angles are 0.5% of a step and keeping them
saves nothing measurable, while the turning itself is 15.8%. What the
profile points at instead is attention written out, its scores and
weights for 128 small matrices a block taken one at a time, forward and
backward: 11.03's work.

## Where this fits

last bit.

## The idea

### Timers on the pieces

The plainest profile is a clock read before and after each piece. A
PyTorch layer can carry **hooks**, functions it calls just before its
forward pass and just after; a hook that reads 06.02's clock on the way
in and again on the way out gives the layer's time. Hung on every layer
of the GPT that is a piece (the embedding, each norm, attention's four
Linear layers, its rotary turning, attention whole, the MLP, each block
whole), the hooks time the forward pass piece by piece. Attention whole
less its timed parts is the rest of attention: the scores, the mask, the
softmax, the weighted sums, the repeat of grouped keys. The clock
around the backward pass, the clipping and the optimiser times those.

What hooks cannot see is the backward pass piece by piece: autograd runs
it as one call, from the loss back.

### PyTorch's profiler

`torch.profiler` records every operation PyTorch runs (an `aten::`
operation, PyTorch's own name for its kernels) with its time, the time
of the operations it called, and where it ran: inside a
`record_function` label, or, in the backward pass, inside autograd's
function for the gradient of a forward operation (`MulBackward0` makes a
multiply's gradient). It can split the backward pass where the hooks
cannot. It also costs something for every operation it records, so a
step under it is not the step without it.

### What the track's notes measured

At load 6.5, the mean of 10 steps of the page's GPT: the forward pass
9.40 ms (36.7% of the step), the backward pass 14.15 (55.3%), the
optimiser 1.65 (6.4%), the clipping 0.31, the batch 0.09. In the forward
pass, attention without its Linear layers and rotary 3.42 ms (13.4%),
the MLP 1.76, rotary's turning 1.55, the norms 1.11, attention's four
Linear layers 0.86. The step took 25.60 ms with the hooks on and 25
without them: the hooks cost nothing that shows.

![One training step of the page's GPT taken apart by plain timers, the mean of 10 steps: the forward pass by piece, the backward pass, the clipping and the optimiser, as shares of the step](figures/11.02-step.svg)

Under the profiler the same steps took 30.88 ms by the plain timers,
21% more, for 31,502 operations a step. The profiler's time for each
piece is the plain timers' under it to within 0.03 ms (attention 7.63
against 7.60, the backward pass 16.84 against 16.84): the two agree
with each other. What they do not agree with is the step without the
profiler, and the cost falls hardest on the pieces made of many small
operations: attention 31% more, the forward pass 24%, the backward
pass 19%, the norms 10%.

![PyTorch's profiler beside the plain timers, ms a step for each piece: the timers on steps without the profiler, the same timers on steps under it, and the profiler's own times](figures/11.02-profiler.svg)

### What it points at

The operations with the most time of their own, in that run: `bmm`, the
batched matrix product, 24 calls a step and 15.7% of all the
operations' time; `mul`, 13.3%; `copy_`, 10.3%; then `sum`, `select`
(9,218 calls a step), `mm`, `addmm`. Where they ran says why. Attention's
scores and weighted sums are two batched products a block over 32
windows x 4 heads, 128 small matrices of 64 x 16 each, and PyTorch's CPU
kernel takes them one at a time: three `aten::select` for each, 3,072 in
a forward pass, and in the backward pass about 17,000 operations under
`BmmBackward0`, 30% of the backward pass. Attention written out is the
largest piece of the step, forward and backward. Next come the
element-wise operations: `mul`, `add_`, `sum`, each a pass over memory,
from rotary's turning, the written-out RMSNorm (10.01) and SwiGLU's
product (10.03), and their gradients.

![The operations with the most time of their own in a step, under the profiler, with their calls; in the accent, the ones attention's batched product and its backward pass make](figures/11.02-ops.svg)

### What 10.02 suspected

10.02's rotary model was 18 to 30% slower a step than the model with
learned positions, and 52% at 10.05's larger size, and 10.02 put it on
the angles: `Rotary.forward()` made the cos and sin of every place again
on every call, for the queries and for the keys, in every block. Timed
alone, making the angles of 64 places takes 20.4 to 21.7 microseconds,
8 calls a step: 0.16 ms, 0.5% of the step. The turning, the multiplies
and the add that use them, forward and backward, at the queries' and
keys' own sizes: 1.37 ms a block, 5.5 ms a step, 15.8% of it (at load
5.8). The turning was the cost, not the angles.

The fix 10.02 expected was built anyway, and is right: `Rotary` keeps
the angles it made and hands them back when the same places, type and
device are asked for again, the same tensors made by the same
operations, so every number is the same to the last bit. Three training
steps with the angles kept and made every call leave every weight equal.
And it saves nothing measurable: the smallest of 15 interleaved timings,
32.6 ms kept against 32.5 made every call at load 6.1, x1.003; x1.015 at
load 6.8, x0.972 at load 10, x0.960 at load 18; in the recorded
benchmark (11.06), 23.0 seconds to the target against 22.7, t = +0.5.

![Rotary's angles made again on every call, against the turning itself, as shares of the step; then the step with the angles kept against made every call, the smallest, the median and the largest of 15 timings](figures/11.02-rotary.svg)

### What the run does

1. One training step of 11.01's baseline (the angles made every call, as
   10.02 left them), plain timers on its pieces, the mean of 10 steps;
   the step without the timers, timed as 11.01 times; the backward pass
   against the forward.
2. 10 more steps under the profiler, the timers running too: each
   piece's time by the profiler, by the timers under it, and without it;
   the 12 operations with the most time of their own; where each piece's
   time went.
3. Rotary's angles alone and the turning alone, as shares of the step;
   then the angles kept (`Rotary.keep = True`): every weight after 3
   steps against the angles made every call, and both steps timed in
   rounds that take turns.

### `piece_of`

Which piece of a forward pass a layer of the GPT belongs to, from its
name in the model, or none for a layer not timed on its own.

### `timers`

Hooks on every layer that is a piece: the clock read as it starts and as
it ends, the seconds added to that piece; with labels, each inside a
`record_function` of the piece's name, for the profiler.

### `where`

The profiler's operations put under the piece each ran in, and in the
backward pass under the autograd function that made them: calls and
time for each.

This run was at load 8 to 9, and its times are its own; the shares
move less. Which operations make the top twelve, and in what order, can
change from run to run with the times: `bmm`, `mul` and `copy_` lead in
every run the notes record.

**The step, taken apart.** The pieces add up to the step: the run
checks that, and the figure above is drawn from them. More than half of
a step is the backward pass, about one and a half times the forward
pass, as a backward pass that computes two gradients for most products
should be. The optimiser is 6%, the clipping and the batch 1% between
them. In the forward pass, attention without its Linear layers is the
largest piece, about a third of the forward pass; its four Linear layers,
which do most of its arithmetic, are a twelfth.

**The profiler agrees with the timers, and costs a fifth of a step.**
Under the profiler each piece's time is the timers' time under it, to a
few hundredths of a millisecond: both measure the same steps. Both are
larger than the step without the profiler, by about a fifth, most where
the operations are many and small. A profiler's numbers are shares to
compare, not times to quote.

**The turning, not the angles.** In this run as in the notes', the
angles alone are under 1% of the step and the turning 14 to 16%; kept
and made every call, the smallest steps differ by about a percent, one
way or the other from run to run. The weights after 3 steps are the same
bit for bit.

What the sources said, and what held here:

| | the source | here |
|---|---|---|
| profile first | Horace He: time goes to compute, to memory, or to "overhead: everything else"; "Another way is to use the PyTorch profiler" | the profile found the cost where module 10 did not expect it |
| overhead | He: PyTorch runs about 280 thousand operations a second on his machine, Python 32 million additions | 31,502 operations in a step of 25.6 ms: the 3,072 `select` of one forward pass are per-matrix bookkeeping, not arithmetic |
| element-wise work | the tuning guide: "pointwise operations are memory-bound; PyTorch eager-mode initiates a separate kernel for each operation" | `mul`, `copy_`, `sum`, `add_`: a third of the operations' time, the turning, the norms and the gate among them |
| the profiler's cost | PyTorch's documentation: tracing shapes and stacks "results in additional overhead" | without either, +21% on this step |

## How it works

### Hooks around each piece

```
        handles += [layer.register_forward_pre_hook(before), layer.register_forward_hook(after)]
```

`before` reads the clock as the layer starts and `after` as it ends, so
each piece's time includes everything the layer runs inside, its own
smaller layers too. That is why attention whole is timed and its parts
taken from it, and why a block whole less its norms, attention and MLP
is the adds into the stream and the dropout. The hooks are taken off
afterwards (`handle.remove()`): left on, every later forward pass would
read the clock.

### Every operation under its piece

```
        while up is not None and piece is None:
            if up.name in names:
                piece = up.name
            elif up.name.endswith(("Backward0", "Backward1")) and ":" not in up.name:
                grad = grad or up.name
            up = up.cpu_parent
```

Each operation the profiler recorded knows the event it ran inside, and
that one its own, up to the step. `where` climbs until it finds a
label of one of the pieces, and on the way notes the first autograd
function it passes: in the backward pass every operation runs inside
`backward`'s label and inside the function of the gradient it makes, so
`BmmBackward0`'s 17,000 operations are counted under that name. Only an
operation's own time is added (`self_cpu_time_total`), never its
children's, so nothing is counted twice.

### The angles, kept bit for bit

```
        key = (start, T, dtype, device)
        if self.kept is not None and self.kept[0] == key:
            return self.kept[1]
```

`Rotary.angles` hands back the cos and sin it made last when the places,
the type and the device are the same, and makes them as 10.02 did when
not: the same operations in the same order, so the numbers are the same
to the last bit, which is what makes the change safe to leave on for
every earlier lesson. One entry is kept, not a table: a training run
asks for the same places on every call.

## Decide the boundary

The plain timers cost nothing that shows and see only what a hook is
hung on: a piece nobody named is lumped into whatever holds it, and the
backward pass is one piece. The profiler sees every operation, in the
backward pass too, and makes the step a fifth longer, most where the
operations are small. A third way would time each operation alone, out
of the model, which costs nothing either and misses what one operation
does to the next (a tensor still in the cache, a thread pool already
awake). For a step whose largest piece is a few milliseconds, which
would you believe first, and how would you check the other?
