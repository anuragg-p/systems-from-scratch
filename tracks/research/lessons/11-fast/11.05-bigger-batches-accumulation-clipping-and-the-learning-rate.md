# 11.05 Bigger batches: accumulation, clipping and the learning rate

11.01 found a step of half the batch shorter and no sooner. This lesson
goes the other way. A step of a larger batch is not much longer than a
small one's on this CPU, so tokens a second grow with the batch, 3.4
times from 8 windows to 128. And a batch too large for memory can be
had anyway, by **gradient accumulation**: the batch cut into parts, each
part's gradient added up before one step. Accumulation gives the large
batch's gradient exactly, to 3e-16 in float64, and no speed: four parts
take four parts' time. Then the measure that counts: to 3.2 bits, a
larger batch takes fewer steps and more tokens, and in seconds the
batches from 16 to 64 come out about level, with the rate held or
doubled with the batch. Last, the clip of 07.03: early in training it
cuts nearly every step, and without it the run takes 16% more steps.

## The idea

### What a larger batch costs

A step of 32 windows does the same operations as a step of 8, each on
four times the numbers. When each operation is small, the time goes to
PyTorch's work around it (11.02's thousands of operations a step), and a
larger batch costs little more: Horace He, "if you double your batch
size but your runtime only increases by 10%, you're likely overhead
bound". When the operations are large, the arithmetic is the time and a
step grows with the batch. In the track's notes, at load 5.7: 13.3 ms a
step at 8 windows, 20.6 at 16, 29.3 at 32, 50.3 at 64, 62.9 at 128;
tokens a second from 38,409 to 130,147, 3.4 times as many. The page's
GPT on this CPU is between the two.

### Accumulation

The loss of a batch is the mean of its windows' bits. Cut a batch into
k equal parts: the mean of the whole is the mean of the parts' means,
and since a gradient is linear, the whole batch's gradient is the mean
of the parts' gradients. So each part's mean bits, divided by k, can go
through its own backward pass, and PyTorch adds each pass's gradients
into the same `.grad` of every weight: after the last part they hold
the whole batch's gradient, and one optimiser step uses it. Only one
part's activations are in memory at a time.

![Accumulation as arithmetic: a batch of 32 windows cut into 4 parts of 8, each part's mean bits divided by 4 and its backward pass adding into the same gradients](figures/11.05-parts.svg)

It buys memory, not speed: four parts of 32 take about four steps of
32's time, so a batch of 128 accumulated runs at the part's tokens a
second, not the whole batch's (67,890 against 130,147 at load 5.7).

### Dropout's masks

One thing differs. A model with dropout (05.02) draws a new mask each
time it runs, and four parts draw four masks where the whole batch drew
one. With the same seed before each, the gradient of 4 parts is 0.061 of
its length away from 1 part's: as far as the gradient of 1 part with
another seed, 0.063. Not wrong, another draw. The page's GPT has no
dropout.

### Larger batches and the rate

A larger batch's gradient is a better estimate of the same gradient,
not a longer step: with the rate held, a step moves the model about as
far as before. So a larger batch needs fewer steps, but not
proportionally fewer, and more tokens in all. A common rule raises the
rate with the batch, to take the larger steps the better estimate
allows. The race measures both: batches of 16, 32 and 64 at the rate of
0.003, and 64 at 0.006.

### The clip

Since 07.03 every run clips its gradient: when its length, the root of
the sum of every weight's gradient squared, is over 1, it is scaled down
to 1. A clip meant for rare large gradients may be doing more than that.
The run counts how often it cuts in the first 40 steps, and races batch
16 without it.

### What the run does

1. A training step of the page's GPT at batches of 8 to 128 windows,
   and 128 accumulated from 4 parts of 32: ms a step and tokens a
   second, the smallest of 3 timings.
2. Exact, in float64: the gradient of a batch of 32 in 2, 4, 8 and 32
   parts against the whole; 08.04's loop for 5 steps with the batch in
   1 part and in 4; with dropout, 4 parts against 1 and against another
   seed.
3. The race to 3.2 bits, 2 seeds a row: batches of 16, 32 and 64 at a
   rate of 0.003, and 64 at 0.006: seconds, steps, tokens and ms a step.
   (3.2 bits, short of 11.01's 3.0, keeps the run near a minute.)
4. The gradient's length over the first 40 steps at each batch and how
   often the clip cut it; batch 16 raced without the clip.

### `accumulated`

The gradient of a batch cut into equal parts: each part's mean bits
divided by the number of parts, each backward pass adding into the
same gradients.

This run was at load 9; its times and seconds are its own, and on a run
this short the load can reorder rows a few tenths of a second apart.
The steps, the tokens and every number of parts 2 and 4 are the same in
every run.

**Tokens a second grow with the batch.** In this run as in the notes',
a step of 128 windows takes about five times as long as a step of 8 for
sixteen times the tokens: the larger the batch, the less of the step is
PyTorch's work around each operation. Accumulated from 4 parts of 32, a
batch of 128 takes four parts' time and runs at the part's rate, half
the whole batch's.

**The large batch's gradient, exactly.** Cut into 2, 4, 8 or 32 parts,
the gradient of a batch of 32 differs from the whole batch's by 3.3e-16
at most, where the largest gradient is 1.07: float64's rounding, in the
order of the additions. Through 08.04's loop for 5 steps, the logged
losses of 1 part and 4 agree to 8.9e-16 and the weights to 1.2e-16.

**More tokens a second is not sooner.** To 3.2 bits, batch 16 takes 173
steps, 32 takes 126 and 64 takes 101: a larger batch, fewer steps, never
in proportion. In tokens it goes the other way, 177 thousand, 258
thousand, 414 thousand. In seconds, the notes' run at load 5.7: 4.08 at
16, 4.1 at 32, 4.71 at 64; and doubling the rate with the batch, 64 at
0.006, 94 steps and 4.17 seconds, level again. With 11.01's target of
3.0 (load 9.2), 7.88, 7.25 and 7.00 seconds, and 7.75 with the rate
doubled. What a larger batch gains in tokens a second it gives back in
tokens to the target, and the seconds come out about level, inside the
noise of two seeds. Halving the rate with the batch, the other way, was
the slowest of all: batch 16 at 0.0015, 371 steps to 3.0 bits.

![Larger batches on this CPU: tokens a second against the batch and the batch of 128 accumulated, then the steps, the tokens and the seconds each batch took to 3.2 bits](figures/11.05-batch.svg)

**The clip cuts nearly every early step.** Over the first 40 steps the
gradient's median length is 1.5 at batch 16, 1.5 at 32 and 1.4 at 64,
and the clip of 1 cut 35, 32 and 31 of the 40. It is not catching rare
spikes; early in training it caps the size of every step, alongside the
warmup. Without it, batch 16 took 201 steps to 3.2 bits instead of 173,
16% more (at 3.0, 361 against 303, 19%). Steps say it more plainly than
seconds: in the notes' run 5.52 seconds against 4.08, while in a run at
another load, like this one, a run taking more steps can still finish
first.

![The gradient's length before the clip at each of the first 40 steps, at batches of 16, 32 and 64, and the clip at 1](figures/11.05-clip.svg)

**In the recorded benchmark.** Twice the batch and twice the rate, on a
GPT of width 128 (11.06): 297 steps to 2.4 bits against 433, each 85.3
ms against 52.5, so 25.4 seconds against 22.7, x1.12, t = +2.3, with
other work back on the machine by then; taken again beside a baseline at

attention as well it is the fastest row of the table.

## How it works

### Each part divided, every gradient added

```
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss = 0.0
        for xs, ys in zip(x.chunk(parts), y.chunk(parts)):
            with torch.autocast(dev.type, dtype=sixteen, enabled=sixteen is not None):
                part = bits(model(xs), ys) / parts
            part.backward()
            loss = loss + part.detach()
```

`opt.zero_grad()` once, before the parts, and not between them: each
`backward()` adds into the gradients the last one left. Each part's bits
are divided by the number of parts before its backward pass, so the
sum of the parts' gradients is the mean, as the whole batch's is; and
the logged loss is the sum of the parts' divided bits, the whole batch's
mean. With `accumulate` 1, the default, the loop is one part, the whole
batch, and every earlier run is the same to the last bit.

### Only equal parts

```
    if cfg.batch % parts:
        raise ValueError(f"a batch of {cfg.batch} does not split into {parts} equal parts")
```

The mean of the parts' means is the whole batch's mean only when the
parts are the same size: a batch of 48 in parts of 16, 16 and 16 is
fine, 3 parts of a batch of 32 would not be. `chunk` would make them
unequal without a word, so the loop refuses first.

### Clipped after all the parts

```
        if cfg.clip:
            torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), cfg.clip)
```

The clip comes after the last part, on the whole batch's gradient,
never on a part's: clipping each part would change the sum.

## Decide the boundary

On this CPU, at this size, the batch barely moves the time to the
target: what it changes is tokens a second, memory, and the steps. A
lab with a GPU that a small batch leaves idle would see larger batches
win in seconds, up to the point where more tokens no longer buy fewer
steps; a lab short of memory would accumulate and pay in time. The clip
here does the work of a smaller rate early in training. If you found
your clip cutting every step, would you lower the rate, lengthen the
warmup, or keep the clip and call it part of the method?

The logged loss of 4 parts is now four times the batch's, 18 bits off.

1.2e-16. The gradient is four times too large, but the clip scales
every early gradient down to a length of 1 whatever it was, and Adam
divides each step by the root of the gradient's own average square
(03.03): a factor of 4 cancels in both, but for Adam's eps. The model
would train almost as before, and only the logged number, or a run
without the clip on plain SGD, would show the mistake. Put the division
back.
