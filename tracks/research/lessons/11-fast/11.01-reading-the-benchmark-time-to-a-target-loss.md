# 11.01 Reading the benchmark: time to a target loss

Module 10 left a GPT built the way open models are built now. This
module makes it train and write sooner without changing what it
computes: each change comes with a check that the numbers are the same
and a measurement before and after. Before anything can be made faster
it has to be measured, and a time is a number that moves, as a loss
does from seed to seed (00.03). This lesson gives the lab its timing:
the first step set aside, the same work timed again and again with the
machine's load beside every timing, and the smallest of several kept as
the closest to what the work costs. Then the measure the rest of the
module uses: the seconds of training until the bits on held-out text
reach a target. A step that is a third shorter is not sooner if it
learns less: with half the batch the GPT takes 294 steps to 3.0 bits
where the baseline takes 193, and arrives at about the same time.

## Where this fits

![Where 11.01 fits](figures/11.01-map.svg)

This lesson's piece is the module's own, in the models row: fast, the
GPT of module 10 made to give the same numbers sooner. It adds timing to
the lab and a race to the language loop: seeds of a model trained until
their held-out bits reach a target, the training timed and the scoring
not. It stands on 06.02's clock, which waits for the device, on 00.03's
spread and Welch's t, and on 08.04's loop and 10.05's GPT. Every later
lesson of the module adds a row to this lesson's table. After it a
change to the model or the training is judged by how much sooner the
same loss arrives, not by how fast a step looks.

## The idea

### A time is a draw from a spread

Time the same training step twice and the two numbers differ. The first
step of a process pays for things done once: memory found for every
tensor, the optimiser's averages made, caches filled. After that, every
step does the same work, but other programs on the machine take a core
from it now and then, for longer or shorter. They can make a timing
longer, never shorter. So one timing says little, and a timing taken
while the machine is busy says something about the machine.

The **load average** is how busy the machine is: the number of
processes running or waiting for a core, averaged over the last minute
(`os.getloadavg()`'s first number). This laptop has 14 cores and
PyTorch uses 10 threads; at a load past the cores, work waits for them.
The module's numbers were taken with other work running, at loads from
2 to 20, and every time in it comes with its load.

### The smallest, the median, the largest

The lab times a piece of work in **rounds**: one call untimed (a
warm-up), then several timings, each of a few calls in a row, read by
06.02's clock before and after. It prints three numbers of the timings:
the smallest, the **median** (the middle one when they are put in
order), and the largest.

The track's notes record the same step of the page's GPT, 15 timings of
5 steps each, at three loads. At load 6: the smallest 28.5 ms, the
median 28.8, the largest 33.7. At load 10 to 11: the smallest 41, the
median 50, the largest 91.7, more than twice the smallest. At load 18:
the smallest 53, medians of 60 to 80. And on the quiet start of the
module's recorded benchmark, at load 2.4, with a larger GPT, all 15
within 6% of each other. The smallest moved least with the load: what
the step itself costs is closest to it, since nothing can make a step
faster than its work. The median says what a step takes on this machine
as it is now. The largest says how busy it was. The first step of the
process is none of these: 44 ms against 28.5 at load 6, 238 against 41
at load 10.

![One training step of the page's GPT, timed in 3 rounds of 5 timings of 5 steps, as the machine was and again with 10 more processes spinning beside it: each timing a ring, the smallest, the median and the largest of the 15 marked, the first step of each process apart](figures/11.01-repeats.svg)

### Tokens a second, and why it is not the goal

A training step of the page's GPT reads 32 windows of 64 characters,
2,048 tokens. Its **throughput**, tokens a second, is those tokens over
the time of a step: 71,924 a second at the smallest time at load 6. It
is the number most speed-ups are reported in, and it can be raised
without making training any sooner: halve the batch and each step is
shorter, but each step is also a noisier estimate of the gradient and
moves the model less far. A run is not a number of steps; it is the
model that comes out.

### The time to a target loss

So the module's measure is the **time to a target**: the seconds of
training until the model's bits per character on held-out text first
reach a chosen value. modded-nanogpt, a public race to train a small
GPT fast, is run this way: "the fastest algorithm to use 8 NVIDIA H100
GPUs to train a language model that attains 3.28 cross-entropy loss on
the FineWeb validation set". Its first record took 45 minutes and its
92nd 0.665 of a minute. Its rules also say what this module's rule is:
a new record must show, over several runs, that its loss reaches the
target with "a statistical significance level of p<0.01", unless the
change only makes the same computation faster.

Here the target is 3.0 bits per character on 32 fixed windows of the
validation text, scored every 10 steps. The scoring is not timed: it is
the measurement, not the training. The target falls between two logged
rows, one above it and one at or below; the crossing is put on the
straight line between them, in steps and in seconds alike.

![bench.time_to on the baseline's seed 0: held-out bits logged every 10 steps, the target crossed between two rows, and the crossing put on the straight line from one to the other](figures/11.01-crossing.svg)

Steps to the target are the run's own numbers: the same seed gives the
same steps on any machine at any load. The seconds are the clock's, and
move as the step's time does. Both are reported, over seeds.

### What the run does

1. One training step of 10.05's "all four" GPT (RMSNorm, rotary
   positions, SwiGLU, 2 key and value heads for 4; 4 blocks of 4 heads,
   width 64): the first step of the process, then 3 rounds of 5 timings
   of 5 steps, each round's smallest, median and largest with the load;
   tokens a second at the smallest.
2. The time to 3.0 bits, 2 seeds a row: the baseline, the model as
   module 10 left it, which every later lesson compares with, and half
   the batch, a step that is faster and learns less. The steps, the
   seconds, Welch's t on the seconds, ms a step and tokens a second; the
   seconds kept in `times.json`, beside the runs.

### `load_average`

How busy the machine is, as the system counts it: the processes running
or waiting for a core over the last minute.

### `timings`

Work called once untimed, then timed again and again, each timing over
a few calls in a row: the seconds a call, one number a timing.

### `spread_ms`

The timings in one line: the smallest first, then the median, the
largest, how many, and the load they were taken at.

### `time_to`

Where a run's held-out bits first reach the target, between the logged
rows on either side: the steps taken by then and the seconds.

### `race`

Seeds of one GPT trained to the target, the training timed and the
scoring not, and each seed's steps and seconds returned.

### `rows`

The rows a benchmark can run, each a change to the model's arguments or
to the training's settings; this lesson has two, and each later lesson
adds one.

### `board`

The table: each row's seconds over seeds, its steps, its ratio to the
first row with Welch's t, ms a step and tokens a second.

Every time above is this run's, at this run's load; run it again and
they move. The steps, and every number written in `log.jsonl` and
`summary.json`, do not.

**The first step is not the step.** In every run the track's notes
record, the first step of the process takes longer than any timing
after it: 44 ms against 28.5 at load 6, 238 against 41 at load 10. A
benchmark that timed from the first step would charge one change with
another's set-up. The lab never times it, and `race` takes one untimed
step of the optimiser before its seeds, as 10.01's `compare` does.

**Rounds that agree, and rounds that do not.** With little else to do,
the rounds agree to a few percent and their smallest timings to a
percent or two: the largest of the fifteen is 1.06 times the smallest
in the notes' run at load 2.4 and 1.18 times at load 6. On a busy
machine, one timing of fifteen
can take twice as long as the rest. Their **mean**, the usual summary,
follows it: the fifteen timings at load 10 have a mean of 52.4 ms and a
spread (00.03's sd) of 11.2, most of it from that one timing of 91.7,
where the median is 50 and the smallest 41. The page prints the smallest
first, and compares changes by it when they are timed side by side.

**Half the batch: each step shorter, more of them.** 193 steps for the
baseline and 294 for half the batch, the same in every run on every
machine. In seconds the two arrive together: at load 6, 5.41 s against
5.46, x1.01, t = +0.3, each of half the batch's steps a third shorter
(18.6 ms against 28.0) and 52% more of them; at load 10, 8.41 against
9.76, x1.16, t = +1.6, inside the noise of two seeds. Tokens a second
are higher for the full batch, 73,247 against 55,182 at load 6: a step
of twice the batch is not twice as long, a thing 11.05 measures. Neither
batch is sooner to the target, and the steps say why more plainly than
the seconds: a faster step that learns less a step is not sooner.

## How it works

### The scoring is left out of the clock

```
            if times is not None:
                now = lab.clock(torch, dev)
                spent, mark = spent + now - mark, now
                times.append(spent)
```

At each logged row of 08.04's loop the time since the last mark is
added to what was spent, before the held-out windows are scored; once
the scoring is done, the mark is taken again, so the next stretch starts
after it. What is timed is the training alone. `lab.clock` waits for
the device first (06.02), so on a GPU this is the work, not the time to
queue it.

### Seconds stay out of the summary

```
        run.finish(steps_to_target=steps, held_bits=last["held_bits"], steps=last["step"] + 1)
```

A race's `summary.json` has the steps to the target and the last
held-out bits, and no seconds; the seconds go to `times.json`, beside the
runs. So the same seed gives the same `log.jsonl` and `summary.json`
byte for byte, 00.02's rule, and only one file of a benchmark differs
from run to run.

### Between two rows, a straight line

```
            f = (b - target) / (b - b1)
            return s0 + 1 + f * (row["step"] - s0), t0 + f * (seconds - t0)
```

`b` and `b1` are the held-out bits at the last row above the target and
the first at or below it, `f` how far between them the target lies. A
row's step counts from 0, so after the row of step `s0`, `s0 + 1` steps
have been taken. The same `f` places the seconds between the clock at
the two rows. The bits do not fall along a straight line between rows
ten steps apart, but nearly, and the error is the same for every row of
a table.

## Decide the boundary

The table compares rows by their mean seconds over seeds, each seed's
seconds its own clock's. A change that is 5% faster can be lost in a
spread of 8% from the machine's load, or found where there is none if
one row ran while another program was busy. This lesson reports the
smallest timing and the median beside it, with the load, and the steps,
which no load moves. Another lab could time on a machine with nothing
else running, or count operations instead of seconds, and pay with a
number that is not what a run on the reader's own machine takes. When
the machine is shared, which number would you put in a paper's table,
and what would you print beside it?

0 still takes 191 steps: its row at step 190 was already under the

295 and 293, to 301. Differences under the scoring's 10 steps are now
decided by where each run happens to cross the grid of rows: the
baseline's two seeds, 5 steps apart, are now 10 apart, and half the
batch's, 2 apart, are the same. Its seconds no longer measure when it
got there either, only which row it got there by. The measure can be no
finer than the scoring, unless something fills in between the rows.
Put the straight line back.
