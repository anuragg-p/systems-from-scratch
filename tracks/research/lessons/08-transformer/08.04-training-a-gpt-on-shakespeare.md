# 08.04 Training a GPT on Shakespeare

The GPT is whole; this lesson trains it, with everything the track has
built for training, and puts it on module 07's ladder. A minute of the
laptop's CPU is not enough: the page's run sees six tenths of the text once
and ends below the count of pairs and far above the rest of the ladder. The
module's long run, recorded, is a GPT of 2.7 million weights trained for 56
minutes, two seeds with dropout and two without. Without dropout its
held-out curve turns up two thirds of the way through, as Vaswani et al.'s
table says a transformer without dropout overfits; with dropout it is
still falling at the end, and both seeds end below the best model of
module 07.

## Where this fits

![Where 08.04 fits](figures/08.04-map.svg)

This lesson's piece is the GPT again, now trained: the model of 08.01 to
08.03 with module 03's AdamW, warmup and cosine decay, 05.02's dropout and
07.03's clipping, scored on module 07's ladder with 07.03's way of scoring
a whole text. The module's loop gains a curve of held-out bits as it
trains, and a checkpoint: the trained model in 02.05's safetensors format,
with the settings that build it written inside, so that the next lesson,
and modules 09 to 14, can load a GPT without knowing how it was made.
After this lesson the track has a trained character model better than
anything module 07 built.

## The idea

### Everything at once

The training loop is 08.01's, and every piece of it is a lesson of its
own: AdamW (03.04) at a rate warmed up over the first steps and then
lowered along half a cosine to 0 (03.05), weight decay 0.1 on the weight
matrices and the embeddings, the gradient clipped to a length of 1
(07.03). The model is 08.03's GPT with learned positions. Dropout (05.02)
sits where Vaswani et al. put it, section 5.4: "We apply dropout to the
output of each sub-layer, before it is added to the sub-layer input and
normalized. In addition, we apply dropout to the sums of the embeddings and
the positional encodings in both the encoder and decoder stacks." Ours
drops each block's two additions and the embeddings' sum, in training
only.

The score is module 07's: bits per character on the test text, each
character guessed with at least a quarter of a window before it, as
07.03's `window_bits()` reads a text, and beside it the same on the
validation text. Every setting is chosen on validation; every reported
number is on test, the end of The Taming of the Shrew and The Tempest,
which scores about 0.2 bits worse than validation for every model of
module 07.

### A curve of held-out bits

05.01 split its data into three to see memorising happen: the training
loss goes on falling while the loss on data the model never trains on
stops falling and turns up. That is **overfitting**, and the place it
starts is the place to have stopped. Here the loop logs, every `every`
steps, the bits of the training batch it just took and the bits of the
same 64 windows of the validation text, chosen once: one curve the model
trains on, one it never sees. Where the second turns up while the first
keeps falling, the model is learning the training plays at the cost of
the rest.

### The paper's claim, and the ladder

Vaswani et al.'s table 3, rows (D), varies the dropout of their base model
on English to German: without dropout, perplexity 5.77 and BLEU 24.6 on
their development set; at their base 0.1, 4.92 and 25.8; at 0.2, 4.95 and
25.5. Their reading: "dropout is very helpful in avoiding over-fitting".
That is the claim this lesson runs again: the same GPT with and without
dropout, where its held-out curve turns up, and what dropout does about
it. And the GPT goes on module 07's ladder, which ended at 2.283 bits, 07.02's
network mixed half and half with deleted interpolation.

### The checkpoint

The run keeps the model with the lowest validation bits of all its seeds
and settings, saves it as a safetensors file (02.05's
`lab/safetensors.py`), loads it back and checks that the loaded model
gives the same scores, number for number. Then the loaded model writes 500
characters, with 07.02's `sample()`.

### `held_bits`

The bits of a fixed batch of held-out windows, in eval mode (no dropout)
and without a graph.

### `save`

A GPT's weights as a safetensors file, and in its metadata, as JSON, the
settings that build it again and anything worth keeping beside them: the
steps, the seed, its validation bits.

### `load`

The GPT a checkpoint holds: its settings read from the file, the model
built from them, the weights loaded in; returned in eval mode, with the
metadata.

### `ladder`

Module 07's count models scored on the test text again, from their counts:
equal chances, single characters, pairs, the best add-k and deleted
interpolation, as 07.01 and 07.02 computed them.

### `lowest`

Where a run's validation curve was lowest, and where it ended.

**In a minute, below the pairs and nowhere near the rest.** The ladder is
module 07's own, the count models computed again to the same digits. The
GPT without dropout scores 3.332 bits on the test text: below 07.01's
pairs (3.599), far above add-k at four characters (2.629) and every network
of module 07. 300 steps of 32 windows of 64 are 614,400 characters, six
tenths of the training text, each seen once. Its text has speakers' names
in capitals and lines of about the right length, and few words.

**Dropout only costs here.** 3.474 with dropout 0.2 against 3.332 without,
t = +12.1. Each of the four validation curves is lowest at its last point,
step 299: nothing has turned up, because nothing has been seen often
enough to be memorised. Dropout makes the training harder, and there is no
overfitting yet for it to prevent.

So the page's run cannot show the paper's claim. That needs a model that
sees the text many times over.

## How it works

### The same windows every time

```
    held = windows(val_t, torch.from_numpy(np.random.default_rng(0).integers(0, len(val) - cfg.context, cfg.held)).to(dev),
                   cfg.context)
```

The validation windows are drawn once, from a generator of their own with
seed 0, before any run starts: every point of every curve, in every seed
and setting, is the same 64 windows. A fresh batch at each point would add
the windows' own differences to the curve, and a turn of a few hundredths
of a bit would drown in them. `held_bits()` scores them in eval mode, so a
model with dropout is measured whole.

### A checkpoint that builds itself

```
    metadata = {"config": json.dumps(config, sort_keys=True), **{k: json.dumps(v) for k, v in notes.items()}}
```

A safetensors file holds named arrays and a small dictionary of strings,
its metadata (02.05). `save()` writes into it the arguments that build the
GPT, so `load()` needs nothing but the file. The page's run left one, in
the project's folder:

The model the run chose, dropout 0.0 and seed 1, built again from the
file: 208,320 weights, in eval mode, ready to use. 08.05 loads its GPT
this way.

### Every character with a quarter of a window before it

```
    warm = cfg.context // 4
```

`window_bits()` cuts the test text into spans, each read in a window that
starts `warm` characters early, so every character is scored with at least
`warm` before it: 16 in the page's run, 64 in the long run, the same 64 as
07.03's recurrent network had, so the long run's numbers sit beside
module 07's.

## Decide the boundary

Without dropout, the long run's validation windows were lowest at 2.040
and 2.027; a run that had kept its model at that point, and stopped,
would have had them. With dropout and the whole run, 1.998 and 2.027:
better in one seed, level in the other. Stopping when the held-out curve
turns does part of dropout's work for nothing, and needs the curve to
know when; dropout costs every step a little of the model and lets it
train on. Is it better to stop a model when it starts to memorise, or to
make memorising harder?

## Go deeper

- [Attention Is All You Need](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762) by Vaswani et al., 2017 - section 5.4 for residual dropout, where it goes and the base model's rate of 0.1; section 5.3 for their optimiser, Adam with a warmup of 4000 steps and then a rate falling as one over the square root of the step; table 3, rows (D), for dropout and label smoothing.
- [nanoGPT](https://github.com/karpathy/nanoGPT) by Andrej Karpathy - its README trains a character-level GPT on Tiny Shakespeare, "a 6-layer Transformer with 6 heads in each layer" with "a context size of up to 256 characters", the long run's shape at twice its width, to a best validation loss of 1.4697 in nats (2.120 bits) on its own split of the text; and a smaller version for a laptop's CPU.
- [Build a Large Language Model (From Scratch)](https://github.com/rasbt/LLMs-from-scratch) by Sebastian Raschka - chapter 4, "Implementing a GPT Model from Scratch", and chapter 5, "Pretraining on Unlabeled Data", whose folder has a standalone training script, `gpt_train.py`.
