# 01.01 A derivative by nudging

Training moves every weight a little against the derivative of the loss,
so every derivative it uses has to be right. This lesson builds the check
the whole track holds its derivatives to: move the input a little each
way, watch the output, and divide. It needs no formula, only a function it
can call.

## Where this fits

![Where 01.01 fits](figures/01.01-map.svg)

The nudge is the first piece of the engine, and the one piece of it that is
never part of a model: it is a gradient check. It is given a function and a
point, calls the function, and gives back the slope there. It knows nothing
about how the function works, which is why it can check anything. The lab
of module 00 is still under it, recording every run. The pieces above it,
from the next lesson's values on, work out derivatives by program, and each
is held to the nudge. After this lesson the track can check any derivative
it is handed, and knows how close that check can get.

## The idea

The **derivative** of f at x is how much f(x) moves for each unit that x
moves, for a move small enough that the curve looks straight. The check
follows the words: move x by a small h each way, see how far f moved, and
divide. f(x + h) and f(x - h) are two points of the curve, one each side of
x, and the line through them is a **chord**; its slope is
(f(x + h) - f(x - h)) / 2h. As h shrinks the chord turns towards the
tangent, whose slope is the derivative. That is the **central difference**.
The **one-sided difference**, (f(x + h) - f(x)) / h, uses x itself and one
point to its right.

<!-- verify: fact cmd='python3 -B -c "from engine import nudge; print(round(nudge.derivative(lambda x: x ** 3 - 2 * x, 1.5, 0.1), 6))"' expect=4.76 -->

A worked example, the function this lesson's experiment uses:
f(x) = x^3 - 2x at x = 1.5. The rules of calculus give f'(x) = 3x^2 - 2,
which is 4.75 there. With h = 0.1, f(1.6) = 0.896 and f(1.4) = -0.056; their
difference, 0.952, over 0.2 is 4.76: off by 0.01. The figure draws the same
with h = 0.5, big enough to see: the central chord has slope 5, the
one-sided chord 7.25.

### `derivative`

The central difference of a function of one float, at one point: two calls
of the function, and a division. h is 1e-6 unless the caller gives one; why
that value and not a smaller one is what Run it shows.

### `gradient`

A function of several numbers has a **partial derivative** for each: how
much the result moves per unit move of that one input, with the others held
still. The list of them all is the **gradient**. `gradient()` nudges one
coordinate at a time, both ways, on copies of the point, so the caller's
list is never changed: two calls of the function for each input.

### `by_hand`

The experiment, `experiments/nudge.py`, holds the nudges to derivatives
worked out by hand. `by_hand()` returns them: f'(x) = 3x^2 - 2, and the
two partials of a function of two numbers, g(x, y) = x y^2 + sin(x) / y,
which are y^2 + cos(x) / y and 2 x y - sin(x) / y^2. The rest of the script
prints how far each nudge of f lands from 4.75, for h from 0.1 down to
1e-13, then nudges g at (1.5, 2) and compares, and plots the first table.

## How it works

![How far the nudge lands from 4.75 against h, both on log scales, from the run above: the central error is a V with its point at h = 1e-5; the one-sided error falls more slowly and joins it on the left](figures/01.01-v.svg)

### Why the error falls as h squared

For this f the chord's error can be worked out exactly. Expand the cubes:
(x + h)^3 = x^3 + 3x^2 h + 3x h^2 + h^3, and (x - h)^3 = x^3 - 3x^2 h +
3x h^2 - h^3. Their difference is 6x^2 h + 2h^3; the -2x part adds
-2(x + h) + 2(x - h) = -4h. So f(x + h) - f(x - h) = 6x^2 h + 2h^3 - 4h, and
dividing by 2h gives 3x^2 - 2 + h^2: the derivative plus h^2, exactly. That
is 1.0e-02 at h = 0.1, 1.0e-04 at 0.01, and on to 1.0e-08 at 1e-4: the first
four rows of the table, to every digit printed.

The one-sided difference keeps a term the central one cancels:
f(x + h) - f(x) = 3x^2 h + 3x h^2 + h^3 - 2h, and over h that is
3x^2 - 2 + 3x h + h^2, an error of 4.5h + h^2 at x = 1.5: 0.46 at h = 0.1,
the table's 4.600e-01.

For any smooth function the same expansion (Taylor's) gives an error of
about h^2 times a sixth of the third derivative for the central difference,
and h times half the second derivative for the one-sided one. Here the
third derivative is 6, which makes the first exactly h^2, and the second is
6x = 9, which makes the second 4.5h. The curve bends the same way on both
sides of x, so the central chord cancels the bend. That is why the track
always nudges both ways.

On a function where the two rules are not exact they still hold. For sin at
x = 1 with h = 0.01 they predict -cos(1) / 6 x 0.0001 = -9.005e-06 for the
central difference and -sin(1) / 2 x 0.01 = -4.207e-03 for the one-sided
one; the nudges are off by -9.005e-06 and -4.216e-03. (The one-sided rule
leaves out the next term, h^2 times a sixth of the third derivative, which
is the -9e-06 between the two.)

### Why it rises again

<!-- verify: fact cmd='python3 -c "import sys; print(sys.float_info.epsilon)"' expect=2.220446049250313e-16 -->

A Python float keeps about 16 significant digits: the gap between 1.0 and
the next float, `sys.float_info.epsilon`, is 2.220446049250313e-16.
f(1.5 + h) is made of x^3, about 3.4, and 2x, about
3, each rounded in its last digit, so it is off by around 1e-15 whatever h
is. As h shrinks, f(x + h) and f(x - h) agree in more and more of their
leading digits; the subtraction cancels those and leaves the rounding
behind, and the division by 2h then magnifies it: an error of about
1e-15 / 2h. That is 5e-10 at h = 1e-6, where the table has 4.5e-10, and
5e-3 at 1e-13, where it has 2.7e-3. Once it is larger than the chord's
error, it is the only error left, which is why the two columns end the
same.

<!-- verify: fact cmd='python3 -c "print(round((5e-16) ** (1 / 3) * 1e6, 1))"' expect=7.9 -->

The two errors are equal where h^2 = 1e-15 / 2h, that is h^3 = 5e-16, about
h = 7.9e-6: the table's closest nudge, 1e-5, is the power of ten next to it.
Where the point of the V sits depends on the function, on the size of its
numbers and on how much it bends. h = 1e-6, the default, sits near the
point for numbers of about one, but a nudge is never good to sixteen
digits: about ten, here. So the track compares a derivative with the nudge
by the size of the gap. A gap like 1e-10 is the nudge's own error, and the
two agree; a gap like 3.0, in Break it below, means one of them is wrong.

### Two calls per input

`gradient()` calls the function twice for each input. For g that is four
calls. For a network of a million weights it would be two million runs of
the network for one gradient, and each run is itself a million
multiplications. That is why the nudge is the track's check and not the way
it trains: the next lesson gets every partial derivative from one pass
backwards through the computation, and holds it to the nudge.

## Break it

Make the opening's slip in a hand-worked derivative the nudge can see. In
`by_hand()` in `experiments/nudge.py`, drop the 2 from the partial
derivative with respect to y:

<!-- verify: patch file=experiments/nudge.py
-    return 3 * x ** 2 - 2, [gy ** 2 + math.cos(gx) / gy, 2 * gx * gy - math.sin(gx) / gy ** 2]
@block
-->
```
    return 3 * x ** 2 - 2, [gy ** 2 + math.cos(gx) / gy, gx * gy - math.sin(gx) / gy ** 2]
```

and run nudge again, `./learn run research --at 01.01 -- nudge`. The table
is the same; its last lines are not:

```

dg/dy at (1.5, 2.0): nudge 5.750626254  by hand 2.750626253  difference 3.0e+00

```

The nudge printed the same 5.750626254 as before: it knows nothing of the
formula. The formula is now short by x y = 3.0 at (1.5, 2), ten orders of
magnitude more than the nudge's own error. The fit at the start of this
lesson made the same slip and said nothing; the nudge sees it at once. Put
the 2 back.

## Go deeper
