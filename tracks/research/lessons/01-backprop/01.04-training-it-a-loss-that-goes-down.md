# 01.04 Training it: a loss that goes down

Training is a loop: score every training point, measure how wrong the
scores are as one number, the loss; run `backward()`; move every weight a
little against its gradient; and again. This lesson trains 01.03's
network to tell two groups of points apart, and runs again, at laptop
size, the claim Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams made for backpropagation in
1986: that hidden units learn what a network without them cannot.

## Where this fits

![Where 01.04 fits](figures/01.04-map.svg)

This lesson fills two pieces of the map: the two groups, the module's
experiment, and the points it learns from, made from a seed. It uses
everything the module built: the network of 01.03, the backward pass of
01.02, and the nudge behind both. Under it, the lab of module 00 records

## The idea

![Seed 0's fifty training points and where each model, trained for 80 steps, puts its line between the groups: the straight line of a model with no hidden layer gets 44 of 50, the bent line of the network with two hidden layers all 50](figures/01.04-boundary.svg)

The data are points in the plane in two groups, two half circles that
reach into each other's gap; the figure shows the fifty that seed 0 trains
on. The model has to say which group a point is in. The obvious model has
no hidden layer: one unit, a weighted sum of the point's two coordinates
plus a bias, as the score; above 0 means group 1. That is
`MLP(2, [1])`, and the experiment takes the sizes as a setting:

Training does something: the loss falls to about a quarter of where it
started, and nine points in ten end up on the right side. Then it stops
improving.

<!-- verify: fact cmd='python3 -B -m experiments.two_groups "sizes=[1]" steps=800 | sed -n "s/^training accuracy *//p"' expect="0.9 +- 0.02 (min 0.88, max 0.92, 3 seeds)" -->

With ten times the steps, 800, the training accuracy is still
0.9 +- 0.02 (min 0.88, max 0.92, 3 seeds). The limit is the model, not
the training: a weighted sum is 0 along a straight line, so this model can
only cut the plane in two with a line, and no line separates these groups.
The figure's left panel is the line seed 0 found.

That is the problem Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams set out from in
"Learning representations by back-propagating errors" (Nature, 1986). Some
tasks "cannot be done by just connecting the input units to the output
units"; theirs was telling whether a row of six bits reads the same
backwards. With an intermediate layer, two hidden units, their network
learned it after 1,425 sweeps through its 64 possible inputs (their
Figure 1). Their claim, in their abstract's words, is that through the
weight changes of backpropagation "internal 'hidden' units which are not
part of the input or output come to represent important features of the
task domain". The claim run again here: a network with hidden units,
trained by backpropagation, learns a task that the same training cannot
teach a network without them. At laptop size, on our own task: these two
groups.

Training needs three things. A **loss**: one number that says how wrong
the scores are, smaller being better. A way to move each weight so that
the loss falls: **gradient descent**, which moves every weight by minus
the **learning rate** times its gradient, the paper's
Delta w = -epsilon dE/dw (their equation 8). And a loop that does it
again and again, each pass a **step**.

The loss here is the **hinge loss**. Give each point a sign t, +1 for
group 1 and -1 for group 0; t x score is positive when the point is on
its right side. The point's loss is relu(1 - t x score): nothing once its
score is on the right side by a margin of 1, and more the further it falls
short. A group 1 point scored 2 costs 0, scored 0.3 costs 0.7, scored -1
costs 2. The loss of the training set is the mean over its points.

### `two_groups`

Makes n points from the run's random number generator: half on the upper
half of a circle, half on the lower half of another, shifted right and
down into the first one's gap, each moved by a little noise, and
shuffled. The same seed gives the same points; the experiment draws 50 to
train on and then 200 fresh ones, which it never trains on, to test.

### `hinge_loss`

Runs the network on every point, which builds one graph of Values through
all of them, and returns the mean hinge loss, a Value, so that one
`backward()` reaches every weight from every point. It also returns the
fraction of points on the right side, a plain number: the **accuracy**.

### `train`

One step sets every weight's gradient to 0, computes the loss on all the
training points, logs it, runs `backward()`, and moves every weight by
`-lr * p.grad`. The experiment runs 80 steps at a learning rate of 0.5, for
three seeds, with MLP(2, [8, 8, 1]): two hidden layers of 8 relu units.

The network starts no better than a coin, accuracy 0.50 for two of the
seeds, and ends with all 50 training points right for seed 0 and 49 for
the others; on 200 fresh points it gets 0.975 +- 0.015. The loss falls
from about 1 to 0.012, 0.040 and 0.061, where the straight line's stayed
above 0.2: the figure shows the two side by side, and the right panel of
the first figure shows the bent line seed 0's network learned. The curves
of the run are also in runs/two_groups/loss.svg in its folder.

So the claim held, at this size. Beside the paper's: their network, two
hidden units, learned its task in 1,425 sweeps through 64 cases; ours, 16
hidden units, reached 0.987 +- 0.0115 of its training points in 80 sweeps
through 50, where a network without hidden units, trained the same way,
stops at 0.9. The tasks differ, so the sweep counts are not a race. Not
tested here: their tasks, their units (the logistic function, where
these use the relu), their error (the squared error, where this uses the
hinge loss), their momentum, which module 03 brings, and what the hidden
units come to represent, which needs a look inside the network that this
lesson does not take. Three seeds is also too few to say anything about
their remark that the network "very rarely gets stuck in poor local
minima", beyond that none of the three did.

## How it works

### Why a small step lowers the loss

Move every weight w_i by a small amount d_i. By the definition of the
derivative, 01.01's, the loss moves by about g_i x d_i for each, g_i being
its gradient, and for small moves the changes add up: the loss changes by
about the sum of g_i x d_i. Gradient descent takes d_i = -lr x g_i, so the
change is about -lr times the sum of the g_i^2: never positive. One step
of seed 0 at a small rate, then at the experiment's own:


<!-- verify: fact cmd='python3 -B -c "import numpy as np; from engine.value import MLP; from data.points import two_groups; from experiments.two_groups import hinge_loss; x, y = two_groups(50, 0.1, np.random.default_rng(0)); m = MLP(2, [8, 8, 1], seed=0); hinge_loss(m, x, y)[0].backward(); print(round(sum(p.grad ** 2 for p in m.parameters()), 3))"' expect=1.607 -->

At the start of seed 0 the sum of the squared gradients is 1.607. At
lr = 0.005 that predicts a fall of 0.005 x 1.607 = 0.008, and the loss
went from 1.161 to 1.153. At lr = 0.5 it predicts 0.80, and the loss fell
by 0.49: a step that big has gone past where the straight-line estimate
holds. It still went down here, and further than the small step did, but
nothing promises that it will. The rate is a choice, made by trying, and
module 03 is about making it.

### Gradients back to 0 every step

```
        for p in params:
            p.grad = 0.0
```

`backward()` adds to every gradient (01.02), so without these two lines
each step's gradients would land on top of all the earlier ones. With
them, the **invariant** at each update is that every `p.grad` is the
gradient of this step's loss and nothing else. Break it shows what
happens without.

### Training points and fresh points

Accuracy on the training points says how well the network fits what it
was shown; accuracy on fresh points, drawn from the same two half circles
but never trained on, says how well it learned the groups. Here the two
are close, 0.987 and 0.975. They need not be: a network can fit every
training point and be wrong on fresh ones, which is module 05's subject.
A claim that a model works is a claim about points it did not train on.

## Decide the boundary

This loop computes the loss on all 50 points before every step, so each
step follows the gradient of the whole training set, and costs a run of
the network on every point. The paper names the other choice: change the
weights after every single case, which needs no memory for the sum and
takes 50 steps for the same work, each one following the gradient of one
point instead of all of them. Between the two is a step after each small
batch of points, which is what module 03 does on 60,000 digits. Is one
exact step worth more than many rough ones?

## Break it

Forget to set the gradients back to 0. In `train()` in
`experiments/two_groups.py`, make the loop do nothing:

<!-- verify: patch file=experiments/two_groups.py
-            p.grad = 0.0
@block
-->
```
            pass
```

and train again (`./learn run research --at 01.04 -- two_groups`):

<!-- verify: session wait=240 run="./learn run research --at 01.04 -- two_groups" timeout=900 -->
```
seed 0: loss 1.161 -> 1.048, accuracy 0.50 -> 0.64, on fresh points 0.65
seed 1: loss 0.875 -> 0.653, accuracy 0.68 -> 0.82, on fresh points 0.75
seed 2: loss 1.176 -> 0.663, accuracy 0.50 -> 0.70, on fresh points 0.65
training accuracy   0.72 +- 0.0917 (min 0.64, max 0.82, 3 seeds)
accuracy on fresh points 0.683 +- 0.0577 (min 0.65, max 0.75, 3 seeds)
curves in runs/two_groups/loss.svg
```

Nothing crashed, the loss went down for every seed, and the network is
now worse than the straight line: 0.72 of its training points instead of
0.987. Each step's gradients were added to every earlier step's, so the
step at step k was the learning rate times the sum of k gradients: steps
that grew as training went on, and kept pushing in directions the loss had
long stopped going down in. A bug like this gives a curve that looks like
training and a result that is merely bad, which is why every result in the
track is compared with a baseline. Put `p.grad = 0.0` back.

## Go deeper

- [Learning representations by back-propagating errors](https://doi.org/10.1038/323533a0) by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams (Nature, 1986) - the abstract states the claim; Figure 1 and its caption are the symmetry network (six inputs, two hidden units, 1,425 sweeps, epsilon 0.1 and alpha 0.9); page 535 has why a layer of hidden units is needed for it, the two ways of using dE/dw (after every case, or summed over all of them, which they used), equation 8 for the plain step and 9 for the step with momentum, and their remark on local minima.
- [micrograd](https://github.com/karpathy/micrograd) by Andrej Karpathy - its README describes `demo.ipynb`, the same job as this lesson's: a binary classifier with two hidden layers of 16 on the moon dataset, an "svm max-margin" loss, and plain gradient descent.
- [The spelled-out intro to neural networks and backpropagation: building micrograd](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VMj-3S1tku0) by Andrej Karpathy - "creating a tiny dataset, writing the loss function" (1:51:04) and "doing gradient descent optimization manually, training the network" (2:01:12), where he finds he forgot to zero the gradients before the backward pass: this lesson's Break it, in his words "for the 20th time in my life".
