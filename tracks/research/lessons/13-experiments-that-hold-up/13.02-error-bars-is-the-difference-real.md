# 13.02 Error bars: is the difference real?

08.02 cut a GPT's attention into four heads at the same number of weights
and found it a little better than one head: 3.407 bits per character
against 3.433, t = -2.4 at three seeds, inside the noise at two. Its page
said the direction was the paper's and the size "not settled". This
lesson settles it, and gives the lab what it takes: the standard error,
Student's t and intervals, the bootstrap, paired runs, the test text's
own noise, and how many seeds a difference needs. At 10 seeds a side
four heads are better by 0.025 bits, lower on 10 of 10 seeds. 08.02's
caution was right for three seeds compared as two separate sets; but its
comparison was paired all along, one seed giving both arms the same start
and the same batches, and paired, its three seeds had already decided it.

## Where this fits

## The idea

### The result, as 08.02 reported it

08.02's part 3: a GPT of 4 blocks of width 64 without positions, 300
steps of 32 windows of 32 characters, one head of 64 against four heads
of 16. The heads change only how each position's query, key and value
are cut, so both models have exactly 204,224 weights. On the test text,
two seeds gave 3.430 against 3.406 bits per character, t = -1.3; three
gave 3.433 against 3.407, t = -2.4, "a little beyond the noise, and
still the seeds touch". Vaswani et al.'s table 3 has four heads better
than one too. Is the difference real?

### How it could be wrong

Two ways. Three seeds may be too few to see a real difference of this
size, or to tell it from a difference that chance made: Welch's t at
three seeds is itself rough, and 00.03's "past about 3" was for five
seeds a side. And every number is a score on one test text, the last
5% of Tiny Shakespeare, most of it The Tempest; another stretch of
Shakespeare would score both models differently, and nothing in a spread
over seeds says by how much.

### How far a mean moves: the standard error

00.03 derived it: a mean of n draws moves from one set of n to another
by the sd of one draw divided by the square root of n. That is the
**standard error** of the mean, sem = sd / sqrt(n). One head's three
seeds have sd 0.0158, so their mean's sem is 0.0091.

### Student's t, and degrees of freedom

A mean within 1.96 standard errors of the truth 95 times in 100 is the
normal distribution's rule, and it holds when the sd is known. Here the
sd was measured from the same three numbers, and three numbers can
easily happen to sit close together: the sd comes out small, and the
interval with it. **Student's t** is the spread of (mean - truth) / sem
when the sem is measured that way, from numbers drawn from a normal
distribution. It is a bell like the normal's with fatter tails, and how
fat depends on the **degrees of freedom**: the number of independent
distances from the mean that the sd is made of, n - 1, since the n
distances from their own mean always add up to 0 and the last is fixed
by the others. At 2 degrees of freedom 95% of t lies within 4.30, at 9
within 2.26; with many, it is the normal's 1.96.

![Student's t: how far a mean of n seeds falls from the truth, in its own standard errors, when the standard error was measured from the same n; at 3 seeds (2 degrees of freedom) and at 10 (9), beside the normal; 95% of each lies between its marks](figures/13.02-t.svg)

A **95% interval** is mean +- t sem with t at 95%: a range made by a
rule that holds the true mean, the mean of infinitely many seeds, in 95
of 100 experiments run this way. Two arms differ when the interval of
their difference does not hold 0. That is the same decision as Welch's
t past the 95% point, written as a range of sizes the difference could
have.

### Paired runs

Two arms are **paired** when each seed's run of one arm shares
something with that seed's run of the other: here the same starting
weights (the heads change no weight's shape, so `torch.manual_seed(seed)`
draws the same numbers for both) and the same batches in the same order
(the run's own generator). 08.02's experiment did both without saying
so. Then the seed-by-seed differences leave out whatever a seed does to
both arms alike, a lucky start or a kind batch order, and their spread
can be far smaller than either arm's. The interval of a paired difference
is the interval of those differences. Unpaired, the two standard errors
are added in squares, as 00.03's Welch's t does.

### Resampling the seeds

The **bootstrap** makes an interval without a formula: draw the
seeds' numbers again, as many as there are, with replacement (one may
come twice, another not at all), take the mean, and do it 10,000 times;
the middle 95% of those means is the interval. It works for any
statistic, a median or a correlation as easily as a mean. It knows only
the numbers it has, and with three numbers that is very little.

### The test text is a sample too

Miller (2024) puts the evaluation set as a sample: "Suppose that the
questions in an eval do not represent all possible questions, but instead
were drawn at random from a (hypothetical, infinite, unseen)
super-population of questions". The test text is the same: a score is a
mean over its 55,754 positions, and its standard error says how far
another stretch of text drawn the same way would move it. But
neighbouring positions are not independent: they share a scene, its
speakers and its names. So the positions are taken in **batches** of
consecutive windows and the standard error is the batches' (Miller's
clustered standard errors, with the clusters made from the text's
order). As the batches grow it rises, until they are longer than what
moves together.

Bouthillier et al. (2021) measured where the variance of a benchmark
comes from and found the data's sampling the largest source:
"Bootstrapping data stands out as the most important source of variance.
In contrast, model initialization generally is less than 50% of the
variance of bootstrap". For one model's score here, the run measures both.

### How many seeds

A study can be planned: the chance that a true difference of a given
size comes out with its 95% interval clear of 0 is its **power**, and
four times in five is a common aim. From the spread a few seeds show, the
number of seeds for that follows: the size must be about (t at 97.5% plus
t at 80%) standard errors of the difference.

### What the run does

1. 08.02's two arms, seeds 0 to 2: their spreads and Welch's t, and
   whether one seed starts both arms from the same weights.
2. Each arm's mean, its standard error, and three 95% intervals:
   Student's t, the normal's 1.96 in its place, and the bootstrap.
3. The difference, as two independent sets and as pairs.
4. Seed 0's models scored position by position: the standard error of
   each score over the test text, in batches of 1 to 256 windows, and of
   the difference.
5. The seeds a side that differences of 0.010, 0.026 and 0.050 need.
6. The recorded run, 10 seeds a side, read and judged the same way.

### `sem`

The standard error of a mean: the sd over the square root of n.

### `t_density`

Student's t's density at x for df degrees of freedom, from its formula.

### `t_cdf`

The chance that t is below a value: the area under the density, by
Simpson's rule.

### `t_quantile`

The value t is below with a given chance: `t_cdf()` turned round, by
Newton's method.

### `interval`

A mean and its 95% interval, with t at the seeds' degrees of freedom.

### `difference`

The difference of two arms' means with its interval, paired or not.

### `bootstrap`

An interval by resampling, for any statistic of any groups of numbers.

### `seeds_needed`

The seeds a side for a difference of a given size to show, four times
in five, at a measured spread.

### `batch_sem`

The standard error of a mean of numbers whose neighbours move together:
the sem of the means of consecutive batches.

### `position_bits`

07.03's `window_bits()` score, kept position by position instead of
averaged.

## How it works

### Why 4.30 and not 1.96

```
    half = t_quantile((1 + level) / 2, len(xs) - 1) * sem(xs)
    return mean(xs) - half, mean(xs) + half
```

A 95% interval needs the t that Student's t is below with chance 0.975,
leaving 2.5% in each tail. `t_cdf()` adds up the density from 0 by
Simpson's rule (each slice of the curve taken as a parabola through three
points), and `t_quantile()` turns that round with Newton's method: from
t = 0, each step goes the chance still missing divided by the density
there, the slope of the chance. In the project's folder:

<!-- verify: session needs="torch" -->
```
$ cd build/at/research/13.02
$ python3 -c "
import lab
print([round(lab.t_quantile(0.975, df), 3) for df in (1, 2, 4, 9, 30, 1000)])
"
[12.706, 4.303, 2.776, 2.262, 2.042, 1.962]
```

The t table's values: 12.706 at 1 degree of freedom (2 seeds), 4.303 at
2, 2.262 at 9 (10 seeds), and 1.962 at a thousand, the normal's 1.96.
With 2 seeds a 95% interval is 12.7 standard errors either side of the
mean: two seeds can say almost nothing about where a mean is.

### Welch's degrees of freedom

```
    va, vb = sem(a) ** 2, sem(b) ** 2
    df = (va + vb) ** 2 / (va ** 2 / (len(a) - 1) + vb ** 2 / (len(b) - 1))
```

Unpaired, the noise in a difference of two means is sqrt(va + vb), as
00.03 derived, with va and vb the squared standard errors. Its degrees
of freedom are not simply the seeds less two: when one arm's spread is
much the larger, the sum is mostly that arm's, and has about that arm's
degrees of freedom. Welch and Satterthwaite's formula weighs the two that
way.

<!-- verify: fact cmd="python3 -c \"import json, lab; a, b = ([json.load(open(f'runs/errorbars/heads{h}/seed{s}/summary.json'))['test_bits'] for s in range(3)] for h in (4, 1)); va, vb = lab.sem(a) ** 2, lab.sem(b) ** 2; print(round((va + vb) ** 2 / (va ** 2 / 2 + vb ** 2 / 2), 1))\"" expect=3.2 -->

For the page's three seeds it gives 3.2, between one arm's 2 and both
arms' 4.

### What a pair takes away

```
        lo, hi = interval([x - y for x, y in zip(a, b, strict=True)], level)
```

For one seed's two runs a and b, the variance of a - b is var(a) +
var(b) - 2 cov(a, b), where cov, the covariance, is the average product
of the two runs' distances from their means; Miller writes the same "- 2
Cov(s_A, s_B)" for two models scored on the same questions. When the
arms move together the covariance is positive and the difference moves
less than either arm. The paired interval simply takes the differences
as the numbers, so the covariance is in their spread without being
computed. `strict=True` refuses arms of unequal length: a pair needs both
of its runs.

### Ten resamples, and a sum divided by n

<!-- verify: session needs="torch" -->
```
$ python3 -c "
import itertools, json, lab
one = [json.load(open(f'runs/errorbars/heads1/seed{s}/summary.json'))['test_bits'] for s in range(3)]
means = {round(sum(one[i] for i in c) / 3, 9) for c in itertools.product(range(3), repeat=3)}
print(len(means), round(min(one), 4), round(max(one), 4), [round(v, 4) for v in lab.interval(one)])
"
10 3.4149 3.4442 [3.3937, 3.4724]
$ cd ../../../..
```

Every resample of three seeds, 27 orders, makes only 10 different means,
all of them between the smallest seed and the largest, 3.4149 and
3.4442; the t interval runs from 3.3937 to 3.4724. Resampling also
spreads its means as if the variance were the sum of squared distances
over n, not n - 1 (00.03's correction), and gives no fat tails for an sd
that was itself measured. At ten seeds those matter less, and the
bootstrap's interval comes close to t's; at three it is too narrow by
half. It is the right tool where no formula exists, and the wrong one
for the mean of three seeds.

### Batches that grow until they stop

```
    k = len(xs) // size
    return sem([mean(xs[i * size:(i + 1) * size]) for i in range(k)])
```

If neighbouring positions moved independently, the means of batches of
any size would give the same standard error of the whole mean. They do
not: a batch of 64 windows, 1,088 positions, is part of one scene, and
its mean carries that scene's difficulty. The standard error grows with
the batch until a batch is longer than what moves together, then levels;
past that, too few batches are left to measure it (12 at 256 windows).
The difference of two models, position by position, is six times
smaller and grows little, from 0.0014 to 0.0018 at 64 windows: a hard
scene is hard for both.

### Four times in five

```
        t = t_quantile((1 + level) / 2, df) + t_quantile(power, df)
        if n >= k * (t * sd / size) ** 2:
```

For the interval to clear 0, the measured difference must come out more
than t at 97.5% standard errors from 0. If the true difference is size,
the measured one falls around it with its own standard error, and it
lands that far out four times in five when size is t at 97.5% plus t at
80% standard errors from 0. The standard error is sd sqrt(2 / n) for two
independent arms of n and sd / sqrt(n) for n pairs, so n follows; the
t's depend on n, so the code starts from the normal's answer and counts
up until they agree.

## Decide the boundary

This lesson ran more seeds of one fixed test text and paired the arms
by seed. Bouthillier et al. randomise everything a run depends on,
including which examples are trained and tested on ("As many sources of
variation as possible should be randomized whenever possible"), so that
a comparison holds for the method, not for one split of the data; that
costs far more runs and changes every number on every page that keeps a
fixed split. A fixed split answers for this text, cheaply and exactly
repeatably. Which question is your result about: these two models on
this text, or the two methods on any text like it?

## Go deeper

- [Accounting for Variance in Machine Learning Benchmarks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03098) by Bouthillier et al., MLSys 2021 - sections 2 and 3 for the sources of variance (the data's sampling, augmentation, initialisation, dropout, the order of the batches, the hyperparameter search) and how large each is; section 4 and appendix C for their decision rule, P(A > B) against a threshold of 0.75, how many trainings it needs, and why to pair, "re-using the same seed for A and B in each pairs".
- [Adding Error Bars to Evals: A Statistical Approach to Language Model Evaluations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640) by Evan Miller, 2024 - the evaluation set as a sample of a super-population, its standard error and "1.96 x SE", clustered standard errors for questions that come in groups, paired differences ("We therefore recommend using the paired version of the standard error estimate wherever practicable"), and the power analysis `seeds_needed()` follows with t's for z's.
- [Deep Reinforcement Learning that Matters](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.06560) by Henderson et al., AAAI 2018 - the section on random seeds and trials, and figure 5: ten runs of one method split into two groups of five, t = -9.0916, "the variance between runs is enough to create statistically different distributions just from varying random seeds"; then the section on reporting evaluation metrics, with bootstrap intervals and a power analysis.
