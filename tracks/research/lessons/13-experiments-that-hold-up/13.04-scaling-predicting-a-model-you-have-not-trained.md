# 13.04 Scaling: predicting a model you have not trained

The track has leaned more than once on a larger model scoring better:
08.02's four blocks beat one, and every long run since has trained a
larger model than its page's minute could. Kaplan et al. (2020) say by
how much: the loss falls as a power
of the number of weights, a straight line on log-log axes, so a line
through small models can be followed to a large one before it is
trained. This lesson fits that line through five widths of the track's
GPT on TinyStories, writes down what it predicts for a model four times
larger than any it was fitted to, and then trains that model. The
prediction, made while the largest model was still training, was 3.904
bits per token, between 3.836 and 3.973; trained, it scored 3.925,
inside. Counting the embeddings as weights too, the same runs predict
3.803, and the trained model is outside that interval, 3.2% above: the
paper's reason for leaving the embeddings out, held by a prediction. The
exponent here, 0.097, is not the paper's 0.076, and is not the same
measurement.

## The idea

### The result, and Kaplan et al.'s law

08.02 found four blocks better than one at the same width, 3.406 bits
per character against 3.503, and the long runs of modules 08 to 10 each
trained a larger model than its page's minute could, for a lower score.
How much lower should a larger model be? Kaplan et al. trained language
models from 768 to 1.5 billion weights and found the loss to fall in a
regular way with size. Their equation 1.1: "For models with a limited
number of parameters, trained to convergence on sufficiently large
datasets: L(N) = (N_c / N)^alpha_N; alpha_N ~ 0.076, N_c ~ 8.8 x 10^13
(non-embedding parameters)", L the cross-entropy in nats on held-out
text. If that holds at a laptop's sizes too, the small models already
trained say what a larger one will score.

### A power law

A **power law** is y = a x^-alpha: y falls by the same factor every time
x is multiplied by the same factor. alpha is its **exponent**: with
alpha = 0.1, doubling x multiplies y by 2^-0.1, 0.933, a fall of 6.7%,
whatever x was. Take logarithms and it is a straight line, ln y = ln a -
alpha ln x: slope -alpha on log-log axes. So a power law is fitted by
fitting a line to the logarithms, and a line can be followed past its
last point. Kaplan et al.'s (N_c / N)^alpha is the same law with a
written as N_c^alpha; the exponent is the same in bits as in nats, since
bits are nats divided by ln 2.

### Which weights are N

The paper is exact about what it counts. Its equation 2.1: "N ~ 2 d_model
n_layer (2 d_attn + d_ff) = 12 n_layer d_model^2 ... where we have
excluded biases and other sub-leading terms. Our models also have n_vocab
d_model parameters in an embedding matrix, and use n_ctx d_model
parameters for positional embeddings, but we do not include these when
discussing the 'model size' N; we will see that this produces
significantly cleaner scaling laws." Figure 6 shows why: "When we exclude
embedding parameters, the performance of models with different depths
converge to a single trend." This lesson counts N the paper's way, every
weight but the two embedding tables (biases and layer norms included, a
few per cent at the smallest width), and also every weight, to see what
the other count does.

### The experiment

08's GPT at one depth, 4 blocks of 4 heads, the width W varied, so that
N is about 12 x 4 x W^2 = 48 W^2. Text: 09.04's TinyStories tokens, a
vocabulary of 4096, the model's own embedding table 4096 x W. Every
width trains for the same number of tokens with 08.04's loop (AdamW,
warmup then cosine to 0) at one learning rate, from the same seeds, and
is scored on the first held-out stories in bits per token, with bits per
byte beside it as 09.04 reports.

### A forecast written down first

The law is fitted through every run of every width but the largest, and
what it says for the largest is written down, with an **interval**: the
range that holds the mean of that width's runs with chance 95%, given how
far the fitted runs scatter about the line and how far beyond them the
largest lies. Then the largest is trained. Inside the interval, the law
held at that size; outside, it did not.

### The recorded run

Five widths to fit, 32, 48, 64, 96 and 128, three seeds each, and width
256 to predict, two seeds: its N, 3,159,552, is four times width 128's.
Windows of 256 tokens, 800 steps of 32 windows, 6.55 million tokens for
every width, 0.39 passes over our 16.7 million training tokens, so no
model sees a token twice. 51 minutes of this laptop's CPU with other work
running; the prediction for width 256 was worked out from the five
widths' summaries while width 256's first seed was training. The page's
run is the same script at sizes that take seconds, and it reads the
recorded run.

### What the run does

1. Each width's weights, counted the paper's way, as 12 L W^2, and all
   of them.
2. Each width trained and scored, two seeds of each.
3. The law fitted through all but the largest, its prediction for the
   largest with an interval, and the largest's runs beside it, both ways
   of counting.
4. The recorded run's table, fitted and judged the same way.

### `power_law`

A straight line through the logarithms of the points, by least squares:
the exponent, the constant, and what an interval needs.

### `power_interval`

What a fitted law says at x, with the 95% range for the mean of k new
runs there.

### `counted`

A GPT's weights as the paper counts them: all but the two embedding
tables.

### `predict`

The test: the law through every width but the largest, its interval for
the largest, and whether the largest's runs land inside.

**The exponent is not the paper's.** 0.097 here, 0.076 there, and the
two are not the same measurement. The paper's is for models "trained to
convergence on sufficiently large datasets": 2.5 x 10^5 steps of 512
windows of 1024 tokens on WebText2's 22 billion tokens. Ours stopped
every width at the same 6.55 million tokens, far from convergence: every
curve is still falling when the run ends.

![Every width's held-out bits over the recorded run (the mean of its seeds, 64 held-out windows every 50 steps), against the tokens seen, both on log scales: every curve is still falling when the run stops](figures/13.04-curves.svg)

For a fixed number of steps the paper's own law is its equation 1.6,
L(N, S) = (N_c / N)^alpha_N + (S_c / S_min(S))^alpha_S: a power law in
N plus a term for the steps, which at a fixed S is a constant, and which
would flatten the line on log-log axes at large N, a bend not seen over
our range. Larger models are also "more sample-efficient than small
models, reaching the same level of performance with fewer optimization
steps", so at equal tokens, early in training, they are further ahead
than at convergence. Nor is the data what the paper means by large
enough: nothing here was seen twice, so nothing was overfitted, but
their rule for training without overfitting, D of about 5 x 10^3
N^0.74 tokens or more, asks for 3.2 x 10^8 at our largest N, 19 times
the 16.7 million we have. One learning rate served every size (theirs
fell with size), one depth (they varied both), windows of 256 (theirs
1024), a vocabulary of 4096 and children's stories. The paper itself
says its constants "depend on the vocabulary size and tokenization and
hence do not have a fundamental meaning"; our N_c, 10^12.6 in bits on
our tokens, means nothing beside its 8.8 x 10^13.

**The verdict.** A power law in the weights held at laptop scale: five
widths over 1.2 decades on one line within 1%, and a model four times
larger than any of them predicted, before it was trained, inside its
interval. Counted with the embeddings it missed, and figure 6's reason
held by the miss. The exponent says how fast loss fell with size at this
budget of tokens, not at convergence; the paper's precise exponent is not
checked here, and cannot be on a laptop. What is checked is the use the
law is put to: a line through small models told what a larger one would
score.

## How it works

### A straight line through logarithms

```
    u, v = [math.log(x) for x in xs], [math.log(y) for y in ys]
    mu, mv = mean(u), mean(v)
    sxx = sum((x - mu) ** 2 for x in u)
    slope = sum((x - mu) * (y - mv) for x, y in zip(u, v)) / sxx
```

Least squares puts the line through the centre of the points, (mean ln x,
mean ln y), with the slope that makes the squared vertical distances
smallest: the products of the points' distances from the centre, summed,
over the squares of their horizontal distances, sxx. The residuals, the
points' distances from the line, have n - 2 degrees of freedom, two
numbers having been fitted, and their sd s is the scatter the page
prints. The slope's standard error is s / sqrt(sxx): the more spread out
the points along x, the better pinned the slope. Every seed is a point of
its own, 15 here, so the scatter includes the seeds'.

### Wider further out

```
    spread = (1 / k if k else 0.0) + 1 / fit["n"] + (math.log(x) - fit["mean_log_x"]) ** 2 / fit["sxx"]
    half = t_quantile((1 + level) / 2, fit["n"] - 2) * fit["s"] * math.sqrt(spread)
```

Three things make a prediction uncertain, each a term under the root:
the mean of k new runs moves by s^2 / k; the line's height at the centre
is known only to s^2 / n; and its slope is known only to s^2 / sxx, which
matters more the further x is from the points' centre, by the square of
the distance. Width 256 is 1.4 in ln N past the largest fitted width,
far from the centre, so that last term is the biggest. t at 13 degrees of
freedom (13.02) makes it a 95% range on the log scale, and the ends are
turned back into bits. From the recorded summaries alone, width 256
left out, in the project's folder:

### The embeddings, in or out

```
    return sum(p.numel() for name, p in model.named_parameters() if name not in ("tokens.weight", "positions.weight"))
```

The GPT names its two embedding tables `tokens` and `positions` (08.02,
08.03), so leaving them out is a test on the name. Everything else is
counted: attention's four Linear layers and the MLP's two, their biases,
and the layer norms. The paper's 12 L W^2 leaves out the biases and
norms too, as "sub-leading terms"; the run prints both, 50,880 against
49,152 at width 32 (the biases and norms 3.4% of N) and 3,159,552
against 3,145,728 at 256 (0.4%). A constant factor in N would only move N_c; a share that shrinks
with the width bends the line a little, and these are small enough not
to.

### One seed, the same batches at every width

```
            run = lab.Run(cfg, seed, label=f"width{w}")
            torch.manual_seed(seed)
```

Each width's run of a seed has its own `lab.Run`, whose generator gives
the batch order, made from the seed alone: every width of seed 0 reads
the same windows in the same order. So the widths differ only by width
and by their starting weights, and a hard stretch of stories early in
training falls on every width of that seed alike, as 13.02's pairs
shared their batches. The starts cannot be shared: the shapes differ.

## Decide the boundary

This lesson trained every width for the same number of tokens. Kaplan
et al. train each to convergence, which a laptop cannot; a third choice
gives every width the same compute, so smaller widths train longer, and
asks which size is best for a budget, a question the paper also takes
up. Equal tokens is the cheapest to run and says how
loss falls with size at that budget; it does not say what each size
could reach. When you predict a larger model, which of the three are you
predicting, and which will the person reading your prediction assume?
