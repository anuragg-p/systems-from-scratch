# 13.01 A hypothesis, and a baseline tuned as hard as your method

03.03 ran the Adam paper's race on MNIST and found a tie at laptop size.
A paper in a hurry gets a clear win out of the same two optimisers: it
searches for its own method's learning rate and runs the baseline at a
rate nobody searched for. This lesson writes down a hypothesis, and the
rule that will decide it, before any run, then runs 03.03's two
optimisers both ways. The hurried comparison says Adam is better by 8.37
points of test error, t = +21.1. Given the same search as Adam, SGD is
0.81 points behind, t = +2.7: a tie, by the rule written first, so
03.03's reading stands. And a search twice as large, still run on one
seed, picks a rate for SGD that is unstable on fresh seeds.

## Where this fits

![Where 13.01 fits](figures/13.01-map.svg)

This lesson's piece is the first of module 13, in the research row:
experiments that hold up, the habits and tools that let a result survive
someone trying to break it. Every lesson of the module takes a result the
track has already reported and tries to break it, on models the track
already has: here 03.03's network and its two optimisers, trained by
06.03's PyTorch loop on MNIST. It stands on 00.03's spread and Welch's t,
and on 05.01's rule that choices are made on validation and the test set
is only reported. The lab gains a way to work out what a search of n
trials bought. After this lesson a comparison comes with its hypothesis
and its rule written down first, and gives every arm the same search.

## The idea

### The result, as 03.03 reported it

03.03 put Adam against SGD with Nesterov momentum 0.9 on a network of two
hidden layers of 256 (784-256-256-10), pixels on the paper's scale of 0 to
1, each optimiser at the best of a grid of three rates, three passes over
MNIST's 60,000 training images, three seeds. On the network no gap in the
training cost was beyond the noise, either way: t = +1.3, +0.6 and -1.3
after the three passes. The page's verdict was that "Adam shows better
convergence" did not show at this size.

### How the same two optimisers make a clear win

The tie is easy to turn into a win without changing a line of either
optimiser. Search Adam's learning rate, and run SGD at the rate PyTorch's
documentation shows it with, `optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=0.01,
momentum=0.9)`: a sensible-looking rate that nobody chose for this
network. The comparison then measures the search as much as the
optimisers. Henderson et al. (2018), on reinforcement learning, put it
plainly: "poor hyperparameter selection can be detrimental to a fair
comparison against baseline algorithms." The Deep Learning Tuning
Playbook gives the rule: "For a fair comparison, the baseline should also
have its learning rate equally well tuned." It adds why one rate cannot
serve both: the learning rate "is a different hyperparameter for
optimizer="Nesterov_momentum" versus optimizer="Adam"", since the same
number means a different step in each.

The **baseline** is the method a new one is compared with; the **arms**
of a comparison are the methods, each run its own way. Here Adam is the
method and SGD with Nesterov momentum the baseline.

### A hypothesis and a rule, before any run

A **hypothesis** is one sentence a run can prove false, and the **rule**
is how the runs will decide it, fixed before any number is seen. The
experiment holds both as constants and prints them before it trains
anything:

<!-- verify: skip reason="the experiment's hypothesis and rule as prose, quoted from its printout below" -->
```
the hypothesis: Adam reaches a lower test error than SGD with
  Nesterov momentum 0.9 on this network after the same passes,
  when each optimiser's learning rate is found by the same search.

the rule: held if Adam's test error over the fresh seeds is below
  SGD's with |t| of 3 or more (Welch's t, 00.03); the reverse if
  SGD's is below by as much; a tie otherwise.
```

A rule written after the numbers can be bent to fit them; 14.04's Break
it shows what that does to a report. The line at 3 is 10.05's.

### The same search for both

A **search** is a set of **trials**, one run at each of several
settings, here learning rates, of which the best is kept. Each optimiser
gets 8 trials. Their rates are drawn **log-uniform** over a range: a
number u drawn evenly between 0 and 1, and the rate low (high / low)^u,
so that every factor of 10 in the range gets the same share of the
trials. Adam's range is 1e-4 to 1e-2 and SGD's 3e-3 to 3e-1, two decades
each, around where 03.03's grids found each one's best. The 8 u's are
drawn once and put into both ranges, so the k-th trial of each search
sits at the same place in its own range: the same search, put where each
optimiser's rates live.

![draw_rates: 8 numbers u drawn once between 0 and 1, and each put into both ranges as low (high / low)^u, so the k-th trial of each search sits at the same place in its own range](figures/13.01-draws.svg)

Every trial trains 03.03's network for 2 passes over the first 10,000
training images, on seed 0, and is scored on the **validation** images,
the last 10,000 of MNIST's 60,000 training images, which nothing trains
on (05.01's validation set). The trial with the lowest validation error
is chosen. Then each chosen rate is run again on **fresh seeds**, 1 to 5,
which the search never saw, and scored on MNIST's 10,000 test images:
that is the number reported. The chosen trial's own score is not, because
it was chosen for being the best on seed 0, and part of being the best is
luck.

### What a larger or smaller search would have bought

How many trials are enough? The 8 trials a search made say what a
smaller search would have found: draw n of the 8 at random, keep the best
by validation, and average over every way of drawing n. With n = 1 the
search is a rate picked at random from a sensible range; with n = 8 it is
the search that was run. If the expected best is still falling steeply at
8, the search was too small to call either arm tuned.

### What the run does

1. The hypothesis and the rule, printed first.
2. The search: 8 trials an optimiser, each one's validation and test
   error, and the rate chosen on validation.
3. The hurried comparison: Adam at its chosen rate against SGD at the
   documentation's 0.01, both on fresh seeds 1 to 5.
4. The fair comparison: SGD at its own chosen rate, on the same seeds.
5. The expected best of n trials, n = 1 to 8, for each optimiser.

### `best_of`

The expected best of n of the trials, drawn at random with none twice,
counted exactly from the trials' scores; with a second list, the
expected reported number (the test error) of the trial so chosen.

### `draw_rates`

One set of u's, and every optimiser's rates made from it in its own
range.

### `run_one`

One optimiser, one rate, one seed: 03.03's network built from that seed,
trained in the seed's batch order, scored on validation and test.

### `verdict`

The rule, as code: Welch's t of SGD's test errors against Adam's, and
which of the three outcomes it gives.

**The hurried comparison: a clear win.** Adam at its searched rate,

every number is a real run.

**The fair comparison: a tie.** SGD at the rate its own search chose,
0.127, makes 7.06%. Adam is still lower on average, by 0.81 points, but
t = +2.7 falls short of the 3 written down first: by the rule, a tie. Of
the 8.37 points the hurried comparison found, 7.56 were the baseline's
missing search.

**The search, trial by trial.** Adam's validation error falls from 15.5%
at 1.1e-4 to 5.17% at 0.00423 and rises again at 0.0067; SGD's falls
from 30.4% at 0.0032 to 7.64% at 0.127 and rises at 0.2. Neither choice
sits at the edge of its range, so a better rate outside it is not
likely. The documentation's 0.01 lies inside SGD's range, and the trial
nearest it scored 12.42% on validation: a fine rate for some network,
and a poor one for this one after two passes.

![The search, trial by trial: each optimiser's 8 learning rates, drawn log-uniform over its own two decades, every one on seed 0, and its error on the validation images; the one chosen in the accent](figures/13.01-search.svg)

**What the search bought.** With one trial, a rate picked at random in
its range, SGD would expect to be 6.52 points behind Adam on validation
(15.02% against 8.50%); with all 8, 2.47 behind (7.64% against 5.17%),
and 2.88 on test for the trials chosen. Most of what 8 trials buy comes
in the first 3 or 4, for both: at n = 4 SGD's expected best is 8.16%,
0.52 from the 7.64% of all 8, and Adam's 5.40%, 0.23 from its 5.17%. The
curves have flattened, so 8 trials were enough to call both arms tuned,
at this budget.

![What a search of n trials buys, worked out from the 8 each search made: the expected best validation error of n trials drawn at random (solid), and the test error of the trial so chosen (dashed)](figures/13.01-bestof.svg)

**One seed is not the setting's score.** The chosen trials' own test
errors, on seed 0, were 5.66% for Adam and 8.54% for SGD. On fresh seeds
the same rates make 6.25% and 7.06%: Adam's chosen trial was luckier than
its rate, SGD's less lucky. Reported, the search's own trials would have
widened the gap to 2.88 points. Neither number is the setting's; the
fresh seeds' mean is.

The 8 new trials hold a rate for SGD, 0.222, that beat 0.127 on seed 0's
validation images by 0.07 points. On the five fresh seeds it is worse:
8.84% against 7.06%, and far less steady, one seed at 13.5% where the
worst at 0.127 was 7.66%. At 0.222 SGD is near the rate at which it
stops training steadily, and seed 0 happened to be a seed on which it
did. Adam's choice moved too, from 0.00423 to 0.00519, better on seed
0's validation by 0.32 points and worse on fresh seeds by 0.33 (6.58%
against 6.25%). The verdict is still a tie, at a gap of 2.27 points.

![A search twice as large, still on one seed: SGD's 8 more trials (accent) hold a rate a little better on seed 0's validation images, and on 5 fresh seeds it is worse and far less steady](figures/13.01-wider.svg)

A search on one seed rewards the rate that was luckiest on that seed,
and the more trials it runs, the more chances one has to be lucky. The
remedy is to run the best few trials again on several seeds before
choosing, as the playbook advises ("before adopting a candidate change,
consider running the best trial N times to characterize the run-to-run
trial variance"); this lesson does not build it, so it has no number to
show for it. The 8-trial search is the experiment's default.

**The verdict.** 03.03's tie holds. Run fairly, with the same search for
both and the result read on fresh seeds, Adam is 0.81 points lower on
average and not by 3 times the noise in the gap: the hypothesis is not supported, and
not refuted either. This is not 03.03's measurement: 03.03 compared
training cost after 3 passes over 60,000 images, this compares test error
after 2 passes over 10,000, so that 31 runs fit in a minute. Its reading
is unchanged; the hurried comparison would have reversed it. Henderson et
al.'s call to give baselines "standard hyperparameter searches", and the
playbook's equally tuned baseline, held here by 7.56 points.

## How it works

### Every draw, counted at once

```
    return sum(reported[j] * math.comb(N - 1 - i, n - 1) for i, j in enumerate(order)) / math.comb(N, n)
```

Put the N trials in order of their scores, best first, and number them
from 0. A draw of n of them, none twice, is one of comb(N, n) equally
likely draws. The i-th trial is the best of a draw exactly when it is in
the draw and the other n - 1 all come from the N - 1 - i trials after it:
comb(N - 1 - i, n - 1) draws. So the expected best is each trial's score
times its count of draws, summed and divided by the number of draws. With
n = 1 every count is 1 and the result is the plain mean; with n = N only
the best trial has a draw, and the result is the best score. For N = 8
and n = 3 the counts run 21, 15, 10, 6, 3, 1, 0, 0, which add up to the
56 draws. Counted by hand on Adam's trials from the Run it, in the
project's folder:

<!-- verify: session needs="torch" -->
```
$ cd build/at/research/13.01
$ python3 -c "
import itertools, json, lab
val = [100 * json.load(open(f'runs/baseline/search-adam-{k}/seed0/summary.json'))['val_error'] for k in range(8)]
draws = list(itertools.combinations(val, 3))
print(len(draws), round(sum(min(d) for d in draws) / len(draws), 4), round(lab.best_of(val, 3), 4))
"
56 5.5748 5.5748
$ cd ../../../..
```

Every one of the 56 draws, its best taken and the 56 averaged, against
the formula: the same 5.5748, the table's 5.57 at n = 3. The order is by
validation, and with a second list the same counts weigh the test errors
instead: the test error of the trial a search of n would have chosen,
not the best test error, which nobody could have chosen.

### The same u's, two ranges

```
    u = rng.random(trials)
    return {name: [float(low * (high / low) ** x) for x in u] for name, (low, high) in ranges.items()}
```

The u's are drawn once, outside the loop over the optimisers. Drawn
inside it, each optimiser would get its own 8 places in its range, and
one could get a lucky spread and the other an unlucky one: the
comparison would carry the luck of the draws as well as of the seeds.
The ranges themselves are each optimiser's own. Both span two decades,
the same width on a log scale, so the search is as fine for one as for
the other; where each range sits comes from what 03.03 knew of each
optimiser. Plain random draws, rather than the playbook's quasi-random
ones (it allows a grid in one or two dimensions), keep any n of the 8 a
fair sample of the search, which is what `best_of()` needs.

### Fresh seeds for the report

```
    fresh = range(1, cfg.seeds + 1)
```

Every trial runs on seed 0, the reported runs on seeds 1 to 5. A trial
chosen as the best of 8 on seed 0 is the best partly by the luck of seed
0's start and batch order, and its own score keeps that luck; seeds it
was not chosen on do not. The validation images take the choice and the
fresh seeds take the luck of the seed, and the test images are read
once, for the report, as 05.01 asked.

### The line at 3

```
    t = lab.welch_t(sgd, adam)
    return t, ("held: Adam lower" if t >= 3 else "the reverse: SGD lower" if t <= -3 else "a tie")
```

Welch's t (00.03) is the gap between the two arms' means over the noise
in that gap. With five seeds a side, a t of 3 or more by chance alone is
rare; 13.02 shows how to say how rare, and what an interval adds. The rule has three outcomes, not two: a tie
is a result, and here it is the one 03.03 found.

## Decide the boundary

This lesson gave each arm the same number of trials, each on one seed,
each range two decades wide. Another rule would give each arm the same
compute, or tune the baseline until it matches the score its own paper
reported (Henderson et al.: "find the working set which at least matches
the original reported performance of baseline algorithms"), or search
the baseline's momentum too, not only its rate. Each costs runs, and a
lab with a new method has every reason to spend them on the method. How
much search does a baseline deserve, and how would a reader of your
paper know it got it?

## Break it

Give both optimisers the same rates: "the same search" read as the same
numbers, not the same place in each one's range. In
`build/at/research/13.01/experiments/baseline.py`, in `CONFIG`, change
SGD's range `nesterov=[3e-3, 3e-1]` to Adam's, `nesterov=[1e-4, 1e-2]`:

## Go deeper

- [Deep Learning Tuning Playbook](https://github.com/google-research/tuning_playbook) by Godbole, Dahl, Gilmer, Shallue and Nado - "Identifying scientific, nuisance, and fixed hyperparameters" for the rule this lesson follows: the optimiser is the scientific hyperparameter, each one's learning rate a nuisance hyperparameter to be optimised before they are compared; "Why use quasi-random search instead of more sophisticated black box optimization algorithms during the exploration phase of tuning?" for the search it prefers to plain random draws; and its advice to run the best trial several times before adopting a change.
- [Deep Reinforcement Learning that Matters](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.06560) by Henderson et al., AAAI 2018 - the section on hyperparameters, where a baseline's poor settings decide a comparison, and the conclusion's call to find settings for baselines that at least match their published results "through standard hyperparameter searches".
- [An Opinionated Guide to ML Research](http://joschu.net/blog/opinionated-guide-ml-research.html) by John Schulman - "Aim High, and Climb Incrementally Towards High Goals": "A method that slightly improves on the baseline better be very simple, otherwise no one will bother using it"; a gain that disappears when the baseline is tuned was never there to weigh.
