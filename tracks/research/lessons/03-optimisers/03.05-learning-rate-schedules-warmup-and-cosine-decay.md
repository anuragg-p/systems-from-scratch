# 03.05 Learning-rate schedules: warmup and cosine decay

A constant learning rate is a compromise between the start of training,
where big steps make fast progress, and the end, where only small steps
can settle into the minimum the big ones keep jumping across. A schedule
stops compromising: it changes the rate as training goes. This lesson
writes PyTorch's `LambdaLR`, which sets the rate from any function of the
step, and the function most training runs use today: a short warmup, then
a cosine decay to 0.

## Where this fits

![Where 03.05 fits](figures/03.05-map.svg)

This lesson closes the module's strip, in the engine's optimisers. A
schedule sits beside the optimiser, not inside it: between two steps it
writes a new `lr` into the optimiser's param_groups, which is why 03.01 kept
the rate there. Any optimiser takes one, Adam here. The training loop of
03.01 gains one call after each step. After this lesson the engine has
`torch.optim.lr_scheduler.LambdaLR`, and the module's optimisers are
complete.

## The idea

![Top: the learning rate at each of the 938 steps of the five schedules: two constant, a cosine from 0.003 to 0, a cosine from 0.01, and the same with 200 steps of warmup first. Bottom: the test error of each after two passes, three seeds, and the worst batch loss of its first 300 steps; the cosine from 0.003 ends lowest](figures/03.05-schedules.svg)

Train 03.03's network (two hidden layers of 256) with Adam for two passes
over MNIST. At Adam's default rate, 0.001, it ends with a training cost of
0.0659 and gets 2.77% of the test digits wrong. A bigger constant rate is
not better: at 0.003 the cost is 0.0683 and the error 2.98%. With a
constant rate, the rate that makes fast progress early is the same one the
last steps have to settle with.

A **schedule** gives each step its own rate. **Cosine decay** starts at a
peak and follows half a cosine wave down to 0 at the last step: the rate
falls slowly at first, fastest in the middle, and slowly again near the
end. From a peak of 0.003, the rate the constant run could not settle at,
it ends at 0.0382 and 2.02%: lower than either constant rate, early steps
as big as the bigger one and late steps smaller than the smaller one.

A higher peak brings a new failure. From 0.01 the batch loss jumps above
where it started within the first steps, to 3.459 against 2.298, the loss
of the untrained network; it recovers, but ends worse than the cosine from
0.003. As 03.03 showed, Adam's first step moves every weight by about the
rate, so at 0.01 every one of the first layer's 200,704 weights moves by
0.01 at once. A **warmup** raises the rate from near 0 to the peak over the
first steps, here 200 of them, while Adam's averages are still made of a
handful of gradients. With it the spike is gone: the worst loss of the first
300 steps is 2.298, where the loss began.

### `warmup_cosine`

The function of the step that LambdaLR multiplies the rate by: (step + 1)
/ warmup during the warmup, then half a cosine wave from 1 down to a floor
(0 by default) over the remaining steps, and the floor after them. It is
plain Python, with no tensors, so it can be checked by printing it.

### `LambdaLR`

PyTorch's scheduler for any such function. It remembers each group's
starting rate, and every `step()` counts one more and sets each group's
`lr` to its starting rate times the function of the count. It takes that
first step itself, when it is made, so the optimiser's very first step
already uses the function's value at 0. `train()` from 03.01 now takes a
`scheduler` and calls its `step()` right after the optimiser's.

### `make_scheduler`

No scheduler for a constant rate; for a cosine, LambdaLR over
`warmup_cosine()` with the run's warmup and its total number of steps, so
the rate the optimiser was made with becomes the peak. The experiment
trains the five ways in the figure, three seeds each, and prints for each
the worst batch loss of the first 300 steps, the training cost at the end
and the test error.

Over three seeds the cosine's worst test error, 2.05%, is below the
constant rate's best, 2.6%. The claim this tests is in Loshchilov and
Hutter's AdamW paper: "Adam can substantially benefit from a scheduled
learning rate multiplier", such as cosine annealing, shown in their
figure 1 on CIFAR-10. Held here, at laptop size: decay to 0 from a peak higher than the best constant rate ends
lower, 0.0382 against 0.0659 in training cost and 2.02% against 2.77% in
test error. The cosine itself is from their earlier SGDR paper, which
decays the rate this way and then restarts it; this run has one decay and
no restart.

The warmup is practice rather than one paper's claim (Goyal and co-authors
gave the gradual form for SGD with large batches). Here it did what it is
for: the worst early loss falls from 3.459 to 2.298, and the run ends
better, 2.34% against 2.6%. It does not make the peak of 0.01 pay, though:
the cosine from 0.003 without any warmup is better than both.

## How it works

### Half a cosine wave, checked

After the warmup, with p the share of the remaining steps done (0 at the
first, 1 at the last), the rate is the peak times `(1 + cos(pi p)) / 2`. At
p = 0 that is (1 + 1) / 2 = 1, at p = 0.5 it is (1 + 0) / 2 = 0.5, and at
p = 1 it is (1 - 1) / 2 = 0. Its slope is 0 at both ends, so the rate
leaves the peak gently and arrives at 0 gently. Two passes of 469 steps are
938; with a warmup of 200, step 0 has 1 / 200 of the peak, step 99 half,
step 199 all of it, and the halfway point of the decay, p = 0.5, falls on
step 200 + 369 = 569. In the project's folder:

### The scheduler writes, the optimiser reads

LambdaLR changes nothing in the optimiser but the number in each group's
`lr`, and the optimiser reads that number afresh on every step. So the
order in the loop is the contract: `opt.step()` with the rate set for this
step, then `scheduler.step()` to set the next one. The first rate has to be
set before the first `opt.step()`, which is why LambdaLR's constructor ends
by calling its own `step()`: from then on, the rate of step k is the
starting rate times the function of k, the **invariant** the class keeps.

### Why the spike comes from the first steps

Move each of a hidden unit's 784 weights by 0.01, and its input can change
by up to 0.01 times the sum of the 784 pixel values' sizes: several units,
in one step, if the moves line up with the pixels. Adam does not hold back on the first step,
because the bias correction makes it exactly the rate (03.03). The warmup's
first step is 1/200 of the peak, 0.00005, and by the time the rate is at
the peak, Adam's averages have 200 gradients in them.

## Decide the boundary

This schedule decays to 0 exactly at the last step, so the run must know
its length before it starts: stop it early and it has never had its small
steps; train it longer and the rate is already 0. A schedule that cuts the
rate when the loss stops falling needs no length, but has to decide what
"stops falling" means in a noisy loss. Which should a run that may be
stopped, or extended, use?

## Go deeper

- [Decoupled Weight Decay Regularization](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05101) by Loshchilov and Hutter - the introduction's "Adam can substantially benefit from a scheduled learning rate multiplier" and section 4.1 with figure 1: Adam and AdamW on CIFAR-10 with a fixed rate, step drops and cosine annealing, where cosine "yields clearly superior results".
- [SGDR: Stochastic Gradient Descent with Warm Restarts](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.03983) by Loshchilov and Hutter, ICLR 2017 - equation (5), the cosine annealing written here, and figure 1, the schedules drawn; the restarts that are the paper's own idea and that this lesson leaves out.
- [Accurate, Large Minibatch SGD: Training ImageNet in 1 Hour](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02677) by Goyal and co-authors - section 2.2, "Warmup": why a constant low rate at the start was not enough and the gradual warmup, a rate raised by the same amount every iteration for the first five epochs.
- [Deep Learning Tuning Playbook](https://github.com/google-research/tuning_playbook) - the FAQs "Which learning rate decay should I use as a default?" (linear or cosine) and "What is the best learning rate decay schedule family?" (an open question, but some schedule that is not constant, tuned, matters).
