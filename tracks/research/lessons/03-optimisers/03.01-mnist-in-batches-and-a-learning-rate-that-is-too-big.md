# 03.01 MNIST in batches, and a learning rate that is too big

The track leaves the points it made from a seed and trains on real data:
MNIST, 70,000 scanned images of handwritten digits. They are too many to take
a gradient over all of them for every step, so the network learns from a
batch of 128 at a time, with the engine's first optimiser, `SGD`. Its one
setting, the learning rate, decides everything here: too small and one pass
over the data gets nowhere, too big and the network is wrecked in three
steps.

## Where this fits

![Where 03.01 fits](figures/03.01-map.svg)

This lesson adds MNIST to the data, beside the points of module 01, and the
experiments' third script, digits, which modules 03 to 05 all train on. It
also brings the first optimiser into the engine: the optimisers box turns
solid here, and the rest of the module fills it. Below it sit the tensors,
layers and cross-entropy of module 02, unchanged; above it, every
experiment from here on. After this lesson the track can train a network on
real images, a batch at a time, and measure it on images it never trained
on.

## The idea

![The batch loss of every step of one pass over MNIST, seed 0, at four learning rates: 0.01 creeps down, 0.1 and 0.5 fall, and 2 jumps to 194 on its fourth step and then sits at the loss of a guess](figures/03.01-rates.svg)

In 01.04 and 02.03 each step of training computed the gradient over every
point there was: 50 points, then 200. MNIST's training set is 60,000
images of 28 by 28 pixels, 47 million numbers; a gradient over all of them
for every single step would make each step a pass over the whole set. So a
step looks at a **batch**: 128 images drawn from the set, whose average
gradient is a noisy but cheap estimate of the whole set's. Taking a step on
each batch in turn is **stochastic gradient descent**, SGD. Shuffle the
60,000 images, cut them into 469 batches (the last one smaller), take a
step on each: that is one pass over the data, an **epoch**.

Every step moves every weight by the **learning rate** times its gradient.
The figure is one pass of the same network at four rates. At 0.01 the loss
creeps down. At 0.1 and 0.5 it falls fast. At 2 it jumps from 2.36 to 194
within four steps, then drops back and stays near 2.30, the loss of a
network that gives every digit the same score: after one pass it gets 21%
of the test digits right, barely better than a guess. Why a step can make
things worse is one line of algebra, worked through under How it works:
on a bowl that curves by h, a step of lr times the gradient lands further
from the bottom than it started as soon as lr times h is more than 2.

The code is the data, the batches, the optimiser and the loop.

### `fetch.py`

Nothing in the track downloads anything on its own. MNIST comes from one
command, `python3 -m data.fetch mnist`, which puts the four original files
into a cache outside the project and keeps each only if its size and its
SHA-256 match the ones written in the file. Every script that needs MNIST
calls `need()` first, which stops it with one line naming that command
when the files are not there.

### `read_idx` and `load`

The files are in IDX, a format of their own: a gzip around a few bytes of
header (the type of the numbers and the size of each dimension, big-endian)
and then the raw bytes, one per pixel or per label. `read_idx()` turns one
file into a numpy array of that shape. `load()` gives a split as rows of
784 floats and a vector of labels, the pixels scaled to 0..1 and then
**standardised**: the training images' own mean subtracted and the result
divided by their standard deviation, so the inputs are centred on 0 with a
spread of 1, the scale the layers' starting weights were chosen for.

### `batches`

The order of one pass: the positions 0 to n-1 shuffled by the run's random
generator, then cut into consecutive slices of the batch size. Each call is
a new order, so each pass is too, and the seed decides them all.

### `Optimizer` and `SGD`

An optimiser holds the model's parameters, in `param_groups` (a list of
dictionaries, each a list of parameters and the settings for them, so a
script can change a group's rate between steps), and a `state` for anything
it remembers from one step to the next. `zero_grad()` clears every
gradient; `step()` moves the parameters. SGD's step is the one line every
training loop so far has written by hand: each parameter that has a
gradient moves by minus lr times it. The names and the defaults are
PyTorch's.

### `train` and `evaluate`

`train()` is the loop every later experiment uses: for each batch of each
pass, the scores, the cross-entropy, `zero_grad()`, `backward()`, `step()`;
the loss of every `every`-th step goes to the run's log, and a loss that is
no longer a finite number ends the run. `evaluate()` scores a whole split
in chunks, without building a graph, and gives the mean loss and the share
of digits it got right. Both take the engine as their first argument, so
the same loop runs on PyTorch when a script imports that instead.

### `one_run`

One rate and one seed: the network 784-100-10 with a relu between, one
pass of SGD with every step logged, then the test set. Besides the loss and
the accuracy it counts the **dead** hidden units: the units whose relu is
shut, 0, for every one of the 10,000 test digits.

## How it works

### Too big, in one line of algebra

Take the simplest bowl, f(w) = h w^2 / 2, whose curve is h (its second
derivative). The gradient is h w, so a step takes w to
`w - lr h w = (1 - lr h) w`. Each step multiplies the distance from the bottom by 1 - lr h. When lr h
is below 1 the factor is between 0 and 1: the weight walks down one side.
Between 1 and 2 it is between -1 and 0: the weight jumps over the bottom
each step, but lands a little closer. At exactly 2 it lands as far out as
it started. Above 2 the factor is below -1, and every step lands further
out on the other side than the last: the run **diverges**. The rate that
works is set by the steepest curve there is: lr below 2 / h.

The engine's SGD on f(w) = 2 w^2 (so h = 4, and the limit is lr = 0.5),
from w = 1, four steps at 0.4 (a factor of -0.6) and at 0.6 (a factor of
-1.4), in the project's folder:

<!-- verify: session -->
```
$ cd build/at/research/03.01
$ python3 -c "
import engine as torch
for lr in (0.4, 0.6):
    w = torch.tensor([1.0], requires_grad=True)
    opt = torch.optim.SGD([w], lr=lr)
    for step in range(4):
        opt.zero_grad()
        (2 * w * w).sum().backward()
        opt.step()
        print(lr, step + 1, round(w.item(), 4))
"

0.4 2 0.36
```

Exactly the powers of -0.6 and of -1.4. A network is not one bowl, but
near any point its loss curves more steeply in some directions than in
others, and the steepest one sets the limit the same way. At lr 2 some
direction is past it.

### Why the network does not recover

A run that diverges usually ends in numbers too big to hold, and
`train()` stops it there. This one does not, and the dead units say why.
The jump of the first steps moves the first layer's weights so far that,
for most hidden units, the input to the relu is below 0 for every digit.
A relu that is off outputs 0 and, as in 01.03, passes back no gradient, so
nothing ever moves that unit's weights again. With 6 to 12 of its 100
units left, the network gets 21% of the digits right and its loss sits near
log(10). The best rate here, 0.5, already kills 11 or 12 units in one pass,
where 0.1 kills none: it is nearer the edge.

### One pass is one sweep

`batches()` draws one shuffled order of all the positions and cuts it into
consecutive slices:

```
    order = rng.permutation(n)
    for start in range(0, n, batch_size):
        yield order[start:start + batch_size]
```

so each pass uses every image exactly once, in an order the seed decides.
That is the **invariant** of an epoch, and the loss of the last 50 steps is
honest only because of it: those batches are fresh images from all over
the set, not ones the network has just been fitted to.

### Clearing the gradients

`backward()` adds into `.grad` (02.02's `_accumulate`), so that a weight
used twice in a graph gets both parts of its gradient. Across steps that
is wrong: the gradient of step 2 would carry step 1's in it. So the loop
calls `zero_grad()` before every `backward()`, and the optimiser's `step()`
changes `p.data` in place, outside any graph, so the update itself is never
part of the next backward.

## Decide the boundary

This experiment takes a step every 128 images. It could take one per image,
469 times more steps a pass, each from a gradient of one example; or one
per pass, from the gradient of all 60,000. A bigger batch gives a truer
gradient but fewer steps for the same work; a smaller one gives more steps,
each noisier. At a fixed number of passes, which of the two buys more?

## Break it

Make every batch the same 128 images. In `data/__init__.py`, in
`batches()`, change the slice so that it always starts at 0. Then run one
rate; the quotes around the setting keep a shell such as zsh from reading
its brackets as a pattern of file names.

batches are the data. Put the slice back.

## Go deeper

- [Why Momentum Really Works](https://distill.pub/2017/momentum/) by Gabriel Goh, Distill 2017 - "First Steps: Gradient Descent" and "Choosing A Step-size": gradient descent on a quadratic taken apart direction by direction, each shrinking by a factor of 1 minus the step times its curvature, stable only while that product is under 2; the playground shows the divergence.
- [Deep Learning Tuning Playbook](https://github.com/google-research/tuning_playbook) by Godbole, Dahl, Gilmer, Shallue and Nado - "Choosing the batch size": why the batch size is chosen for speed, not tuned for the result, and how the learning rate has to be tuned again when it changes.
