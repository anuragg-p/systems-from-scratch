# 03.03 Adam: a step size for each parameter

Momentum still moves every weight by the learning rate times its gradient,
summed over recent steps, so one rate has to suit the steepest direction
there is, and every other direction pays for it. Adam divides each
weight's step by the size of that weight's own recent gradients, so one
rate serves weights whose gradients differ a thousandfold. This lesson
writes it from the paper that introduced it, Kingma and Ba's, and runs the
paper's own comparison on MNIST again.

## Where this fits

![Where 03.03 fits](figures/03.03-map.svg)

This lesson is the third box of the module's strip, in the engine's
optimisers beside SGD. It is a second optimiser with the same two methods,
`zero_grad()` and `step()`, so any training loop takes it in place of SGD
by changing one line. Like momentum it keeps a state for each parameter,
here two running averages instead of one velocity. After this lesson the
engine has `torch.optim.Adam` with PyTorch's settings, and later modules
train with it.

## The idea

![The valley's loss against the step at s = 50 and s = 5000: momentum at 0.03 reaches the floor in 52 steps at s = 50 and diverges at s = 5000; momentum at 0.0003 takes 3742 steps at both; Adam at 0.1 takes 109 and 111](figures/03.03-one-rate.svg)

Take 03.02's valley, (w0^2 + s w1^2) / 2 from (-4, 1), and make its walls
steeper: s = 5000 instead of 50. At the start the gradient is -4 for w0
and 5000 for w1, more than a thousand times bigger. By 03.01's algebra a
rate that works must be small enough for the steep direction: momentum's
0.03, which reached the bottom of the s = 50 valley in 52 steps, diverges
at once in this one, and so does every rate down to 0.001. The rate that
survives, 0.0003, moves w0 by 0.0012 on its first step and then crawls
along the floor for 3742 steps. The steep direction sets the rate, and
the shallow one pays.

**Adam** (Kingma and Ba, 2014) keeps two running averages for each weight:
m, of its gradient, and v, of its gradient squared. sqrt(v) is then the
typical size of that weight's recent gradients, and Adam moves each weight
by `lr * m / (sqrt(v) + eps)`. The gradient's size is in m and in sqrt(v) alike, so it cancels: where the
gradient is steady, m / sqrt(v) is about 1 in size and every weight moves
by about lr, whether its gradients are 4 or 5000. eps, 1e-8, only keeps the
division away from 0. In the figure Adam at 0.1 takes 109 steps at s = 50
and 111 at s = 5000: the same rate, about the same steps, a thousandfold
apart in gradient. The paper says it in section 2.1: the step "is also
invariant to the scale of the gradients": multiply every gradient by c and
m grows by c, v by c^2, and the two cancel.

### Adam's `__init__`

PyTorch's settings and defaults: lr 0.001, betas (0.9, 0.999) for the two
averages, eps 1e-8, and a `weight_decay` that this lesson leaves at 0.

### Adam's `step`

For each parameter with a gradient: count the step, update m with weight
beta1 on its old value and v with beta2, divide each by one minus its beta
to the power of the step count (the bias correction, under How it works),
and move the parameter. The two averages live in `self.state[p]` as
`exp_avg` and `exp_avg_sq`, PyTorch's names, next to the count.

### `weight_decay`

PyTorch's Adam has a `weight_decay` setting that adds weight_decay times
the weight to the gradient before anything else, as an L2 term in the loss
would. It goes through Adam's division like the rest of the gradient;
03.04 shows what that does.

### `make_optimizer`

The experiment has two parts. The first is the valley at s = 50 and
s = 5000, momentum at five rates against Adam at one, then the first step
of each and the valley with its loss multiplied by 1000. The second is the
paper's own comparison, built on `make_optimizer()`: logistic regression
(a single linear layer, 784 pixels to 10 scores) and a network with two
hidden layers of 256, on MNIST with pixels on the paper's scale of 0 to 1,
Adam against SGD with Nesterov momentum 0.9, each at the best rate of a
grid of three, three passes, three seeds, and after each pass the cost on
the whole training set.

Part 1 is the failure and the fix. Momentum's rates either diverge at
s = 5000 or crawl; Adam's one rate takes 109 and 111 steps. On its first
step at s = 5000 momentum moves w1 1250 times as far as w0, in proportion
to their gradients; Adam moves each by 0.1, its rate. And on the same valley
with the loss multiplied by 1000, every gradient a thousand times bigger,
momentum's rate diverges and has to be divided by 1000 to take its old 52
steps, while Adam takes the same 109 steps at the same rate, every weight
within 7.8e-09 of where it was on the loss itself. That is the paper's
claim from section 2.1, and it holds: eps is the whole difference.

Part 2 is the paper's race. Its claims, from section 6 and figures 1 and 2:
for logistic regression on MNIST, "the Adam yields similar convergence as
SGD with momentum"; for a network of two hidden layers of 1000 units, "Adam
shows better convergence than other methods". Here, with each optimiser at
the best rate of its grid:

- Logistic regression: Nesterov is lower after the first pass, clearly
  (0.2968 against 0.3091, t = -11.8); Adam is lower after the second and
  third, within the noise (t = +1.8 and +1.2). About the same, which is
  what the paper's figure 1 shows for this model. That held.
- The network: Adam is lower after passes 1 and 2, Nesterov after pass 3,
  and no gap is beyond the noise (t = +1.3, +0.6, -1.3). "Adam shows better
  convergence" did not show at this size.

This is not the paper's experiment at full size, and not a reproduction of
it. The paper's network has 1000 units a layer and trains with dropout
for 200 passes; its logistic regression has an L2 term and a rate that
shrinks as 1/sqrt(t). Ours is 256 units, no dropout, no L2, a constant
rate and three passes. The claim that held is the one about the step
itself, which part 1 tests directly. Adam is the less steady of the two
here: its final cost on the network spreads over 0.0425 to 0.0595 across
three seeds, Nesterov's over 0.0425 to 0.0463.

## How it works

### Why the size cancels

Multiply every gradient by a number c. m is an average of gradients, so it
becomes c m; v is an average of their squares, so it becomes c^2 v, and
sqrt(c^2 v) = c sqrt(v). The step is
`lr * c m / (c sqrt(v) + eps) = lr * m / (sqrt(v) + eps / c)`: the same
step with eps divided by c. With eps at 1e-8 and gradients
anywhere near 1 in size, eps / c changes the step in about the eighth
decimal place, which is what part 1 measured: 7.8e-09 at most, over 109
steps. Momentum has no such division, so its step is c times bigger, and a
rate tuned for one scale diverges at the other.

### The averages start at zero

m and v start at 0 and each step keeps beta of the old value and adds
(1 - beta) of the new one. Unrolled, after t steps,
`m = (1 - beta1) (g_t + beta1 g_(t-1) + ... + beta1^(t-1) g_1)`. If every
gradient were the same g, that would be
g (1 - beta1) (1 + beta1 + ... + beta1^(t-1)) = g (1 - beta1^t): too small
by a factor 1 - beta1^t, because the weights on the gradients add up to
1 - beta1^t, not 1. The missing part is the weight still on the starting 0.
So the step divides m by 1 - beta1^t and v by 1 - beta2^t: the **bias
correction** (the paper's section 3 derives it for v the same way). Early
on it matters a lot: on the first step 1 - beta1 = 0.1 and
1 - beta2 = 0.001, so the corrected m and v are exactly g and g^2, and
the step is lr * g / |g|: lr itself, in the gradient's direction. That is
part 1's first step: 0.1 for w0, whose gradient is 4, and 0.1 for w1,
whose gradient is 5000.

The averages after ten steps of the same gradient, 3, at lr 0.1, in the
project's folder:

<!-- verify: session -->
```
$ cd build/at/research/03.03
$ python3 -c "
import engine as torch
w = torch.tensor([0.0], requires_grad=True)
opt = torch.optim.Adam([w], lr=0.1)
for t in range(10):
    opt.zero_grad()
    (3 * w).sum().backward()
    opt.step()
s = opt.state[w]
print(s['step'], s['exp_avg'].round(4), s['exp_avg_sq'].round(4), round(w.item(), 4))
"
10 [1.954] [0.0896] -1.0
$ cd ../../../..
```

3 (1 - 0.9^10) = 1.954 and 9 (1 - 0.999^10) = 0.0896: the averages are
the true ones times 1 - beta^t, as derived. Corrected, they are 3 and 9
again, every step was exactly lr, and ten steps moved the weight by
exactly 1.

### What the steps do not know

The step is about lr wherever a weight's gradient is steady, so Adam's
rate is a distance: roughly the most a weight moves in one step, whatever
the curves. The paper puts it as the steps being "approximately bounded by
the stepsize setting". Where a
weight's gradients keep flipping sign, m averages them towards 0 while v
does not, so m / sqrt(v) is small and so is the step: Adam slows down
where the gradient disagrees with itself, as momentum does, and it does so
for each weight separately.

## Decide the boundary

Adam's step ignores how big a weight's gradient is. That is what lets one
rate serve the steep valley; it also means a weight whose gradient is tiny
but steady moves as fast as one whose gradient is huge, and a term added to
the gradient, such as `weight_decay`'s, is resized along with it. When
should the size of a gradient count?

`step()`, change the line that corrects v so that it uses v as it is:

The first step is now 3.16, 31.6 times the rate: v on the first step is
0.001 g^2, and 1 / sqrt(0.001) = 31.6, just as the algebra above says.
The extra size fades as 1 - beta2^t grows towards 1, but slowly: on step
100 the step is still 1 / sqrt(1 - 0.999^100) = 3.24 times too big. In the valley that
does no harm (93 and 103 steps; a bowl forgives a big start). On the
network it does: after one pass the cost is 0.307, against 0.0966 with the
correction in the Run it above, and the Nesterov runs, which never touch
Adam, are unchanged at 0.1060. The paper warns of exactly this in sections
3 and 5: without the correction, a beta2 close to 1 leads to very large
first steps. Put the line back.
