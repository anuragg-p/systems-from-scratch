# 14.04 Ablations, and the report

14.03's run held the figure's shape and not its size. This lesson asks
what the late generalising depends on, one setting at a time, and then
writes the report. Without weight decay no seed generalises in 6000 steps,
as the paper's figure 2 has it on another task; with less data
generalising is steeply slower, as its figure 1 says. And at the paper's
own 50% of the equations, where the GPT as
module 08 built it never memorises first, weights started 10 times larger
bring the delay back, 4 to 8 times. The paper's text does not say how its
weights start: a detail the text leaves out decides the result. The
report then sets each claim of 14.01 beside ours, read from the logs, with
the verdict of the rule fixed before any run: seven held, two held in
direction only, one did not hold.

## Where this fits

After this lesson the track has reproduced a published result end to end:

the ablations and a report anyone can run again from the recorded runs.

## The idea

### One change at a time

An **ablation** changes one setting of a run, or takes one thing out, and
runs again: whatever moves, the change moved it. Here each starts from
14.03's set-up and changes one setting, three seeds each:

1. **Weight decay**, at 40% of the equations: none, 0.3 and 1. The paper,
   section 3.3: "We find that adding weight decay has a very large effect
   on data efficiency", and figure 2's caption: "Weight decay improves
   generalization the most". Nanda et al.: less of it, slower grokking.
2. **The fraction trained on**, with weight decay 1: 35%, 40% and 50%.
   Figure 1's centre: "Training time required to reach 99% validation
   accuracy increases rapidly as the training data fraction decreases."
3. **The start**, at the paper's 50% and weight decay 1: the GPT as it
   starts, and every weight but the layer norms' 10 times larger.

Each run stops at the first measured step, 10 a decade, at which its
held-out accuracy is 99% or more, which is what it was run to find, or
else at 6000 steps. The setting that two sweeps share, 40% with weight
decay 1, runs once: 6 settings, 18 runs, 27 minutes of this laptop's CPU
with other runs going. With 14.03's run, the module's recorded runs took
34 minutes 24 seconds.

### Why the start

A model's **start** is the weights it has before the first step. 08.02
gave the GPT GPT-2's: every weight drawn with a spread of 0.02, biases at
0, layer norms at 1 and 0. The paper's text says only "a standard
decoder-only transformer". The authors' code builds its layers with
PyTorch's own starts, which are far larger: an embedding table drawn with
a spread of 1, a Linear layer's weights drawn evenly between minus and
plus one over the square root of its inputs. A factor of 10 on GPT-2's
start, outside the layer norms, is one way to ask whether that matters.

### `settings`

Every run the three sweeps need, as (fraction, weight decay, start), each
once and in order.

### `sweep`

One row of a table for each value of a sweep: from the runs read back,
those that match the settings held fixed, and for each seed its steps to
99% on both halves and its best held-out accuracy.

### `scaled_start`

The model's start made a factor larger: every weight multiplied, the layer
norms' left alone.

### `read_seeds`

One setting's runs, read back from its folder: each seed's settings and
log.

### `read_runs`

The same for every setting a script ran, by its label.

### `median_steps`

The middle of three seeds' steps, where a seed that never got there counts
as later than any step, and a median that is such a seed is only "more
than" the budget.

### Writing it up

`experiments/report.py` reads 14.03's recorded run and these, and prints
three parts: what we ran against the paper's set-up, setting by setting,
and how our model differs from the authors'; each claim with the paper's
number, ours (the median over seeds, and each seed's) and its verdict by
14.01's `verdict()`; and what the start did. It writes the same text to
`runs/report/report.txt`.

### `norm_ratio`

The norm when the held-out accuracy first reached 99%, over its highest
up to then: below 1 if the weights shrank on the way to generalising.

### `outcomes`

Our number for every claim of 14.01, from the logs: each a median over
seeds, a flag when it is only a lower bound, and the seeds' own values.

### `changes`

Every setting of a run that differs from the paper's set-up, as 14.01
wrote it, one line each with where the paper gives it.

## Run it

about 1500 steps of this model, and no setting in the table generalises in
that: the fastest, 50% with the GPT's own start, needs about 630 steps for
one seed. So the script's default trains nothing. It reads the recorded
runs, which the project has, and prints their table, in under a second;
`train=True` runs them again, about 27 minutes:

**Without weight decay, never: held.** At 40% every seed memorises by step
398 and none reaches 99% on the held-out equations in 6000 steps: their
best is 0.027, 0.071 and 0.077. With weight decay 0.3 every seed does, at
3981, 3981 and 2511; with 1, at 1258, 1000 and 1000. The paper's figure 2
(left), on another task, has minibatch Adam without weight decay
generalising hardly at all at 50% and AdamW with weight decay 1 fully; ours
without weight decay gets 0.0583 on average, and `decay` held. Weight
decay 0.3 against 1, median 3981 against 1000: 3.98 times slower, where
Nanda et al.'s figure 27 has about 2 to 4, and `more_decay` held.

**Less data, much later: held.** With weight decay 1 the held-out median
is 2511 steps at 35%, 1000 at 40% and 794 at 50%. If the steps grow as 1 /
fraction^k, the two ends give k = ln(2511 / 794) / ln(0.5 / 0.35) = 3.23,
where steps in proportion to one over the data would be k = 1. Figure 1's
centre, on S5, gives about 4 from 30% to 50%: the same steepness, on
another task and range. And training passes 99% sooner with less data,
398 steps at 35% against 630 at 50%, as section 3.1.1 says it "generally
trends down as dataset size decreases": `less_data` and `train_sooner`
held.

![Steps to 99% by the fraction of the equations trained on, weight decay 1, 3 seeds each, on a log scale both ways: rings training, dots held out, the line through the held-out medians at 35% and 50%](figures/14.04-fraction.svg)

One seed at 50% found neither: from step 600 on it sat near 0.67 on its
training equations and 0.34 on the held-out ones, 0.80 and 0.27 at 6000.
At the paper's own fraction, one run in three of the paper's set-up
learned half the table and stopped.

**The start brings the delay back.** At 50% the GPT's own start memorises
and generalises together: seeds 0 and 1 pass 99% on both at 630, and at
630 and 794, 1.0 and 1.3 times later. Started 10 times larger, the same
model memorises by step 501, 630, 794 with its held-out accuracy at 0.0125, 0.0122, 0.0118,
at chance, and passes 99% on the held-out equations at 3981, 3981 and
3162: 7.9, 6.3 and 4.0 times later. Its norm starts at 109.4, its layer
norms' 25.3 and the rest ten times 10.6, and falls to 23.5, 27.2 and 33.6
by then, 0.21 to 0.31 of where it began: the fall Nanda et al.'s figure 7
shows, which the small start never needed. A trial outside the project
went one step further, with one seed: the authors' model rebuilt from
their code, PyTorch's start and all, at 50% and without weight decay as
figure 1 was run, passed 99% on its training equations at step 501, was at
0.017 on the held-out ones at step 10^4, and its held-out loss climbed to
16.3, 3.6 times a guess's: figure 1's first 10^4 steps as the paper draws
them.

![At the paper's 50% of the equations, weight decay 1, 3 seeds each: the GPT as it starts (every weight with sd 0.02, as GPT-2's) against every weight but the layer norms' 10 times larger; dashed training, solid held out, a dot at each 99%](figures/14.04-start.svg)

So the late generalising here is not a property of the task and optimiser
alone. With the text's optimiser at the text's fraction it appears or
does not according to how large the weights start, which the text does not
give. A reproduction from the text alone at 50% finds no grokking and is
right to; one that copies the authors' code finds it. That is the
module's finding about reproduction, more than any one claim.

## How it works

### Stopping at the step that answers the question

```
            if step in at and measure(step) >= 0.99 and stop:
                return model
```

14.02's loop gains this line in place of its plain measurement: with
`stop`, a run ends at the first measured step at which its held-out
accuracy is 99% or more. That step is the number the sweep wants, and the
rest of a 6000-step budget would only cost time. A run that never gets
there runs the whole budget, and its log says so.

### Never is later than any step

```
    order = sorted(steps, key=lambda s: math.inf if s is None else s)
    middle = order[len(order) // 2]
```

A seed that never reached 99% has no step: dropping it would make the
median the middle of the seeds that did, and a setting where two of three
never generalise would look fast. Sorting it as infinity keeps it in its
place, last. At 50%, seed 2's never sorts last and the median is 794;
with weight decay 0 all three are never, the median is never too, and the
table says "more than 6000".

### Larger weights, the same layer norms

```
            if "norm" not in name:
                p.mul_(factor)
```

Every weight is multiplied but the layer norms'. A larger start of the
kind the authors' code has leaves them where PyTorch starts every layer
norm, gains at 1 and biases at 0, so they stay. The biases elsewhere start
at 0 and stay there too. So "10 times larger" is 109.4 against 27.4: the
layer norms' 25.3 unchanged, the rest 10.6 times 10.

### A power from two points

```
    k = None if more_l else math.log(v_small / v_large) / math.log(large / small)
```

If steps = C / fraction^k, then steps_small / steps_large = (large /
small)^k, and k is the ratio of the two logarithms. It uses the two ends
of the sweep, not a fit through all three, and it needs the larger
fraction's median to be a real step: a lower bound there would leave k
unknown, and `outcomes()` says so with None.

## Decide the boundary

The start decides whether this GPT groks at the paper's fraction. One
report would say: grokking reproduced, at the paper's 50%, with a start
like the authors' code. Another: with module 08's GPT as it starts,
there is no grokking at 50%, and the delay at 35% is 5 times, not 1000.
Both are true of the same runs. A reproduction
can follow the paper's text or the paper's code, and they disagree. Which
one is the paper's claim?

## Go deeper

- [Grokking: Generalization Beyond Overfitting on Small Algorithmic Datasets](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.02177) by Power et al., 2022 - section 3.3 and figure 2 (left) for the optimisers and regularisers on S5, weight decay among them; section 3.1.1 and figure 1 (centre) for the steps against the fraction.
- [Progress measures for grokking via mechanistic interpretability](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.05217) by Nanda et al., 2023 - section 5.3 for weight decay, and its note that "when networks are provided with enough data, there is no longer a gap between the train and test losses"; appendix D.1 and figures 26 and 27 for no weight decay and other amounts of it.
- [Omnigrok: Grokking Beyond Algorithmic Data](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01117) by Liu, Michaud and Tegmark, 2022 - read here only as far as its abstract, which ties grokking to "the mismatch between training and test losses" and says "we are able to eliminate grokking for algorithmic datasets"; its body is where to look for how a start's size enters.
- [An Opinionated Guide to ML Research](http://joschu.net/blog/opinionated-guide-ml-research.html) by John Schulman - "Keep a Notebook, and Review": a daily record of ideas and results, condensed every week or two, which the recorded runs and this report are a small form of.
