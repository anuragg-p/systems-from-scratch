# 14.01 Reading a paper for what it claims

A reproduction starts before anything runs: with the paper read for what
it claims, and every claim written down as a number a run could
contradict. This lesson reads the grokking paper, Power et al. (2022),
that way. It builds the paper's data, every equation x / y mod 97, and
writes ten claims as predictions: where each is in the paper, the paper's
number, and a rule, fixed now, that will say whether ours held. Two
baselines come with them, measured: a guess is right 1 time in 97, and a
model that only memorises gets every training equation right and fewer of
the rest than a guess does.

## The idea

### A paper, read three times

S. Keshav's "How to Read a Paper" reads in three passes. The first, five
to ten minutes, takes the title, abstract, introduction, headings and
conclusions. The second, up to an hour, looks carefully at the figures:
"Are the axes properly labeled? Are results shown with error bars, so that
conclusions are statistically significant?" The third is "to virtually
re-implement the paper: that is, making the same assumptions as the
authors, re-create the work", which in his words lets you "identify not
only a paper's innovations, but also its hidden failings and assumptions".

This module does the third pass in code. This lesson is the reading it
needs first, with two outputs: every setting the experiment depends on,
each with the place in the paper that gives it, and every claim as a
number with a rule.

### The paper

Alethea Power, Yuri Burda, Harri Edwards, Igor Babuschkin and Vedant
Misra, "Grokking: Generalization Beyond Overfitting on Small Algorithmic
Datasets", arXiv 2201.02177, January 2022. They train a small transformer
on part of a table of equations and score it on the rest. In 05.01's words,
**generalisation** is doing well on what the model never trained on, and
**overfitting** is fitting what is particular to the training data. The
paper reports a third thing, in its list of contributions: "long after
severely overfitting, validation accuracy sometimes suddenly begins to
increase from chance level toward perfect generalization. We call this
phenomenon 'grokking'."

The result the module reproduces is their figure 1 (left), division mod
97 with half the equations to train on. Its caption: "Training accuracy
becomes close to perfect at < 10^3 optimization steps, but it takes close
to 10^6 steps for validation accuracy to reach that level, and we see very
little evidence of any generalization until 10^5 steps." **Chance** is
what guessing gets: 1 in 97.

### The data

A **residue** mod 97 is a remainder after dividing by 97, a number from 0
to 96. The table holds every a from 0 to 96 and every b from 1 to 96, and
for each the c with c x b = a (mod 97): that is a / b mod 97. 97 x 96 =
9312 equations. Each residue is a symbol of its own, as the paper says: "the
network is not made aware of any internal structure of the elements". The
model is never told that 5 comes after 4; all it can learn is how the
symbols combine.

The paper trains on a random part of the table, appendix A.1.1: "For each
training run, we chose a fraction of all available equations at random and
declared them to be the training set, with the rest of equations being the
validation set." The model fills in the rest, "much like solving a Sudoku
puzzle", as section 1 puts it.

![x / y mod 97 as a table, a row for each a and a column for each b: seed 0's split at the paper's 50%, the training equations filled and the held-out ones a question mark; left, the corner a = 0 to 11, b = 1 to 11, with each c](figures/14.01-table.svg)

### `divide`

a / b mod p for a prime p. Fermat's little theorem says b^(p-1) is 1 (mod
p) for any b that is not a multiple of p, so b^(p-2) is the number that b
times is 1, and a / b is a times it. Python's `pow()` with three arguments
computes the power without the huge number. Dividing by 0 is refused.

### `equations`

Every equation of an operation, one row (a, b, c) each, in order of a and
then b; b starts at 1 for division, as the paper's 0 < y < p.

### `tokens`

The rows as the paper writes them, five tokens each: a, the operation, b,
"=", c. The residues are tokens 0 to 96; "=" is token 97 and the
operations "+", "-" and "/" are 98 to 100.

![One equation as the model sees it: five tokens, a symbol each; the model reads the first four and is scored on its guess at the fifth](figures/14.01-tokens.svg)

### `split`

The paper's split: the equations as tokens in a random order drawn from a
numpy generator seeded with the run's seed, the first round(fraction x n)
to train on and the rest held out.

### The set-up, as the text gives it

Appendix A.1.2 gives the model and the optimiser. The model: "a standard
decoder-only transformer ... with causal attention masking, and calculated
loss and accuracy only on the answer part of the equation", "a transformer
with 2 layers, width 128, and 4 attention heads, with a total of about 4 x
10^5 non-embedding parameters". The optimiser: "AdamW optimizer with
learning rate 10^-3, weight decay 1, beta1 = 0.9, beta2 = 0.98, linear
learning rate warmup over the first 10 updates, minibatch size 512 or half
of training dataset size (whichever was smaller) and optimization budget of
10^5 gradient updates". The rate is never lowered: "we chose not to anneal
the learning rate".

Read to its last paragraphs, the same appendix says figure 1 was not run
that way: "For the experiments reported in Section 3.1 we increased the
optimization budget to 10^6, and used Adam optimizer with no weight decay,
for emphasizing how late into the optimization process the generalization
can begin." The text's set-up and the run behind the figure differ. The
module runs the text's: AdamW with weight decay 1. Figure 1's 10^6 steps
would be about ten hours of this laptop's CPU for one seed (14.02 times a
step).

### What the code says that the text does not

The authors published their code, openai/grok. Read beside the text, it
answers three questions the text leaves open:

- **Where the loss is taken.** Every equation is written with an end token
  before and after it, and the loss and accuracy are taken on every token
  after "=": c, and the end token, which is always the same. A model that
  guesses c and is sure of the end token has a loss of (ln 97 + 0) / 2 =
  2.29 nats, which is where the chance line of their figure 4 sits, at
  about 2.3. Our
  runs score c alone, as the text says, so a guess's loss is ln 97 =
  4.575. A **nat** is 07.01's unit for the natural logarithm, bits times
  ln 2.
- **The split.** The equations are shuffled once, by a generator seeded
  with 0, so every run of a fraction trains on the same equations; only
  the starting weights and the batches change with the seed. The text says
  each run chose its own. Ours follows the text, a split for each seed.
  Whether it matters was not measured.
- **The defaults.** Run as its README says, the script trains x + y on 5%
  of the equations without weight decay: its own defaults, not any run of
  the paper's, which set these.

![Where the loss is taken: the paper's text scores the answer only; the authors' code scores every token after =, the end token too, which halves the loss of a guess](figures/14.01-scored.svg)

### The claims, as predictions

`experiments/predictions.py` holds ten claims, eight of the paper's and two
of Nanda et al. (2023), a later paper that explains grokking on a smaller
model. Each has six parts: where it is in the paper, the paper's own
words, the setting the paper ran it in, what we will measure, the paper's
number, and the rule. The rules come in three kinds. "about" holds within
a factor of 3 either way, the width a number read by eye off a log axis
needs. "below" and "above" hold when ours is on the stated side of a
number: a bound, as "below 1000 steps", or, where the number is 1, a
direction alone. One claim is an "about", five ask for a direction and
four for a bound. A rule chosen after a run can be fitted to the run;
chosen now, before any, it cannot.

![The claims as predictions, written down before any run: where each is in the paper, the paper's number, and the rule that will say whether ours held](figures/14.01-claims.svg)

Three things the reading turned up, each now part of a claim:

- **One claim, two numbers.** Section 3.1 says validation accuracy "starts
  increasing beyond chance level only after 1000 times more optimization
  steps than are required for training accuracy to get close to optimal".
  The caption says "very little evidence of any generalization until 10^5
  steps", a hundred times the training's under 10^3. The claim `late`
  takes the caption's other number, 99% against 99%: close to 10^6 against
  under 10^3, about a thousand times.
- **Sizes from other runs.** Figure 4's loss is figure 1's run, with Adam
  and no weight decay; the fractions and weight decay are measured on
  another task, the product of permutations in the group S5; Nanda et al.'s
  model has one layer and no layer norm, and learns x + y mod 113. Where a
  claim's number comes from a set-up so far from ours, its rule asks for
  the direction, and the size is set beside it, not judged.
- **A paper that disagrees with itself.** Nanda et al.'s appendix D.1 says
  "Smaller amounts of weight decay lead to slower grokking, while larger
  amounts of weight decay lead to faster grokking", and in the same
  sentence gives the epochs: "around 3k epochs for models to grok with
  weight decay lambda = 0.3, 5-10k epochs for the models to grok with
  weight decay lambda = 1.0, and 20k epochs for the models to grok with
  weight decay lambda = 3.0". The words say one thing and the numbers the
  opposite. Their figure 27's caption sides with the words, "Less
  aggressive weight decay leads to slower grokking", and so does the claim
  `more_decay`.

### `verdict`

Whether a claim held, by its rule: "held", "did not hold", or "not
settled". A run that ended before it got somewhere gives only a lower
bound, "more than 6000 steps", and a lower bound can settle "above" but
only rule out "below" and "about".

### `memoriser`

What a model that only memorises scores: every training equation looked
up in a table, and every other answered with the commonest answer of the
training set. Any run must beat it on the held-out equations before it can
be said to have learned the operation.

## How it works

### Fermat in one line

```
    return a * pow(b, p - 2, p) % p
```

`pow(b, p - 2, p)` is b to the power 95, reduced mod 97 at every step, so
the number never grows past 97 x 97. In the project's folder:

<!-- verify: session -->
```
$ cd build/at/research/14.01
$ python3 -c "
from data.modular import divide
print(divide(20, 15, 97), pow(15, 95, 97), 15 * pow(15, 95, 97) % 97)
"
66 13 1
$ cd ../../../..
```

13 is 15's inverse: 15 x 13 = 195 = 2 x 97 + 1. So 20 / 15 is 20 x 13 =
260 = 2 x 97 + 66, which is 66, and 66 x 15 = 990 = 10 x 97 + 20 checks it.

### Each equation in exactly one half

```
    order = np.random.default_rng(seed).permutation(len(rows))
    k = round(fraction * len(rows))
    return rows[order[:k]], rows[order[k:]]
```

A permutation holds every row once, so its first k and the rest share no
equation and between them hold all 9312. That is the invariant every later
number stands on: an equation held out was never trained on. The seed
fixes the order, so the same seed gives the same split, and seeds 0, 1
and 2 give three different ones, as the paper's "for each training run"
says.

### A lower bound can settle a direction

```
    if more:
        return "did not hold" if ours > 3 * x else "not settled"
```

The ablations of 14.04 stop at 6000 steps, and some runs never reach 99%
in them: all we know is "more than 6000". For an "about" rule, a lower
bound already past three times the paper's number rules the claim out; one
below it could still land anywhere, so it settles nothing. The other kinds
work the same way: more than x holds an "above x", and can only rule out a
"below".

## Decide the boundary

The rules are fixed before any run, and wide: "about" allows a factor of
3 either way, and five of the ten claims ask for a direction only. Tighter
rules would turn errors in reading a figure by eye into failed claims;
looser ones could not fail at all. And the paper's own numbers come from
several set-ups, none of them quite ours. How close must a laptop's number
be to a paper's before "held" means something?

result. A model trained on such a split would show held-out accuracy
climbing with training accuracy and look as if it generalised as it
memorised. The memoriser is the check: a held-out score near the training
fraction, from a model that cannot generalise, is a leak. Put
`rows[order[k:]]` back.

## Go deeper

- [How to Read a Paper](https://web.stanford.edu/class/ee384m/Handouts/HowtoReadPaper.pdf) by S. Keshav - three pages: section 2.1 for the first pass and its "five Cs", 2.2 for what to ask of a paper's figures, and 2.3 for the third pass, re-implementing the paper "making the same assumptions as the authors", which is this module.
