# 12.01 A new head on a pretrained body

GPT-2 (09.03) guesses the next token of a text. A bank wants something
else from text: each question a customer types sorted into one of 77
intents, "card_arrival", "pending_top_up". Radford et al. (2018) showed
the way that most applied work still takes: keep a model pretrained on a
large amount of text, put a new output layer on the vector of its last
token, and train on the task's labelled examples. This lesson builds that
on GPT-2 and Banking77, a set of 13,083 labelled bank questions, beside
the models it must beat. The obvious version fails first: a new layer on
GPT-2's vectors as they come learns almost nothing, 0.187 of the test
questions right, until each of the 768 numbers is standardised (0.773).
Then a second surprise, which the paper does not test: GPT-2's body kept
frozen, with only the new layer trained, is behind a bag of words tuned as
carefully. Fine-tuning every weight, which a laptop does in the module's
recorded run (12.02), is what wins: 0.900 against 0.793 for the same kind
of model trained from nothing. The paper's claim held.

## The idea

### A task GPT-2 was never trained for

![Banking77: six of its test questions and their intents; how its two files are cut into the parts this module uses; and the 77 intents' sizes in the part trained on](figures/12.01-data.svg)

Banking77 (Casanueva, Temcinas, Gerz, Henderson and Vulic, 2020) is
13,083 questions customers asked an online bank, each labelled with one
of 77 **intents**, what the customer wants: "How do I locate my card?" is
`card_arrival`, "I payed with a card and was charged an extra fee"
`card_payment_fee_charged`. Some intents are close to each other on
purpose: a fee for a card payment, for a transfer, for a cash
withdrawal. It comes as two files, `train.csv` (10,003 questions) and
`test.csv` (3,080, exactly 40 of each intent).

It has no validation part, so one is cut out of `train.csv` once, by a
seed: 1,000 questions to choose settings by (05.01's validation set),
8,997 to train on. A question asked twice is one question: 6 of the
training questions are the same as a validation or test question once
both are in lower case with single spaces, and are left out, so nothing
scored was trained on. `test.csv` stays whole, so its numbers can sit
beside other people's.

The score is **accuracy**: the share of test questions whose highest
score is their intent. Always guessing the largest intent scores 1.30%
(40 of 3,080): 77 nearly equal classes leave a lot of room above chance.

### The download

`data/fetch.py` gains Banking77, from PolyAI's task-specific-datasets
repository on GitHub at one fixed commit: three files, 1,081,070 bytes in
all, each kept only if its size and SHA-256 are the pinned ones. Its
licence is Creative Commons Attribution 4.0, as the repository's LICENSE
file gives it, and its README asks that work using it cite Casanueva et
al.'s paper, "Efficient Intent Detection with Dual Sentence Encoders"
(2020). Nothing of it is copied into the project. A second:
`python3 -m data.fetch banking77`.

### Two baselines

A model of 124 million weights has to beat something cheap to be worth
it, and the something has to be tried as hard. Two:

- **The largest class.** 1.30% on test.
- **A bag of words.** Each question becomes a row of counts, one for each
  different word in the training questions, in no order; a Linear layer
  turns the row into 77 scores, and it is trained as any head is. It
  knows which words a question has and nothing about their order or
  meaning. Its learning rate was chosen on the validation questions as
  every model's is: 0.03. In those trials, at 0.001, a rate at which the
  head on GPT-2 does about as well as at its own (0.768 on validation
  after 1,000 steps of 3,000 questions), the bag of words reached only
  0.700: tuned less, the baseline loses, and the comparison with it says
  nothing.

![The baseline: a question as a bag of words, one count for each of the 1,527 words of the training questions, read by a Linear layer; the order of the words is gone](figures/12.01-bag.svg)

### Radford et al.: pretrain, then a new head

Radford, Narasimhan, Salimans and Sutskever, "Improving Language
Understanding by Generative Pre-Training" (2018), the paper before GPT-2,
trained a GPT of 12 blocks on books to guess the next token, then
**fine-tuned** it: trained it further, on each task's labelled examples.
Section 3.2: "The inputs are passed through our pre-trained model to
obtain the final transformer block's activation h_l^m, which is then fed
into an added linear output layer with parameters W_y to predict y". The
pretrained model is the **body**; the added linear layer, new and started
at random, is the **head**.

Their claim, which this lesson runs again: pretraining is what makes the
fine-tuned model good. Their table 5 sets the full model against the
same transformer trained on each task alone, "Transformer w/o
pre-training": on SST-2, a task of sorting film reviews into positive and
negative, 91.3 against 84.0, and section 5 sums it up: "the lack of
pre-training hurts performance across all the tasks, resulting in a
14.8% decrease compared to our full model".

Two things of theirs are not done here. They added the language model's
own loss to the task's while fine-tuning ("lambda was set to 0.5"); their
same table has it helping some tasks and not others (SST-2 is 92.0
without it), and it is left out. And they ended every input with "randomly
initialized start and end tokens (<s>, <e>)", new to the model: GPT-2
already has a token that ends a text, `<|endoftext|>`, called END here
(id 50256), so every question gets END after it and the head reads END's
vector. In a trial made while this module was built, END's vector gave
the frozen body about 2.5 points more than the question's own last
token's; the mean of every position's vector gave more still, but those
trials were scored on the test questions, so they are a comparison, not
a result.

### Where the head reads

![Where the head reads: a question and END through GPT-2's 12 blocks, frozen; the causal mask lets the vector at END read every token of the question; the head, the one part trained, reads that vector standardised](figures/12.01-head.svg)

08.01's causal mask lets a position look at itself and everything before
it, nothing after. So the vector at a question's first token knows one
token, and only the vector at the last position has read the whole
question: that is the one a head can use. Questions of different lengths
go through in one batch padded at the end, and each question's vector is
read where it ends; the padding after it cannot change it, because no
position looks forward.

### Frozen, and read once

Fine-tuning trains every weight, the body's and the head's. On this
laptop's CPU a training step of GPT-2 small on 16 questions takes 0.3 to
0.7 seconds, more with other work running: 600 steps for each of 3 seeds
is ten minutes or more, and a page's run has a minute. So
this page **freezes** the body: none of its weights is trained. A frozen
body gives the same vector for a question every time, so each question
is read once, 3,770 of them in about ten seconds, and the head trains on
the vectors, about a millisecond a step. Training every weight is 12.02's
recorded run, and its number is quoted below.

The same question asked of models that learn from nothing but the
labelled questions: module 08's GPT, small (2 blocks of 4 heads, width
64), with GPT-2's tokens and a head on END's vector, every weight trained
from a random start. It is the kind of model GPT-2 is, at a size that
trains in seconds.

### What the run does

1. Banking77 at a size for the minute: 3,000 of the 8,997 training
   questions (a fixed random part) and 770 test questions (the first 10
   of each intent). The largest class, and the bag of words.
2. Module 08's GPT trained from nothing on the 3,000 questions.
3. GPT-2's body frozen, each question read once at END; a new head on
   those vectors as they come, then the same head on them standardised.
4. Fewer labelled questions: 100, 300 and 1,000, for the bag of words and
   the frozen body.
5. The body fine-tuned: not in this run, which says where its numbers
   are.

### `classes`

The 77 intents, in the dataset's own order: an intent's number is its
place in that list.

### `same`

The form two questions must share to count as one question: lower case,
each run of spaces one space.

### `split`

The training, validation or test questions and their intents: the test
file whole, the training file in an order drawn once from a fixed seed,
its first 1,000 the validation part, the rest less every question the
same as a scored one.

### `last_states`

The body run as the GPT runs itself, up to its last layer norm, and each
text's vector kept at its last token.

### `Classifier`

A GPT's body and a head: a Linear layer from the body's vector to a score
for each class, reading the vector standardised by two fixed vectors,
`center` and `spread`, that `standardise()` sets from the training
questions before any training.

### `pad`

Rows of token ids of different lengths as one tensor, each row followed
by filler up to the longest, and their lengths.

### `vectors`

The frozen body's vector for every text, read once, a batch of texts of
similar length at a time so that little is padding.

### `accuracy`

The share of texts whose highest score is their label, from the texts or
from vectors already read.

### `fit`

The training loop the module shares: AdamW over whatever needs a
gradient, the head at a rate of its own, warmup then a cosine (03.05),
batches drawn from the run's generator (03.01), a row of the log every
`every` steps.

### `words`

A text's words as the bag of words counts them: runs of letters, digits
and apostrophes, in lower case.

### `bag`

Texts as rows of word counts, one column for each word of a vocabulary.

them at all. Fine-tuning every weight beats the bag of words by 1.8
points.

## How it works

### Reading where each question ends

```
    return x[torch.arange(len(ids), device=ids.device), lengths - 1]
```

`x` is (batch, positions, width) after the last layer norm. Indexing with
two tensors of the batch's size picks one row from each text: text `i`
at position `lengths[i] - 1`, its END. A padded text's vector is the one
it would have alone, exactly: in float64 the two agree to 1e-12.
Reading `x[:, -1]` instead would read the padding of every text but the
longest.

### Fixed numbers, not trained ones

```
        self.register_buffer("center", torch.zeros(width))
        self.register_buffer("spread", torch.ones(width))
```

A **buffer** is a tensor a module keeps and saves with its weights but
never trains: it is not among `parameters()`, so no optimiser sees it.
The standardisation's mean and spread are measured once on the training
questions' vectors and then left alone; were they trained, they would
just be more weights of the head. They are never measured on the test
questions. The head then reads

```
        h = (vectors - self.center) / self.spread
```

### A frozen body is never run again

```
        if torch.is_tensor(inputs):
            scores = model.scores(inputs[torch.from_numpy(at).to(inputs.device)])
```

The same `fit` trains every model of this module. Given token ids it runs
the whole model each step; given vectors already read, it runs only the
head. The frozen body's 124 million weights cost one pass over the
questions instead of one a step.

### Two rates in one optimiser

```
    opt = torch.optim.AdamW(groups + [{"params": head, "lr": cfg.head_lr}], weight_decay=cfg.weight_decay)
```

A head starts at random; a pretrained body starts where it is good. When
both train, the body wants a small rate, or it forgets what pretraining
gave it, and the head a large one, or it learns too slowly. In trials
fine-tuning every weight (1 seed, on this laptop's GPU, the head reading
the vectors as they come), the head at the body's rate, 5e-5, had
reached 3.5% on validation after 100 steps and 83.8% after 600; the head
at 0.001, 89.3% after 600. AdamW takes a list of groups, each with its
own rate, and the warmup and cosine scale both alike.

## Decide the boundary

The frozen body lost to the bag of words here, and fine-tuning beat both.
Fine-tuning costs a training run of the whole model for each task and a
copy of 124 million weights to keep for each; a frozen body is read once
and serves any number of heads. Which is worth it depends on the task:
these questions name their intent in their words, which is what a bag of
words counts. For a task where the words alone do not decide, sarcasm or
a question whose meaning turns on its word order, what would you expect
from the frozen body against the bag of words, and how would you find
out before paying for the fine-tune?

find. Raschka's bonus experiments for his chapter 6 (below) found the
same on their two-class task, the first token's 75% against the last's
95%. Put `lengths - 1` back.
