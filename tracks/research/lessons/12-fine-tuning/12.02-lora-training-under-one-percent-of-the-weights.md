# 12.02 LoRA: training under one percent of the weights

12.01's winner, GPT-2 with every weight fine-tuned, changes all 124
million of its weights for one task, and while it trains AdamW keeps two
more numbers for each of them. Hu et al. (2021) froze every weight and
trained, beside two of the weight matrices of each block, a small change
of low rank instead. This lesson writes that change, LoRA, into 12.01's
classifier and checks the two things that must be exact: the adapted
model starts as GPT-2 to the last bit, and the trained change folds back
into the weights with nothing lost but rounding. Then the costs on this
laptop's CPU, and the module's recorded run, 63 minutes with other work
on the machine: LoRA trains 0.08% to 0.28% of the weights and reaches
0.886 to 0.889 on Banking77's test questions, rank 1 as good as rank 8,
with a third of the memory training adds. Every weight trained reaches
0.900. The paper's "on par or better" did not hold at this budget: LoRA
learns faster at first and is behind from step 450 on, beyond the noise.

## Where this fits

![Where 12.02 fits](figures/12.02-map.svg)

This lesson's piece is the second of module 12, in the models row: a way
to fine-tune that leaves the pretrained weights as they are and trains a
small change beside some of them. It wraps layers of module 08's GPT
without changing its file, and trains with 12.01's head, loop and
labelled questions. Above it, 12.03 uses it to teach GPT-2 to answer
instructions in a minute, and 12.04 asks what each way of fine-tuning
cost the model on text it was not tuned on. After this lesson a model
can be fine-tuned on a laptop for a few megabytes of trained numbers, and
the result is again a plain GPT.

## The idea

### What fine-tuning every weight keeps

To train a weight, AdamW keeps the weight, its gradient, and its two
running averages (03.03, 03.04): four float32 numbers, 16 bytes. 12.01's
fine-tuned classifier trains 124,499,021 weights, 498 MB of them and
1,494 MB more for their gradients and averages, and the fine-tuned model
is a new copy of all 124 million for every task it is tuned for. A
training step computes a gradient for every one of them and updates every
one.

### Hu et al.: a change of low rank

Hu, Shen, Wallis, Allen-Zhu, Li, Wang, Wang and Chen, "LoRA: Low-Rank
Adaptation of Large Language Models" (2021), section 4.1: "For a
pre-trained weight matrix W0 in R^{d x k}, we constrain its update by
representing the latter with a low-rank decomposition W0 + Delta W = W0 +
BA, where B in R^{d x r}, A in R^{r x k}, and the rank r << min(d, k).
During training, W0 is frozen and does not receive gradient updates,
while A and B contain trainable parameters."

B A has the shape of W, 768 by 768 for a query layer of GPT-2, but it is
made from two thin matrices: B of 768 rows of r numbers, A of r rows of
768. Every column of B A is a mix of the same r columns of B, so the
change has **rank** at most r: it can move W's outputs in only r
directions. Their bet is that the change a task needs is close to such a
matrix. At r = 8 it is 2 x 768 x 8 = 12,288 numbers where W has 589,824.

![One weight of GPT-2, W_q of a block (768 x 768), frozen, and LoRA's change beside it: B (768 x r) times A (r x 768), started at 0; what it trains at ranks 1, 4 and 8, for one matrix and for W_q and W_v of all 12 blocks](figures/12.02-adapter.svg)

Three details from the same section. "We use a random Gaussian
initialization for A and zero for B, so Delta W = BA is zero at the
beginning of training": the adapted model starts exactly as the
pretrained one, and A, being random, gives B a gradient that is not 0.
The change is scaled by alpha / r, and "we simply set alpha to the first
r we try and do not tune it", so that a rank chosen later needs no new
learning rate. And from section 5.1, "we only apply LoRA to Wq and Wv in
most experiments for simplicity": the query and value layers of
attention (08.01), 2 in each of GPT-2's 12 blocks, 24 matrices. The
count is their formula, 2 x 24 x 768 x r.

Their claim, from the abstract: "LoRA performs on-par or better than
fine-tuning in model quality on RoBERTa, DeBERTa, GPT-2, and GPT-3,
despite having fewer trainable parameters, a higher training throughput,
and, unlike adapters, no additional inference latency". On GPT-2 medium,
their table 3 has LoRA training 0.35 million weights to fine-tuning's
354.92 million, and scoring 70.4 BLEU against 68.2 on a task of writing
restaurant descriptions (E2E). Their table 6 tries ranks on GPT-3: "a
rank as small as one suffices". On memory, section 4.2: "we reduce that
VRAM usage by up to 2/3 if r << d_model as we do not need to store the
optimizer states for the frozen parameters".

### Merged, it costs nothing at inference

Once trained, (alpha / r) B A is a 768 by 768 matrix like any other, and
W + (alpha / r) B A is one weight again: section 4.1, "we can explicitly
compute and store W = W0 + BA and perform inference as usual". The
merged model is a plain GPT, as fast as GPT-2 and giving the adapter's
outputs, up to the order the additions are done in.

![The merge as arithmetic, on a Linear layer of 4 inputs and 3 outputs with a LoRA of rank 1 and alpha 2: W + (alpha / r) B A is one matrix, and it gives the adapter's outputs; in GPT-2, trained 10 steps at rank 8, the same up to float32 rounding](figures/12.02-merge.svg)

### An hour, not a minute

The question is a race between ways of fine-tuning: the head alone on
the frozen body (12.01's), LoRA at ranks 1, 4 and 8, and every weight,
each for the same 600 steps of 16 questions, about one pass over 12.01's
8,997, from the same 3 seeds. A step of LoRA on this CPU is about a
quarter of a second, a step of every weight more, so the race is 45
minutes or more: the module's long run, made once and recorded. The
page's run does what fits in seconds, and prints the recorded table from
its files.

Its settings, each chosen on the validation questions (a trial a
setting, 1 seed): the whole fine-tune at Radford et al.'s 6.25e-5, among
rates that did equally well (3e-5 0.888, 5e-5 0.886, 1e-4 0.890 on
validation); LoRA's A and B at 0.001 for every rank,
alpha 8 at every rank as the paper says (rank 8 at 0.003 was the same,
at 0.01 it fell to 0.808); every head at 0.003, standardised as in 12.01;
warmup over the first 36 steps, then a cosine. Two departures from the
paper: it gives no spread for A's random start, so A is drawn with a
spread of 1 / sqrt(768) (the authors' own code draws it from a uniform
range instead); and its GPT-2 task, five passes of writing E2E's
descriptions at r = 4 and alpha 32, is not run.

### What the run does

1. The count: what LoRA trains at ranks 1, 4 and 8, beside the paper's
   formula and GPT-2's 124,439,808 weights.
2. Exact: GPT-2 adapted at rank 8 and not trained, against GPT-2; then
   trained 10 steps with a new head, merged, against the adapter.
3. A training step on this CPU, every weight against LoRA, on the same
   batches, and the numbers each keeps to train.
4. The recorded race, read from `experiments/recorded/lora/`.

### `LoRA`

A Linear layer, frozen, and beside it a trained change of rank r: its
output is the layer's plus (alpha / r) times B A applied to the input; B
starts at 0 and A at random. `delta()` is the change as one matrix.

### `adapt`

Every weight of a GPT frozen, then the query and value layers of each
block wrapped in LoRA layers. The classifier's head lies outside the
body, so it stays trainable.

### `merge`

Each LoRA layer replaced by its Linear layer with the change added in:
one weight per layer again, under the GPT's own names.

### `trained`

How many numbers training changes: every parameter that needs a
gradient.

**Under one percent, the head included.** Rank 1 trains 36,864 numbers,
0.030% of GPT-2; rank 8, 294,912, 0.237%; exactly the paper's 2 x 24 x
768 x r. With 12.01's head, which every way of fine-tuning here trains,
0.077% to 0.285%.

**Exact where it must be.** Adapted and not trained, the model's scores
for the next token differ from GPT-2's by 0: B is 0, so the change adds
exactly 0. Trained 10 steps and merged, the class scores differ from the
adapter's by 2.41e-05 and the scores for the next token, which run to a
few hundred, by 0.000237: float32 keeps about seven digits, and the
merged weight adds W and B A before multiplying where the adapter
multiplies first and adds after. In float64, on a small GPT, the merged
model's scores agree with the adapter's to 1e-10.

**A step: a little over half the time.** On the same batches, back to
back, LoRA's step took 225 ms against 392 in this run; over the runs made
for this module, 0.50 to 0.58 of a whole step, and the recorded run's
own part 3, at its start, 178 against 329. The paper's saving is "25%
speedup during training on GPT-3 175B". Here LoRA saves computing the
gradients of 124 million frozen weights and updating them, but not the
rest: the forward pass, and the backward pass through every layer's
activations to reach the first block's adapters, are the same in both,
and for a model this size they are most of the step.

**Memory: a third of what training adds.** The numbers kept to train,
computed: 1,494.0 MB for every weight, 4.2 MB for LoRA at rank 8 with
the head, beside about 500 MB of weights for both. The memory a process
really uses is more than that: the activations each step keeps for its
backward pass, and PyTorch's own scratch memory. Measured in a fresh
process, GPT-2 loaded and then six steps of 16 questions trained, the
process peaked at about 4,200 MiB with every weight trained and about
2,300 with LoRA: training added about 3,000 MiB against about 1,100,
LoRA's 35 to 38% in the five measurements made for this module (the
figure is one of them; they differ by up to about 150 MiB).
The paper's "up to 2/3" saved, held on a CPU.

## How it works

### The change starts at exactly nothing

```
        self.A = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(r, in_features, **like) / math.sqrt(in_features))
        self.B = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(out_features, r, **like))
```

B is all 0, so B A x is 0 for every x and the adapted model's first
forward pass is GPT-2's, bit for bit. A is not 0: B's gradient is the
output's gradient times A x, which would be 0 if A were too, and then
neither would ever move. Both are made with the layer's own type and
device, so an adapter on a float64 model is float64.

### Multiplied thin first

```
        return self.linear(x) + (x @ self.A.T @ self.B.T) * self.scale
```

Read left to right: `x @ self.A.T` takes each 768-number vector down to
r numbers, and `@ self.B.T` brings them back up to 768. The 768 by 768
matrix B A is never made while training: each vector costs 2 x 768 x r
multiplications instead of 768 x 768. `delta()` makes the matrix once,
for the merge.

### Frozen means no gradient

```
    for p in model.parameters():
        p.requires_grad_(False)
```

`adapt()` turns off the gradient of every weight of the body before
wrapping the layers, and the LoRA layers' A and B are new parameters,
which need one. 12.01's `fit` gives AdamW only the parameters that need a
gradient, so it never holds averages for the frozen ones: that is where
the memory goes. The classifier's head is not in the body, so it keeps
its gradient; `trained()` counts what is left.

### Merged back under the GPT's own names

```
                    layer.linear.weight += layer.delta()
                setattr(block.attention, name, layer.linear)
```

The change is added into the frozen layer's own weight, and the LoRA
layer is replaced by that layer, under the same name it had in the GPT.
The merged model's weights have exactly the names GPT-2's did, so module
08's checkpoint code saves and loads it as any GPT.

## Decide the boundary

LoRA here adapts only W_q and W_v, as the paper did for most of its
experiments, and leaves every MLP and the other attention layers frozen.
It also learned faster at first and finished lower. Adapting more of the
layers trains more numbers and may close the gap; training at a higher
rank did not (8 was no better than 1). With a budget of a few hundred
steps LoRA was the better choice here; with a thousand, every weight. If
you had to fine-tune GPT-2 for fifty tasks on this laptop, keeping each
result, which would you pick, and what would you measure first to be
sure?

The adapted model no longer starts as GPT-2: before any training its
scores for the next token differ from GPT-2's by tens, 48.1 in this run.
That model is adapted before the run sets a seed, so its random B, and
this number, change from run to run (75.3 in another); with B at 0 it
did not matter, the difference was 0 whatever A was. Twenty-four random
changes of rank 8, one in every query and value layer, have damaged the
body before the task is seen. The new head starts with a
loss of 6.39 where knowing nothing would give ln 77 = 4.34, and ten
steps leave it at 6.59, where with B at 0 the same steps took it from
4.63 to 3.97: the first steps now go to undoing the damage. The merge is
still exact, to the same rounding: it adds whatever B A is, wherever it
started. Nothing here crashes or reports an error; only part 2's first
line, which compares with GPT-2 itself, says that fine-tuning no longer
starts from the pretrained model. Put the zeros back.
