# 12.03 Instructions: a chat template, and a model that answers

GPT-2 was trained to go on with web pages. Asked "Which is larger, 12 or
57?" it writes what a page might say next, more often another question
than an answer. A model that answers is the same model fine-tuned on
examples of asking and answering, written in a **chat template**: plain
markers that say who speaks and where an answer ends. This lesson makes
its examples by code, so that every answer can be checked character for
character, writes a template, trains on the answers' tokens alone with
12.02's LoRA, and scores exact match before and after on three kinds of
question it never trained on. GPT-2 as it is answers none of 180. After
80 steps, under a minute of training, it answers in the template and
stops: 0.375 of new instances right, 0.342 in a wording it never saw. It
learned some tasks well and some not at all, and the one task it was
never shown, "which is smaller", it answers with the larger number.

## Where this fits

![Where 12.03 fits](figures/12.03-map.svg)

This lesson's piece is the third of module 12, in the models row:

stands on 12.02's LoRA and the module's loop, on 09.03's GPT-2 and 09.02's

and a way to say exactly how often it is right.

## The idea

### What GPT-2 does with a question

To GPT-2 a question is the start of a document. Asked in the template
below, it does what web pages do after a question: another question, a
list, a heading. Nothing in its training said that the next text should
be an answer, or where an answer stops. Both are what fine-tuning on
asked-and-answered examples teaches.

### Tasks made by code

A model's answer to "Write a poem about the sea" cannot be scored
exactly; its answer to "Which is larger, 12 or 57?" can: 57 is right and
anything else is wrong. So every task here is made by code, its answer
computed and short:

- **larger**: the larger of two different numbers from 0 to 99.
- **sort**: three different digits, sorted ("3 6 8").
- **upper**: a word in capital letters.
- **reverse**: a word backwards.
- **count**: how many times a letter is in a word; the letter is one of
  the word's own half the time and any of a to z the other half, so that
  "0" is not almost always right.
- **smaller**: the smaller of two numbers. Never trained on.

The words are Tiny Shakespeare's (07.01) different words of 3 to 6 small
letters, 6,396 of them. Each task has 4 **wordings**, ways of asking it
("Which is larger, a or b?", "Give the larger number: a or b.", ...); the
last of each is never trained on. And the instances are cut once, by a
fixed seed, into four fifths to train on and a fifth held out: 1,279 of
the words, 1,980 of the 9,900 pairs of numbers, 144 of the 720 triples
of digits. So every model can be scored on three kinds of question it
never saw: instances held out, asked in the trained wordings; the same
kind, asked in the held-out wording; and a task never trained, smaller,
whose wordings mirror larger's.

![What is trained on and what is scored: five tasks in three wordings each, on four fifths of the words and of the number tasks' instances; scored on instances never trained on, in the trained wordings and in a fourth, and on a sixth task never trained](figures/12.03-held.svg)

### A template

Each example is the user's turn, then the assistant's, each after a
marker on a line of its own, and the end of the answer marked too:

<!-- verify: skip reason="the template as data/instructions.py writes it, an example, not code" -->
```
<|user|>
Which is larger, 62 or 74?
<|assistant|>
74
<|end|>
```

Models trained for chat today have such markers as special symbols of
their tokenizer: SmolLM2's has `<|im_start|>` and `<|im_end|>` (10.04).
GPT-2's has no such symbol, and adding one would mean a new row of its
embedding, trained from nothing. So the markers here are plain ASCII,
and GPT-2's tokenizer cuts them as any text: `<|user|>` is 5 tokens,
`<|assistant|>` 6, `<|end|>` 5. The example above is 22 tokens of
**prompt**, everything up to the line after the assistant's marker, and
7 of **reply**: the answer, a line break, the end marker.

![One example through the chat template, token by token in GPT-2's tokenizer: tokens of prompt, read and not scored, and tokens of reply, each scored by the model's guess at the position before it](figures/12.03-template.svg)

### The loss on the reply alone

Training on text (08.04) scores the guess for every next token. Here the
prompt is given, not to be learned: the loss is the mean of -ln p of the
reply's tokens only, each guessed from every token before it, prompt
included. The model learns to answer, and to write the end marker after
its answer, so that whatever reads its output knows where to stop.

### Exact match, on the first line

**Exact match** is the share of questions whose answer, as the model
wrote it, is the right answer character for character. The answer is
the first line the model writes after the assistant's marker, without
spaces at its ends. Answers are written greedily (08.05): the likeliest
token, again and again.

One departure from the design, for time: the design wrote each answer on
to the end marker. Here the scoring stops each answer at its first line
break, or after 6 tokens; every right answer of these tasks fits (the
longest, a reversed word of 6 letters, is 4 tokens). GPT-2 here keeps no
cache of keys and values (module 11's), so every token written is a whole
pass over the prompt, and going on to the marker took the run past a
minute. Six answers are written on to the end marker, and the run counts
how many wrote it.

### What the run does

1. The tasks, the words, an example in the template, and what each
   marker costs in GPT-2's tokens.
2. GPT-2 as it is, asked in the template: exact match on 12 held-out
   instances of each trained task in the trained wordings, the same
   number in the held-out wording, and 60 of smaller.
3. GPT-2 tuned with LoRA at rank 8 (12.02's, on W_q and W_v) on 2,000
   examples of the five trained tasks in their trained wordings, 80 steps
   of 8, 2 seeds: the same three scores after, and by task; then six
   answers written on to the end marker.

### `words`

The words the word tasks draw from: every different run of 3 to 6 small
letters in Tiny Shakespeare, sorted.

### `instances`

Instances of a task drawn from its training part or its held-out part,
each the fields its wordings are filled from and its right answer,
computed by the task's own function.

### `wordings`

A task's ways of asking: every one but the last to train on, the last
held out.

### `chat`

A prompt in the template, up to where the answer starts.

### `reply`

What the model is to write after it: the answer, then the end marker on
a line of its own.

### `answer_loss`

A GPT's loss on the replies alone: the next-token loss at the positions
whose next token is part of a reply.

### `tune`

12.01's loop for examples of prompt and reply: batches of examples,
joined and padded, AdamW over whatever needs a gradient, the loss
`answer_loss`.

### `answers`

Greedy answers to a list of prompts, prompts of one length together,
each group stopping once every row holds the stop tokens or has written
enough.

What held, of the design's own claims (no paper is tested here): GPT-2

come with it: a task it was not shown. And a score is only as good as
what is looked at beside it: of the 6 right answers to count on each
seed, 5 are a model saying "1" to nearly everything.

## How it works

### Only the reply's positions count

```
    keep = (at[None, :] >= starts[:, None] - 1) & (at[None, :] < lengths[:, None] - 1)
```

The model's scores at position `i` are its guess for token `i + 1`. The
reply's first token is at `starts`, so its guess is at `starts - 1`; the
example's last token is at `lengths - 1`, so the last guess that counts
is at `lengths - 2`. `keep` is true exactly there for each row, false on
the prompt and on the padding after the example, and the cross-entropy
is taken over the kept positions only. A trial made for this page, with
the first half of `keep` made `at[None, :] >= 0` so that the prompt's
tokens are scored too, the same 80 steps and 2 seeds: 0.333 on held-out
instances and 0.283 in the held-out wording, sort down from 0.42 to 0.21.
Most of the tokens of an example are the prompt, and the steps went to
learning to write questions.

### Prompts of one length together

```
        groups.setdefault(len(p), []).append(i)
```

Module 08's GPT has no mask for padding: a padded position is a token
like any other, and a later position would attend to it. Padding at the
end is harmless when only the last real position is read (12.01), but
writing an answer means reading the last position again and again, and
padding on the left would sit in every prompt's past. So `answers()`
writes for the prompts of each length together, with no padding at all.

### Tokens joined as text would be

Prompt and reply are cut into tokens apart and then joined. That gives
the same tokens as cutting the whole example only if no piece of the
tokenizer would cross the join, and GPT-2's pieces can join a space or
punctuation to what follows. The reply starts after a line break, where
GPT-2's pattern (09.02) always cuts. Checked on every task in every
wording, 40 held-out instances each, the joined tokens are the whole
example's.

### Held out once, for every run

```
    pool = _parts()["word" if kind in ("word", "letter") else kind][part == "held"]
```

The cut into training and held-out instances is made once, from its own
seed, and every task and every run draws from the same two parts: a
held-out word is never in a training example of any task, so a model
cannot have seen the word it is asked to reverse.

## Decide the boundary

The markers here are text: 16 tokens of every example are markers, and
a user who types `<|assistant|>` in a question writes the model's own
turn into it. A tokenizer with special symbols for the turns, as
SmolLM2's has, makes a marker one token that no typed text can produce,
at the cost of new rows of the embedding trained from nothing. Exact
match on tasks made by code says exactly what was learned and nothing
about whether the model can answer a question nobody wrote a checker for.
What would you add to this evaluation to learn something about questions
whose answers cannot be computed, without giving up exactness where it
is possible?

answer's line break, so it never saw what comes next and never learned
that nothing does. The score was taken on the first line and stopped
there, which is exactly the part that still works. Put `{END}` back.
