# 05.02 A Bloom filter: the dictionary in far less memory

A Bloom filter answers "is this word in the dictionary?" from a few bits a
word, without keeping the words at all. spell's dictionary goes from 28 MB
in the trie to 72 KB, an eighth of the word list as text, and the price is
that about one misspelling in a hundred gets through.

## Where this fits

![Where 05.02 fits](figures/05.02-map.svg)

The Bloom filter is the second piece of spell's strip, beside the trie.
spell's check can stand on it instead: it asks the filter about each word
of the text and prints those it rejects. Under it is the hash function of
01.03, FNV-1a, which picks the bits a word sets. The filter holds no
words, so completing a prefix still needs the trie, the table or the list.
After this lesson spell checks a text in a few kilobytes of memory; the
edit distance, still dashed, is the last piece.

## The idea

<!-- verify: fact cmd="DS_STATS=1 bin/spell /dev/null 2>&1 | sed -n 's/.*dict bytes \([0-9]*\),.*/\1/p'" expect=28311552 -->

The trie of 05.01 takes 28311552 bytes to hold 60388 words that are 563527
bytes as text; the list takes 1.6 MB, the hash table 3.7 MB. On a laptop
that hardly matters. It mattered to Unix spell, which had to fit in the 64
kilobytes a PDP-11 program could address, and it matters wherever one
program holds many sets at once: a storage engine keeps one for every file
on its disk.

The check never asks the dictionary for its words, only "is this one of
them?", and the answer is one bit. A **Bloom filter** keeps only what that
answer needs: an array of m bits, all 0 at first, and k places in it for
each word, picked by hashing the word. Adding a word sets its k bits to 1.
Testing a word looks at its k bits: if any is 0, the word was certainly
never added, since adding it would have set them all. If all k are 1, it
probably was; but other words may have set those bits between them, and
then the filter says yes to a word it never saw. That is a **false
positive**, and its rate is what you choose when you size the filter.

Here are four words in a filter of 23 bits, 4 places each, the size spell
picks for four words at a rate of 10%:

![A Bloom filter of 23 bits, run by spell on car cart cat dog: each word sets its 4 bits; testing cat finds its 4 bits set, cot finds bit 20 still 0, and hat, no word of the dictionary, finds all 4 of its bits set by the others](figures/05.02-bits.svg)

"hat" was never added, but car and dog set bit 14, cart and cat bit 3, dog
bit 15 and cart bit 4. The filter cannot tell. What it can promise is the
other answer: a word it rejects is not in the dictionary.

<!-- verify: fact cmd="DS_STATS=1 bin/spell --algo=bloom /dev/null 2>&1 | sed -n 's/.*bits \([0-9]*\), hashes.*/\1/p'" expect=578827 -->

For the real dictionary at the default rate of 1%, the filter is 578827
bits, 72354 bytes, and each word sets 7 of them. spell prints a word of the
text when one of its 7 bits is 0; it never prints a word of the
dictionary, and about 1% of misspellings pass for words.

### `bloom_init`, `place`, `bloom_add` and `bloom_test`

`bloom_init()` sizes the filter from the number of words n and the rate p:
m bits, the first prime from the formula up, and k places. `place()` gives
the i-th of a word's k places from the two halves of one FNV-1a hash
(01.03). `bloom_add()` sets a word's k bits; `bloom_test()` looks at them
in order and stops at the first 0.

spell gains `--algo=bloom`, `--fp=P` for the rate (0.01 unless given) and
`--hashes=K` to force k. The filter's size depends on how many words it
will hold, so `load()` reads the dictionary twice, to count and then to
add. `-p` with a filter is refused: there are no words in it to list.

## How it works

### Why about half the bits are on

Add n words, each setting k of m bits. Each time a bit is set, a given bit
is missed with chance 1 - 1/m, so after all kn of them it is still 0 with
chance (1 - 1/m)^kn, close to e^(-kn/m). A word never added finds all k of
its bits on with chance

    p = (1 - e^(-kn/m))^k

Few places a word tell words apart too weakly; many fill the filter until
every test passes. p is smallest when k = (m/n) ln 2, and then e^(-kn/m)
is 1/2: half the bits are on, and p = (1/2)^k. Turned round, for n words
and a rate p, m = n ln(1/p) / (ln 2)^2: 9.6 bits a word for 1%, 4.8 for
10%, 14.4 for 0.1%, however long the words are. `bloom_init()` computes
that. Measured on `unknown.txt`:

| --fp | m (bits) | k | bytes | the formula's p | got through |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | 289417 | 3 | 36178 | 0.1007 | 8473, 10.2% |
| 0.01 | 578827 | 7 | 72354 | 0.0100 | 772, 0.93% |
| 0.001 | 868249 | 10 | 108532 | 0.0010 | 97, 0.12% |

And with m kept at 578827 and k forced with `--hashes`, the same 83421
words get through 9.99% of the time with k = 1, 3.57% with 2, 1.38% with 4,
0.93% with 7, 1.26% with 10 and 2.44% with 14: the best k is the formula's.

### k places from one hash

Hashing a word k times would cost k hashes. `place()` takes one 64-bit
FNV-1a hash and uses its two halves, h1 and h2:

```
    uint64_t h1 = h & 0xffffffffu, h2 = (h >> 32) % b->m;
    if (h2 == 0)
        h2 = 1;
    return (h1 + (uint64_t)i * h2) % b->m;
```

Kirsch and Mitzenmacher showed that places made this way lose nothing in
the false-positive rate against k independent hashes as n grows, and the
measurements above agree. m is a prime so that the k places are different
bits: i h2 cannot come back to the same place mod a prime before i reaches
m. With m = 64, an h2 of 16 would give a word at most four places, however
large k is.

### Bits are only ever set

Nothing in the filter clears a bit, and that is its **invariant**: once a
word is added, its k bits stay 1, so a test of it always passes. The line
that keeps it is the only write `bloom_add()` makes:

```
        b->bits[at / 8] |= (uint8_t)(1u << (at % 8));
```

It is also why a word cannot be taken out: clearing its bits would clear
bits of other words.

### What it saves here, and what it saves elsewhere

Most words of a real text are real words, so in spell the filter's usual
answer is "probably", and spell takes it. Here the filter saves memory,
not work. Bloom's own paper had the other case in mind: a hyphenation
program that handles 90% of words by rule and needs a dictionary on disk
for the rest. Most words are not in that dictionary; the filter's
"certainly not" answers them from memory, and only a "probably" costs a
disk read. Storage engines work the same way: LevelDB stores a filter in
each of its table files, and a lookup skips the files whose filter rules
the key out. Most keys asked of a file are not in it, so there the filter
saves disk reads.

## Decide the boundary

spell with `--algo=bloom` lets about 1% of misspellings through to hold
its dictionary in 72 KB, and the filter cannot list its words, say which
are near one (05.03 builds a trie for that when it must), or forget one.
Another spell could stay exact and still be small: the sorted word list,
binary-searched, is 563527 bytes, eight times the filter. McIlroy's spell
kept sorted 27-bit hashes of its words, compressed to about 14 bits a
word, and let a wrong word through about once in 4096. Which costs your
users more: a misspelling that slips through one time in a hundred, or
the memory never to let one through?

## Go deeper

- [Space/Time Trade-offs in Hash Coding with Allowable Errors](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/spr05/cos598E/bib/p422-bloom.pdf) - Burton Bloom, CACM 13(7), 1970. "Method 2" is this filter; the sample application is the hyphenation program.
- [Development of a Spelling List](https://www.cs.dartmouth.edu/~doug/spell.pdf) - Doug McIlroy, 1982. "Superimposed codes" derives the same formula, half the bits on, for 25,000 words in 400,000 bits; "Compressing conventional hash" is how spell then fit a 64-kilobyte PDP-11.
- [Less Hashing, Same Performance](https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/~michaelm/postscripts/rsa2008.pdf) - Kirsch and Mitzenmacher, 2008. Why h1 + i h2 is enough; its introduction names counting Bloom filters, which allow deletions.
- [LevelDB, "Filters"](https://github.com/google/leveldb/blob/main/doc/index.md) - a Bloom filter at 10 bits a key "to reduce the number of disk reads substantially"; its [table format](https://github.com/google/leveldb/blob/main/doc/table_format.md): "a filter block is stored in each table".
- Programming Pearls, 2nd edition, Jon Bentley: column 13, Searching. Its section 13.8 keeps what the first edition said of a spelling checker in a 64-kilobyte address space.
- The Algorithm Design Manual, 3rd edition, Steven Skiena: chapter 6, Hashing and Randomized Algorithms, for Bloom filters among hashing's other randomized uses.
