# 05.03 Edit distance, and "did you mean"

`spell -s` prints, beside each unknown word, the dictionary's words nearest
to it. Nearest means fewest letters inserted, deleted or replaced: the
edit distance, found by filling in a table of smaller answers. That is
dynamic programming, the track's first; and the trie of 05.01 is what
makes trying it against every word fast.

## Where this fits

![Where 05.03 fits](figures/05.03-map.svg)

The edit distance is the last piece of spell's strip, and the start of the
rail that runs on to module 06: diff fills the same kind of table, for
lines instead of letters. Above it is spell's `-s`, which asks for the
words near each unknown word. Below it is the trie of 05.01, which the
search walks down, a row of the table at each node. With the Bloom filter
of 05.02, which holds no words, spell reads the dictionary again into a
trie the first time a word needs suggestions. After this lesson spell does
the whole job: it finds the misspellings and says what was meant.

## The idea

spell finds "recieve" and prints it, and stops there. To suggest a word
it needs to know how far each dictionary word is from the unknown one. The
**edit distance** (Levenshtein's) is the fewest single-letter edits that
turn one word into the other, an edit being a letter inserted, deleted or
replaced. "kitten" to "sitting" takes three: k to s, e to i, and a g on
the end.

Fewest is the hard part: there are endless ways to edit one word into
another. The way out is that the best edit of two words ends in one of
three ways: the last letter of the first word dropped, the last letter of
the second added, or the two last letters lined up, free if they are the
same and one replacement if not; and before that end comes the best edit
of the shorter words. So keep the answers for every pair of prefixes in a
table, with a word a across the top and a word b down the side (in spell,
the unknown word and a dictionary word). Cell (i, j) holds the distance
between the first i letters of b and the first j letters of a. Row 0 is
0 1 2 ...: from nothing to j letters is j insertions. Every other cell is
the smallest of three cells already filled, plus 0 or 1: the one above,
the one to its left, and the one above and to the left. The last cell is
the answer:

![The table for kitten and sitting, filled by spell a row at a time from its trace: each cell is the smallest of the cell above plus 1, the cell to its left plus 1, and the cell above and left plus 0 or 1; the last cell, 3, is the distance](figures/05.03-table.svg)

This is **dynamic programming**: one big question answered from a table of
smaller ones, each answered once. Module 06's diff fills the same kind of
table.

The obvious way to suggest is the distance from the unknown word to every
word of the dictionary: 60388 tables for each unknown word. For the book's
615 that is 37138620 tables and 2.8 billion cells, 5.6 seconds on this
machine, an Apple M4 Pro; and a text with typos has more unknown words the
longer it gets.

The trie fixes it, because words that start the same share the first rows
of their tables: row i depends only on b's first i letters, the path down
to a node. So walk the trie carrying one row, each child's row made from
its parent's and the child's letter. A node where a word ends, whose row
ends within the limit (`-e`, 2 by default), is a suggestion. A node whose
row has no cell within the limit is left with everything below it: no row
further down can come back under it (How it works). The walk for "cst"
within one edit, over the four words of 05.01:

![The walk for cst over car cart cat dog within one edit, from spell's trace: each node carries its row; car and do have no cell within 1 and are pruned, so cart and dog are never visited; cat ends with 1 and is the suggestion](figures/05.03-walk.svg)

On the book the walk takes 0.15 seconds and fills 86 times fewer cells.

### `edit_next_row` and `edit_distance`

`lib/edit.c` is the table. `edit_next_row()` fills one row from the row
above and one more letter of b, each cell the cheapest of the three. A row
needs nothing but the row above it, so `edit_distance()` keeps two rows and
swaps them, and the trace prints each row as it is made.

### `near_walk` and `trie_near`

In `lib/trie.c`, `trie_near()` makes the root's row and starts the walk.
`near_walk()` makes each child's row with `edit_next_row()`, reports a
word whose last cell is within the limit, and goes down only where the
smallest cell of the row is.

### `near_scan` and `suggest`

In `tools/spell/spell.c`, `near_scan()` is the slow way, kept runnable as
`--near=scan`: the whole distance to every word of the dictionary, in
whatever holds it. `suggest()` asks the walk (or the scan) for every word
within the limit, keeps those at the smallest distance found, and prints
them in byte order, at most `-n` of them (5 unless given).

## How it works

### Why a table

The rule above, written straight as a recursion with no table, is correct
and hopeless: each call makes three more, and they ask for the same pairs
of prefixes again and again. A few lines of Python count the calls:

<!-- verify: session -->
```
$ cat > naive.py <<'EOF'
import sys
calls = 0
def d(a, b):
    global calls
    calls += 1
    if not a or not b:
        return len(a) + len(b)
    return min(d(a[:-1], b) + 1, d(a, b[:-1]) + 1, d(a[:-1], b[:-1]) + (a[-1] != b[-1]))
print(d(sys.argv[1], sys.argv[2]), calls)
EOF
$ python3 naive.py kitten sitting
3 29737
$ python3 naive.py definately definitely
1 12146179
```

29737 calls for a table of 56 cells, and twelve million for one of 121.
The table asks for each pair of prefixes once, in an order where the three
cells a cell needs are always filled already: row by row, left to right.
Each cell is

```
        int del = prev[j] + 1;
        int ins = row[j - 1] + 1;
        int rep = prev[j - 1] + ((unsigned char)a[j - 1] != c);
```

the cell above, the cell to the left, and the cell above and to the left,
whose 0 or 1 is the comparison of the two letters. Filling it costs (length
of a + 1) times (length of b + 1) cells, and only two rows are ever held:
kitten and sitting fill 56 cells and keep 14.

### Pruning, and the line that makes it safe

The first cell of a row is the first cell of the row above plus 1. Every
other cell is a cell of the row above plus 0 or 1, or the cell to its left
plus 1. Going left to right, then, no cell is smaller than the smallest
cell of the row above. That is the **invariant** of the walk: the smallest
cell of a row is never smaller than the smallest cell of the row above it.
Once that smallest cell is past the limit, so is every row below, and the
subtree cannot hold a suggestion. `near_walk()` goes down only when

```
        if (min <= n->maxd)
```

The last cell of the row is not the test: it is the distance from the
whole unknown word to the prefix so far, which is large while the prefix
is short ("speling" is 3 from "spel", above "spelling", which is 1 away).
The smallest cell is the distance from the prefix to the nearest start of
the unknown word. Every prefix of a word within the limit is within it of
some start of the unknown word, so on the way down to a suggestion the
smallest cell never passes the limit. Break it tries the other test.

## Decide the boundary

This distance has three edits, and a swap of two neighbours is two of
them: "teh" is 2 from "the", as the trace above shows, and "recieve" is 1
from "relieve" (one letter replaced) but 2 from "receive". spell offers
only the nearest, so it suggests "relieve". Damerau's distance adds a
fourth edit, two neighbouring letters swapped, which makes both of these 1:
each cell looks two rows up as well, so the table keeps three rows and the
walk carries two down the trie. With it, "receive" and "relieve" are both
one edit away and spell would offer both. It costs a few lines, and a
little more of every cell. Are the mistakes your users make mostly swapped
neighbours, as in teh and recieve, or letters missing, extra or wrong?

## Break it

Prune on the last cell instead of the smallest. In
`build/at/dsa/05.03/lib/trie.c`, in `near_walk()`, change
`if (min <= n->maxd)` to `if (row[n->len] <= n->maxd)`, build, and ask
again:
