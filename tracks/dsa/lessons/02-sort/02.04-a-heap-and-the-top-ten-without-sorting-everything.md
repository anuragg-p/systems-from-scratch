# 02.04 A heap, and the top ten without sorting everything

freq picks its most common words by going over all of them once for each
word it prints. A heap keeps the best ones seen so far, the worst of them
always on top, so one pass over the words is enough. It is also a sort of
its own, heapsort.

## Where this fits

![Where 02.04 fits](figures/02.04-map.svg)

This lesson is the heap box of module 02's strip, and it starts a new rail
of the library: the heap's filled dot is in sort's column, where it is
built. Besides sort's own heapsort, its first user is freq, back in module
01's column: the ring there is freq getting its top N from the heap
instead of from N passes over every word. The rail runs on, dashed for now, to route, gzip
and git, which all need "the smallest so far" over and over. sort gains
heapsort as `--algo=heap`. After this lesson freq ranks every word of a
large text in a fraction of a second, where it took tens of seconds.

## The idea

![freq's heap of the seven best words so far, the worst of them on top, as a tree and as an array: a better word arrives, the top comes out, the last item sifts down from the root, and the new word goes in at the end](figures/02.04-sift.svg)

Since 01.01, freq finds its top N by N passes over all the words it
counted: the first pass takes the most common, the next the most common
of the rest, and so on. For the top ten of the book that is 10 passes
over 7818 words, nothing. `-n 0` asks for every word, ranked: 7818 passes
over 7818 words, about 30 million comparisons, and on a text of a million
words with 90639 different ones, 4 billion. The obvious alternative is to
sort all the counts, as the shell pipeline's `sort -rn` does, and take the
first N: a whole sort to keep ten.

What the job needs is less than either. Keep the best N seen so far, and
for each new word ask one question: is it better than the worst of those?
Most words are not, and cost one comparison. When one is, the worst goes
and the new word takes its place. The structure that makes "the worst of
these" cheap to find, and cheap to replace, is a **heap**.

A heap is a binary tree with one rule between each item and its children.
freq needs the worst of its words at hand, so in its heap no word ranks
above its children: none is more common than they are (or, on equal
counts, bigger byte by byte, freq's tiebreak). The least common of the N
is then at the root. Nothing else about the order is promised: the two
children of an item can be either way round. The tree is kept in an array, level by level, with no pointers: the
item at `i` has its children at `2i+1` and `2i+2` and its parent at
`(i-1)/2`. The figure shows both at once, for freq's heap of seven on the
book.

### `sift_down` and `sift_up`

The two ways to repair the rule after one item changed. **Sift down**
takes an item that may belong lower in the tree: it compares the item's
two children to find the one that belongs higher (in freq's heap, the
lower ranked), and if that child belongs above the item, they swap; then
again from the item's new place, until it has no children or belongs
where it is. **Sift up** is the same walk the other
way: while an item belongs above its parent, they swap. Either walks one
path between the root and a leaf, and a tree of n items has about log2(n)
levels. The code holds the moving item aside and shifts the others, one
write each, as insertion sort did; the figure draws each step as a swap.

### `heap_push` and `heap_pop`

A push puts the new item at the end of the array, the next free place on
the bottom level, and sifts it up. A pop takes the root, moves the last
item into its place, and sifts that down. The heap's items are an Array of
01.01, so it grows as it needs.

### `top_heap`

freq's new way to pick the top N, now its default (`--top=heap`): push
words until there are N; after that, a word that ranks above the heap's
top replaces it, with a pop and a push. At the end, popping everything
gives the N best, worst first, so they are written into `best` from the
back. `--top=select` keeps the old way, with the same output.

### `heap_sort`

The same sifting, used to sort an array in place. First arrange the whole
array as a heap with the largest line on top (sifting down every item that
has children, from the last of them back to the root). Then n times: swap
the top to the end of the heap, shrink the heap by one, and sift down the
line that took the top's place. The largest lines collect at the end, in
order.

## How it works

### The invariant, and where each function keeps it

The **heap invariant** is that no child belongs above its parent: in
freq's heap, no word ranks above its children, so the root ranks lowest
of all. `heap_push()` breaks it at most at the new last
item, and `sift_up()` moves that item up until it holds; `heap_pop()`
breaks it at most at the root, and `sift_down()` moves that item down until
it holds. The line that decides which child goes up is this one:

```
if (child + 1 < n && above(v[child + 1], v[child], cmp, ctx, dir))
    child++;
```

The child that belongs higher of the two must be the one that moves up:
it then ranks no higher than its sibling, which becomes its child. Take the
other one and the rule breaks one level down, out of sight.

`above()` takes a direction: with `dir` 1 the heap keeps the lowest item
on top (freq's heap, and the one `heap_push()` and `heap_pop()` build);
with -1 the highest (`heap_sort()`, which needs the largest line on top to
put it at the end). freq's comparison is its `rank()`, so the lowest item
is the least common word.

### Why the worst of the best is on top

freq wants the most common words, and keeps a heap with the least common
of them at the root. That is not backwards: the root is the one item a
new word has to beat to get in, and the one that leaves when it does. Each
of the n words costs one comparison with the root, and the few that get
in cost a pop and a push, at most about 3 log2(N) comparisons: two a
level down, one a level up. At worst that is a small multiple of
n log2(N); in practice it is n and a little, where the N passes cost N
times n. For `-n 1000` on the book, 40590 comparisons against 7317500;
for `-n 10`, 7982 against 78125.

### Comparisons are not the whole cost

Divide a run's time by its comparisons, and heapsort gets slower per
comparison as the array grows: on these lines, about 26 nanoseconds at
256000 lines and 45 at 2048000, where merge sort stays near 23 and 28. Merge sort reads its runs front to back. A sift jumps
from `i` to `2i+1`, from one end of the array towards the other, and each
jump reads a line far from the last one in memory; the bigger the array,
the more of those reads miss the processor's caches and wait for memory.
Counting comparisons, which is what `DS_STATS` does, misses that cost
entirely. The clock does not.

## Break it

Make sift down always take the left child. In `lib/heap.c`, in
`sift_down()`, delete the two lines that pick the child that belongs
higher:

```
        if (child + 1 < n && above(v[child + 1], v[child], cmp, ctx, dir))     <- delete this line
            child++;                                                            <- and this one
```

-            child++;
-->

```
$ make -s
$ bin/freq data/book.txt
   5630 the
     21 arthur
   3038 i
      5 contents
   2655 of
    349 by
   3018 and
     97 sherlock
   1734 it
   2744 to
$ bin/sort --algo=heap data/book.txt | cmp -s - <(sort data/book.txt) || echo different
different
```

The heap no longer keeps its rule: a sift can move the wrong child up,
to be the parent of a sibling that ranks lower than it, and the root is
no longer the worst. New words are then compared with the wrong item:
"arthur", 21 times, is never the one on top when a better word comes, so
it stays to the end; and the pops at the end come out in the wrong order. Heapsort fails the same way. Nothing crashes
and every count is still a real count: a broken invariant gives wrong
answers that look like answers. Put the two lines back.
