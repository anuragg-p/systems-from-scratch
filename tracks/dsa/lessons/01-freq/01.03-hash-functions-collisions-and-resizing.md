# 01.03 Hash functions, collisions and resizing

The table of 01.02 is quick on the book and quadratic on a big text. This
lesson finds the two reasons and fixes each: a hash that spreads words over
every bucket, and twice the buckets whenever there are as many entries as
buckets. A lookup then stays near one probe however many words there are,
and freq's time grows as the text does.

## Where this fits

![Where 01.03 fits](figures/01.03-map.svg)

## The idea

![The table doubling from 1024 buckets to 2048 at the book's 1025th different word: each entry of old bucket 13 is linked into bucket 13 or 1037 by one more bit of its kept hash.](figures/01.03-resize.svg)

The 01.02 table has two faults, and each alone is enough to make it slow.

The first is the hash. A sum of bytes depends only on which letters a word
has, not their order, and the sums of words are small numbers that crowd
together. The book's 7818 different words have only 917 different sums,
all between 97 and 1979. So whatever the number of buckets, they land in
at most 917 of them, and the longest list holds 42 words. A good hash makes
every byte change every bit of the result, so that words that differ a
little land far apart: "cat" and "act" both sum to 312, and their **FNV-1a**
hashes are `0xf5e307190ce4a327` and `0xe723b5190545606b`, buckets 807 and
107 of 1024. FNV-1a (after Fowler, Noll and Vo) starts from a fixed 64-bit
number and, for each byte, mixes the byte into the low bits with an
exclusive or, then multiplies by a large prime, which spreads the change
up through the higher bits and mixes it with what the earlier bytes left.

The second is the number of buckets, fixed at 1024. A perfect hash still
puts n words in lists of n/1024 on average: twice the different words,
twice the probes for each word, and with twice the words that is four
times the work. The fix is the growable array's again: when the table has
as many entries as buckets, double the buckets. The ratio of entries to
buckets, the **load factor**, then stays at 1 or below, so a list holds
about one entry, at any size.

Doubling has a neat consequence for where entries go. The bucket is the
hash's low bits: 10 bits for 1024 buckets, 11 for 2048. So an entry of old
bucket 13 goes to bucket 13 or bucket 13 + 1024, by the one bit the old
number did not use, as the figure shows for the book's first resize. And
because each entry kept its hash, nothing is hashed again and no entry is
copied: only the bucket heads and the `next` links are made again.

### `hash_fnv1a`

FNV-1a over the key's bytes, in 64 bits, with FNV's offset basis and prime.

### `grow`

Frees the buckets, makes twice as many, all empty, and links every entry
into the bucket its kept hash picks now, walking the entries array in
order. It counts one resize, and as many moves as there are entries.

### `table_init` and `table_put`, changed

`table_init()` now picks `hash_fnv1a()`, and `table_put()` calls `grow()`
before it adds a new key whenever the entries have caught up with the
buckets, unless the table was set not to grow. freq's `--hash=sum` and
`--no-grow` choose the 01.02 table's ways again; the default is FNV-1a with
doubling.

## How it works

### Every byte stirs every bit

![Where the book's 7818 different words land in 8192 buckets: summed, all of them in the first quarter, in 917 buckets; with FNV-1a, spread over all of them.](figures/01.03-spread.svg)

The bucket is the hash's low bits, so those bits must depend on every byte
of the key, and on their order. In FNV-1a these two lines do it:

```
        h ^= p[i];
        h *= 1099511628211u;                /* the FNV prime */
```

Each byte goes into the low 8 bits by the exclusive or, and the
multiplication by an odd 64-bit number, before the next byte comes in,
carries what is there up into the bits above and mixes it with what the
earlier bytes left, so the same letters in another order give a quite
different number. A sum adds each letter's value, never more than 122 for
a lowercase letter, in any order: the book's words sum to at most 1979, so
in a table of 8192 buckets three quarters are never used, and doubling it
again only adds empty buckets.

### The invariant: never more entries than buckets

The table now keeps its load factor at 1 or below: after each put there
are no more entries than buckets. The line that keeps it runs before a new
key is added:

```
    if (!t->no_grow && t->entries.len >= t->nbuckets)
        grow(t);
```

As with the array of 01.01, each grow is costly and rare: it moves every
entry, but only after as many new entries have come in since the last one.
On the book, 3 resizes moved 1024 + 2048 + 4096 = 7168 entries for 7818
words: the cost per word is constant, amortized. And a move is cheap, since
`grow()` takes the bucket from the hash the entry kept, `e->hash & (t->nbuckets - 1)`,
and never touches the key.

## Decide the boundary

FNV-1a is the same function on every machine and every run. Anyone who
knows it can choose keys that all land in one bucket and turn every lookup
back into a walk of one long list, and servers that put the fields of a
request in a hash table have been attacked that way. Languages that hash
input they do not control use a keyed hash instead, such as SipHash, with
a secret key picked at random when the program starts, and pay for a
slower hash. freq hashes books. Who chooses the keys your table holds?

## Break it

Double the buckets but do not link the entries in again. In
`build/at/dsa/01.03/lib/table.c`, in `grow()`, delete the loop:

<!-- verify: patch file=lib/table.c
-    for (size_t i = 0; i < t->entries.len; i++) {
-        Entry *e = array_at(&t->entries, i);
-        size_t b = e->hash & (t->nbuckets - 1);
-        e->next = t->buckets[b];
-        t->buckets[b] = (long)i;

-->
```
    for (size_t i = 0; i < t->entries.len; i++) {
        Entry *e = array_at(&t->entries, i);
        size_t b = e->hash & (t->nbuckets - 1);
        e->next = t->buckets[b];
        t->buckets[b] = (long)i;
    }
```

and build and count the book again:

grow every bucket is empty, so every word counted before it is not found
and is added again, as new, with its count starting over; then the table
fills up faster and grows once more than it should. The counts are spread
over the copies, and freq prints whichever copies rank highest. Fewer
probes than before, too: lists that lose their entries are short. Put the
loop back.
