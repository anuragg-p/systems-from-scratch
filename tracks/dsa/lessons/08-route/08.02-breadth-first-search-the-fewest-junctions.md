# 08.02 Breadth-first search: the fewest junctions

route's first search, breadth-first search, finds the route between two
junctions that passes the fewest of them. It is simple, fast and always
right about what it counts, and on a real map what it counts turns out to
be the wrong thing: that is what the next lesson fixes.

## Where this fits

![Where 08.02 fits](figures/08.02-map.svg)

This is the second box in route's strip: the first search. It stands on
the road graph of 08.01, which it asks for a junction's roads every time
it finishes one, and it keeps the junctions waiting their turn in a queue,
a plain array it allocates itself; nothing from the library's rails yet.
Above it is what route prints: the route, junction by junction, with the
metres so far. After this lesson route answers a request for a route, from
any junction to any other, or says that no road joins them.

## The idea

Until now route stopped at "no search to find a route with yet". Asked for
a route from junction 3 to junction 8 of the tiny map, it has to choose
among many: 3, 1, 8 by the ring road; 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 along the main
street; and every detour through the northern row. It needs a rule for
which is best, and a way to be sure it has found it without trying them
all.

The simplest rule is the fewest roads, which is also the fewest junctions.
Junctions one road from 3 are 0, 1, 2, 4 and 9. Junctions two roads away
are the new ones those lead to: 5, 6, 8 and 10. As soon as a junction turns
up in that order, the number of roads it took is the fewest possible, since
every junction nearer was looked at first. So: deal with the start, then
everything one road away, then everything two roads away, and so on, like
a ripple, until TO turns up.

A queue keeps that order, as it kept make's ready targets in 07.04:
junctions go in at the back and come out at the front, first in, first out. Taking a junction out of the queue and
following its roads is **finishing** it. Each road leads to a junction that
is either new, which is **reached** now, one road further than the junction
being finished, and goes in at the back; or one seen already, which is left
alone. The search also notes, for each junction reached, the junction it
was reached from. When TO comes out of the queue, those notes, read
backwards from TO, are the route.

![Breadth-first search on tiny.map from 3 to 8: one frame per junction finished, each junction labelled with its distance in roads, and the queue under the map](figures/08.02-bfs.svg)

Step through it. The queue only ever holds junctions at one distance, then
junctions at the next: 1s, then 2s, then 3s. Junction 8 is reached from 1,
in the third frame, at 2 roads, and nothing can reach it in fewer. The
route is 3, 1, 8: 2 roads and 1200 metres. Along the main street it would
be 5 roads, but only 1000 metres. The search counted what it was asked to
count.

### `search_bfs`

The search itself: a queue of n junction numbers, with `head` where the
next one comes out and `tail` where the next one goes in. It marks each
junction reached as it goes in, and stops when TO has come out. Around it,
in the same lesson, comes what every search of route shares: `start()`
gives each junction its distance (-1: not reached), the junction it was
reached from and its state; `route_of()` follows those back from TO and
turns them round; `print_route()` prints the route with the metres so far,
adding up each road's length. The table of searches, and `pick()`, which
chooses one of them, have only this one to choose from for now.

## How it works

### Why the first time is the fewest

The **invariant** of the queue: its junctions are in order of distance, and
the last is at most one road further than the first. It holds at the start
(only FROM, at 0). Finishing the front junction, at distance d, adds
junctions at d+1 at the back; everything behind the front is at d or d+1
already, so the order holds. So junctions are finished in order of
distance: all at 0, then all at 1, and so on. A junction at k roads from
FROM has a neighbour at k-1, on its fewest-roads route; that neighbour is
finished before any junction at k, and reaches it then, at k. No junction
further out can reach it first, since none is finished before it. So the
distance a junction gets the first time is final, and the search never
needs to change one:

```
if (state[v] == UNSEEN) {
    state[v] = REACHED;
    reached++;
    dist[v] = dist[u] + 1;
    prev[v] = u;
    queue[tail++] = v;
}
```

### Reached once, so a plain array is enough

A junction is marked when it goes into the queue, not when it comes out, so
it goes in once at most. That is what lets the queue be an array of n ints
with two indexes that only ever move forward: n pushes at most, and no
need to wrap round. Marking it on the way out would let a junction go in
once from each neighbour finished before it.

### Stopping when TO comes out

The loop runs until TO is finished, not just reached:

```
while (head < tail && state[to] != FINISHED) {
```

For this search, stopping when TO is reached would be as right, and
sooner: on the tiny map, 8 is reached in the third frame and finished in
the eighth. The next two searches can change a distance after it is first set, and
only a finished junction's is final, so all three stop at the same place.
The other way out of the loop is an empty queue: every junction that can be
reached was, and TO was not among them.

## Break it

Turn the queue into a stack: take junctions from the back, the newest
first. In `build/at/dsa/08.02/tools/route/route.c`, in `search_bfs()`,
change the line that takes the next junction, `int u = queue[head++];`, to
`int u = queue[--tail];`, and build and run again:

<!-- verify: patch file=tools/route/route.c old="int u = queue[head++];" new="int u = queue[--tail];" -->
<!-- verify: session -->
```
$ make -s
$ bin/route tools/route/tiny.map 3 8
from 3 to 8: 1700 metres, 5 junctions
  metres  junction       x       y
       0         3     400     300
     370         9     200     600
     870        10     700     600
    1270        11    1100     600
    1700         8    1400     300
$ DS_TRACE=1 bin/route tools/route/tiny.map 3 8 > /dev/null
trace: bfs: finish 3 at 0 (start); queue: 0:1 1:1 2:1 4:1 9:1
trace: bfs: finish 9 at 1 (from 3); queue: 0:1 1:1 2:1 4:1 10:2
trace: bfs: finish 10 at 2 (from 9); queue: 0:1 1:1 2:1 4:1 5:3 11:3
trace: bfs: finish 11 at 3 (from 10); queue: 0:1 1:1 2:1 4:1 5:3 6:4 7:4 8:4
trace: bfs: finish 8 at 4 (from 11); queue: 0:1 1:1 2:1 4:1 5:3 6:4 7:4
$ bin/route tools/route/london.map 300,3500 12000,4000 | head -1
from 7484 to 8119: 200316 metres, 2540 junctions
```

The newest junction goes first, so the search runs off down one road as
far as it can, a depth-first walk, and the 1s wait at the bottom of the
stack under 3s and 4s. A junction is still given a distance once, the first
time it is reached, but now that is by whatever long way the walk took:
junction 8 is reached from 11, four roads out, before junction 1 is ever
finished. The invariant is gone, and with it the guarantee. A crossing of
central London that is 12 kilometres as the crow flies now takes 200. Put
the line back and `make -s` again.

## Go deeper

- [Algorithms, 4th edition, 4.1 Undirected Graphs](https://algs4.cs.princeton.edu/41graph/) (Sedgewick and Wayne) - its section on breadth-first search: a queue of "vertices that have been marked but whose adjacency lists have not been checked", and the proposition that BFS finds, for every vertex reachable from s, a path with the fewest edges.
- Introduction to Algorithms, 4th edition (Cormen, Leiserson, Rivest, Stein), chapter 20, Elementary Graph Algorithms - section 20.2, Breadth-first search, proves the claim of How it works with care: the distances it computes are the shortest-path distances.
- [Introduction to the A* Algorithm](https://www.redblobgames.com/pathfinding/a-star/introduction.html) (Amit Patel, Red Blob Games) - its sections Breadth First Search and Early exit: the same frontier, drawn spreading over a grid, and why to stop when the goal comes off the frontier.
- The Algorithm Design Manual, 3rd edition (Skiena), chapter 7, Graph Traversal - breadth-first search beside depth-first search, the two orders this lesson's Break it swaps.
