# 08.04 A*: searching towards the goal

Dijkstra's search spreads out from the start the same way in every
direction, and finishes everything nearer than the goal, behind the start
as much as ahead of it. A* is the same search with one change: it orders
the heap by the distance so far plus a guess of the distance left, the
straight line to the goal. It finds the same route and finishes a fraction
of the junctions, as long as the guess is never too big.

## Where this fits

![Where 08.04 fits](figures/08.04-map.svg)

This is the last box in route's strip. A* stands on what 08.03 stood on:
the road graph of 08.01 for a junction's roads, and the heap of 02.04, on
the same rail, for the next junction to finish. It is the first search to
use where the junctions are, not just which roads join them: the guess
comes from their positions. After this lesson A* is route's default, and
finds a shortest route by looking at a narrow band of the map between the
two points.

## The idea

Look again at Dijkstra on the tiny map. To go from 3 east to 8 it
finished all 12 junctions, among them 0, 2 and 9, west of 3, away from 8.
It had to: it finishes junctions in order of distance from FROM, and 2, at
300 metres, is nearer than 6. Nothing in that order says where TO is.

On London it shows. From one junction in the centre to another 3.75
kilometres away as the crow flies, Dijkstra finishes 6040 junctions to find
a route of 4131 metres: every junction less than 4131 metres from FROM by
road, a disc of the map around FROM, most of it in directions that lead
nowhere near TO.

The fix is to order the heap by another key: the distance from FROM so far
plus a **guess** of the distance still to go to TO. On a map there is an
obvious guess, for the price of a square root: the straight line from the
junction to TO. A junction behind the start has a small distance but a
large guess, so its sum is large and it waits; a junction on the way has a
sum close to the route's length, and comes off first. That is A* (said
"A star").

On the tiny map the straight line from 3 to 8 is 1000 metres, and so is
the main street. Each junction of the street has a sum of exactly 1000,
every other junction more:

![A* on tiny.map from 3 to 8: one frame per junction finished, each junction labelled with its distance so far plus its guess, and the heap under the map](figures/08.04-astar.svg)

Six junctions finished, all on the route. The others were reached and
pushed, but their sums (1183 for junction 1, 1282 for 10, 1600 for 2) never
came to the top before 8 did. Dijkstra finished all twelve to find the
same 1000 metres.

### `guess`

The straight line from a junction to TO, from the junctions' positions in
metres. Nothing else of A* knows what a map is.

### `search_astar`

Dijkstra with the heap, as in 08.03, but the key pushed with each junction
is its distance plus its guess, and the start goes in with its guess, not
0. Each entry still carries the distance alone, which the route is built
from. A* is the last line of the search table, so it is now the default.

## How it works

### Why the guess must never be too big

A* finishes the junction whose distance plus guess is smallest, and stops
when TO comes off the heap. At TO the guess is 0, so its key is its
distance. Suppose TO is on top with a distance D, and a shorter route of
length L exists. Follow that route from FROM to the first junction on it
that is not finished. The junction before it is finished, at its true
distance (the next section says why), so this one is on the heap with a
distance no more than its part of L. If the guess is never more than the
real distance left, its guess is at most the rest of L, and its key at most
L, less than D: it would be on top, not TO. So TO never comes off by a
worse route than the shortest. That is the condition: a guess that is
never more than the real distance left, which books call **admissible**.
A guess of 0 is always admissible, and makes A* Dijkstra: with
`return 0 * sqrt(dx * dx + dy * dy);` it finishes the same 15492
junctions on the crossing.

The straight line is admissible here because of how the map was made: no
road is shorter than the straight line between its ends (`gen.py` measures
each along its bends, rounds up, and checks it), and a route to TO is a
chain of roads, so no shorter than the straight line it bridges. Give a map
roads shorter than that, or the guess in the wrong units, and the
guarantee is gone.

### Why a finished junction stays finished

`search_astar()` skips an entry for a junction already finished, exactly as
`search_dijkstra()` does:

```
if (state[u] == FINISHED) {
    stale++;
```

That needs more than admissible. It needs the guess to be **consistent**:
for every road from u to v, guess(u) is at most the road's length plus
guess(v). For the straight line that is the triangle inequality: going
from u to TO straight is no longer than going to v first. With it, the
keys of the junctions along any route never go down, so a junction comes
off the heap first by its best way in, and finishing it then is right.

## Decide the boundary

route's guess never overestimates, so its route is always the shortest.
Make the guess a little bigger on purpose and A* looks at even less: at
1.1 times the straight line, the centre pair still comes out at 4131
metres with 239 junctions finished instead of 544, and the crossing at
12974 metres, one metre too long, with 1404 finished instead of 5245.
Route planners and games do this deliberately, trading a guarantee for
speed. route does not: it promises the shortest route, and a guess that
can overestimate cannot keep that promise. Do your users need the shortest
route, or one nearly as short, found with a fraction of the work?

## Break it

Double the guess. In `build/at/dsa/08.04/tools/route/route.c`, in
`guess()`, change `return sqrt(dx * dx + dy * dy);` to
`return 2 * sqrt(dx * dx + dy * dy);`:

this far to go"; it says "probably about this far", and the proof of How

## Go deeper

- [Introduction to the A* Algorithm](https://www.redblobgames.com/pathfinding/a-star/introduction.html) (Amit Patel, Red Blob Games) - its sections Heuristic search and The A* algorithm, drawn on a grid: Dijkstra, a greedy search on the guess alone, and A* using both, and "As long as the heuristic does not overestimate distances, A* finds an optimal path."
- [Heuristics](http://theory.stanford.edu/~amitp/GameProgramming/Heuristics.html) (Amit Patel, Amit's Thoughts on Pathfinding) - its sections A*'s Use of the Heuristic and Speed or accuracy?: what a guess below, equal to or above the real distance does, and why a game might choose speed, this lesson's Decide the boundary.
- [Algorithms, 4th edition, 4.4 Shortest Paths](https://algs4.cs.princeton.edu/44sp/) (Sedgewick and Wayne) - the Dijkstra that A* is built on, with a visualization of it on a Euclidean graph, whose vertices have positions as London's junctions do.
- Introduction to Algorithms, 4th edition (Cormen, Leiserson, Rivest, Stein), chapter 22, Single-Source Shortest Paths - its triangle inequality for shortest-path distances, the same inequality that makes the straight line consistent.
