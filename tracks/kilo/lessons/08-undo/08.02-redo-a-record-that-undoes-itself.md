# 08.02 Redo: a record that undoes itself

Ctrl-R now does again what Ctrl-Z took back. It needs no new kind of
record: applying a record now keeps what it replaces, so the same record,
applied a second time, is the redo.

## Where this fits

![Where 08.02 fits](figures/08.02-map.svg)

This lesson builds the redo piece, under the edit piece, beside the
records. The two short arrows between it and the history are the two ways
a step now travels: an undo no longer frees its step but puts it on a
second stack in the history, and Ctrl-R, a new case of the key handler,
takes it from there, applies it, and gives it back to the first. The
records piece does the work both ways. After this lesson one Ctrl-Z too
many costs nothing: Ctrl-R puts the step back, until the next edit.

## The idea

At 08.01 an undo puts the rows back and frees the step. Press Ctrl-Z once
too often and that change is gone for good, a second time.

Look at what `undoPut()` does with a `U_CHG`: it frees the row's text and
puts the record's text in its place. The text it frees is exactly what a
redo would need. So keep it, in the record just applied. The record then
holds what the row was a moment ago, and applying it again puts that back:

![One U_CHG record and its row, step by step: after the edit, the row holds the new text and the record the old; after Ctrl-Z, the two texts have changed places; after Ctrl-R, they change places again, and the row holds the new text once more](figures/08.02-swap.svg)

The other two kinds turn into each other. A `U_INS`, applied, deletes its
row, and keeps the row's text: it is now a `U_DEL`. A `U_DEL`, applied,
inserts its row: it is now a `U_INS`, which would delete it. So every
record, applied, becomes its own opposite, and undo and redo are one act.
Only the order differs: an undo applies a step's records last to first, a
redo first to last, the order they were made in.

Take Enter pressed after `and ` in `and what each line was`, the second of
three rows: row 1, as a record's `at` counts rows, from 0, and as the
figures number them. Its step is a `U_INS` of row 2, then a `U_CHG` of row
1 holding `and what each line was`. Ctrl-Z, last to first: the `U_CHG` puts
the whole row back and keeps `and `; the `U_INS` deletes row 2 and keeps
`what each line was`, as a `U_DEL`. Ctrl-R, first to last: the `U_DEL`
inserts `what each line was` as row 2 and becomes a `U_INS` again; the
`U_CHG` puts `and ` back and keeps the whole row. The text is as Enter left
it, and the step is ready to be undone again.

An undone step goes on a second stack, the **redo stack**; Ctrl-R takes the
step undone last off it, and puts it back on the undo stack:

![The undo and redo stacks, step by step: a typed, then b typed, each a step on the undo stack; Ctrl-Z moves the b step to the redo stack, and a second Ctrl-Z the a step; Ctrl-R moves the a step back; c typed is a new edit, and the redo stack is emptied](figures/08.02-two-stacks.svg)

A new edit empties the redo stack. Its steps were taken back from a text
that the new edit has just changed, and they describe that text, not this
one.

This is Vim's design. Its `u_undoredo()` says it in two sentences: "The
lines in the file are replaced by the lines in the entry list at
curbuf->b_u_curhead. The replaced lines in the file are saved in the entry
list for the next undo/redo." Undo calls `u_undoredo(TRUE)`, redo
`u_undoredo(FALSE)`: one function for both.

<!-- verify: mention names="u_undoredo() u_undoredo(TRUE) u_undoredo(FALSE)" outside reason="Vim's function, not this project's" -->

### `undoApply`

Applies a record and makes it its opposite. For a `U_CHG` or a `U_INS` it
takes the row's text first, then lets `undoPut()` from 08.01 put the row
back, and leaves the record holding what it took, with the opposite kind.

### `editorUndo`

Now applies each record with `undoApply()`, and pushes the step on the redo
stack instead of freeing it. With no memory for the push, it frees the step
and gives the history up.

### `editorRedo`

Ctrl-R. It closes the open step; with nothing on the redo stack it says
`Nothing to redo`. Otherwise it takes the step undone last, applies its
records first to last with recording off, and pushes it on the undo stack.

### `undoSave`

A record that opens a new step is a new edit: before it is kept, every step
on the redo stack is freed.

Outside these: the redo stack in `struct undoState`, which `undoClear()`
frees too, and the Ctrl-R case, which calls `editorRedo()`.

## How it works

### The record takes the row's own text

```
    if (r->type != U_DEL) {
        was.text = E.row[r->at].chars;
        was.len = E.row[r->at].size;
        E.row[r->at].chars = NULL;
    }
```

For these two kinds nothing is copied. The record takes the row's pointer,
and the row's is set to `NULL`, so when `undoPut()` frees the row's text,
as it did at 08.01, it frees nothing; for a `U_INS` the row is then
deleted, its text already safe in the record. The type flips in one line:

```
    was.type = r->type == U_INS ? U_DEL : r->type == U_DEL ? U_INS : U_CHG;
```

and `*r = was;` makes the record what was taken. 08.01's `undoPut()` did
not change; `undoApply()` is wrapped around it.

### First to last

The records of a step were made in order, each against the text the ones
before it had left. After an undo the text is as it was before the first;
a redo walks forward from there, so each record meets the text it was made
against, as it did going backward. In the undo's order the records would
meet rows that are not where they were, as in 08.01's Break it.

```
    for (j = 0; j < s.numrec; j++) undoApply(&s.rec[j]);
```

### A new edit forgets what was undone

```
    if (!U.open) {
        while (U.numredo) undoFreeStep(&U.redo[--U.numredo]);
    }
```

A record that finds no step open is the first of a new edit. The steps on
the redo stack were made against the text before that edit; applied after
it, they would put back rows as they were then, over whatever the edit did.
So they go, and the history is a line: whatever was undone and not redone
before a new edit is gone.

The **invariant** the swap keeps: undo every step, and the text is what was
opened; redo every step, and it is what it was before the first undo. Each
record holds exactly the row it replaced, so applying it again gives back
exactly that.

## Decide the boundary

kilo's history is a line, and a new edit throws the redo stack away. Zed
does the same, `self.redo_stack.clear();` when a transaction ends, and so
does VS Code ("remove the future"). It is simple, and the user never has to
think about where they are. Vim, Neovim and Helix keep a tree instead. In
Vim's words, "This happens when you undo a few changes and then make a new
change. The undone changes become a branch." A new edit loses nothing, but
the user needs a way to walk the tree: `:undolist` lists its leaves, `g-`
goes to the older text state, and "using "u" and CTRL-R will not get you
to all possible text states while repeating "g-" and "g+" does". Helix's
revisions each have a parent, and `earlier` and `later` walk them by steps
or by time. Emacs records an undo in the undo list like any other change,
and "a redo record is just an undo record ... created by an undo command",
so redo is undoing an undo. May a state the user once saw become
unreachable?
